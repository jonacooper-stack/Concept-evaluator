# Business Objectives & Evaluation Rubric

The source of truth for goals, constraints, and scoring. Read **00-evaluation-stage.md
first** — it defines the concept-stage evidence standard and the symmetric
calibration rule. This doc defines the target, the must-have taxonomy, the weighted
scorecard, the regulatory risk gate, and the honest tiers. This doc's income ladder
and weights supersede any older numbers still lingering in docs 02–07.

## The business we're building

### Primary objective
A bootstrapped business built around a **market-pulled, must-have ("need-to-have")
problem** — something customers are already paying to solve, bought without heavy
internal selling. Strong, durable, recurring income for two versatile founders,
with optionality to grow into something larger and lasting. Low cash to launch,
fast to cash-flow positive, quick to grow.

### What "must-have" means — the spine of the thesis (read carefully)
We are done selling **vitamins**. We want a **painkiller / need-to-have**: a
problem the customer is *forced* to solve — urgent, recurring, already budgeted,
and bought without us having to convince them the problem exists. If the customer
has to be educated into believing they have the problem, it is the wrong problem.

**A "must-have" is defined by its FORCING FUNCTION, not by its industry.** A
problem is a need-to-have if NOT solving it has a fast, concrete, painful
consequence the buyer will pay to avoid. There are several equally valid forcing
functions — **regulation is only one of them, and it is not required**:

1. **Regulatory / compliance mandate** — a law, regulation, or standard forces a
   recurring, deadline-driven buy (e.g., CMMC, HIPAA, PCI). *A favorite when
   present, but only one path — never a requirement.*
2. **Contractual / counterparty requirement** — a prime contractor, customer,
   insurer, lender, franchisor, or auditor *requires* the buyer to maintain
   something (an attestation, certification, monitoring, audit) to keep the
   contract, the coverage, the loan, or the franchise.
3. **Critical input to a production / revenue process** — a raw material,
   component, data feed, or service that a customer's output literally depends on;
   if they don't have it, the line stops or the product can't ship. **This is
   exactly as strong a must-have as a compliance mandate.**
4. **Operational / financial continuity** — without it the buyer loses money,
   loses customers, fails an audit, or loses the license/ability to operate
   (e.g., the system of record a business cannot run a day without).
5. **Effectively-mandatory risk / liability mitigation** — not legally required,
   but the cost or probability of the downside makes skipping it irrational for a
   responsible operator (and often something their own customers or insurers check).

Score the **strength of the forcing function** in dimension 1 below. All five
types can earn a 9–10. The questions that matter: *Is the buy forced or optional?
Is it recurring? Is it already budgeted? What breaks, and how fast, if they don't
buy?* A concept with no identifiable forcing function is a vitamin and scores low
on dimension 1 no matter how clean the rest is.

### Founder edge (how we expect to win)
The founders are **versatile, adaptive generalists** — excellent at marketing,
positioning, reaching and persuading buyers, and able to build software. The
expected winning move is to enter a market whose incumbents are **stale, sleepy,
old-school, and poorly marketed** (legacy consultancies, generalist MSPs, old-line
vendors, sleepy distributors) and out-position them.

A market **moat is fine — often desirable.** A regulatory, compliance, standard,
expertise, or supply barrier that forces a recurring buy AND keeps lazy
competitors out is a **feature, not a bug**, PROVIDED: (a) the incumbents behind
the moat are sleepy and beatable, and (b) the barrier is **bridgeable** by
versatile founders via advisors, named practitioners, partners, hires, or
reasonable learning. What the founders avoid is **high-performing, well-funded,
strong-tech incumbents** — anywhere the only winning move is to outspend or
out-engineer them. It is the *quality of the competitor*, not the presence of a
moat, that matters.

The founders are NOT a field-service crew and will not build a business whose core
is sustained manual/field operations.

### Archetype — one shape of "good" (not the only shape)
The most-discussed concept to date is **managed CMMC Level 2 readiness for small
defense subcontractors**. It is a good *illustration* of the pattern — but it is an
EXAMPLE, not the template every idea must copy. Do not over-fit to compliance
plays. The reusable pattern, stated generally:
- A **forcing function** (any of the five types above) drives a recurring,
  budgeted, must-have buy.
- Delivered as a **productized, software-leveraged recurring offering** (fixed
  scope, published pricing, one delivery spine; software/AI does the high-volume,
  low-judgment work).
- **Marketing/positioning-led acquisition + founder-led close** — the founders' edge.
- A **fragmented segment the strong players ignore**, served only by sleepy
  incumbents.
- Any **moat is real but bridgeable**.
- **Capital-light, remote-first, NOT field ops.**

### Financial targets (a ladder, not a single cliff)
- By ~month 12: **~$300K+ in annual income to each founder.**
- By ~month 24: **~$500K+ to each founder, and growing from there.**
- **$2M/founder is upside/optionality, NOT a requirement.** Do not kill an
  otherwise-strong idea for failing to reach $2M. (Older docs that say "$2M in 24
  months" are superseded by this ladder.)
- **Recurring revenue is the spine** — subscription, contract, retainer,
  take-rate, or other repeating models, strongly preferred over one-time sales.

### Capital, competitive, and probability posture
- **Capital:** bootstrapped, very low upfront capital, cash-flow positive fast, no
  large pre-revenue outlay, no outside raise assumed.
- **Competitive:** fragmented markets full of stale, beatable incumbents are
  IDEAL. A bridgeable regulatory/expertise/supply moat with sleepy incumbents
  behind it is a PLUS, not a minus. The thing to avoid is a dominant, well-funded,
  strong-tech incumbent — or a horizontal platform that could reach down into the
  niche within ~2 quarters.
- **Probability:** favor high-probability, market-validated demand over moonshots.

## Hard constraints (any "no" = automatic disqualification)
1. Cannot be built with the founders' versatile skills + reasonable learning, OR
   its core requires sustained manual/field operations.
2. Requires more than modest upfront capital.
3. Has no plausible recurring revenue component.
4. No plausible path to even **~$300K/founder by ~24 months** at reasonable scale.
5. Faces **high-performing, well-funded, strong-tech incumbents** in the target
   segment, where the only winning move is to outspend or out-engineer them.
   (Stale/sleepy incumbents and bridgeable moats are NOT disqualifying — often the
   opposite.)
6. Requires specialized credentials/expertise the founders lack AND **cannot
   scaffold** via advisors, named practitioners, partners, hires, or reasonable
   learning. (A *bridgeable* expertise barrier is acceptable and often attractive.)

Note: "must-have" is NOT a hard constraint here — it is the most heavily weighted
scored dimension below. A weak-pull idea is not disqualified, just scored low and
will fall out on its own. Note also: **being regulated is NOT a hard constraint or
a penalty.** Regulatory exposure is handled by the risk gate below, not by killing
or down-scoring the concept for the regulation existing.

## Evaluation rubric — score each dimension 1–10, weighted to 100

Apply the calibration and evidence standard from 00-evaluation-stage.md. Score
each dimension on absolute merit; do not peek at the running total while scoring.

| # | Dimension | Weight | A 10 | A 1 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Market pull / must-have (forcing function)** | 25 | A strong forcing function (any of the 5 types) makes the buy forced, recurring, already-budgeted; bought without internal selling; fast painful consequence if skipped | Nice-to-have; no forcing function; no budget line; customer must be convinced the problem exists |
| 2 | **Competitive landscape & defensibility** | 18 | Fragmented or moat-protected market whose incumbents are stale, sleepy, poorly-marketed; clear air; any moat is bridgeable and keeps lazy entrants out; defensible once won | Owned/contested by high-performing, well-funded, strong-tech incumbents, or trivially copyable / encroachable by a horizontal platform within ~2 quarters |
| 3 | **Path to founder income (ladder)** | 13 | Conservative math reaches ~$300K+/founder by mo 12 and ~$500K+ by mo 24, room to grow | No credible path to ~$300K/founder even by mo 24 without heroics |
| 4 | **Recurring revenue strength** | 12 | True subscription/contract; >90% repeating; high retention | One-time transactions; no repeat dynamic |
| 5 | **Probability of success / demand evidence** | 10 | Demand evidenced by existing spend, named buyers, analogous markets; clear reachable customer; low novelty risk | Speculative; requires market creation |
| 6 | **Capital efficiency** | 8 | Launchable for low four/five figures; cash-flow positive fast | Needs significant capital before first revenue |
| 7 | **Operational tractability (not field-ops)** | 5 | Lean, remote-first, low-touch; NOT trucks/installs/field visits | Field-service/manual-labor-heavy or large ops team day one |
| 8 | **Skill / founder fit (versatility)** | 5 | Squarely in strategy/finance/marketing/positioning/GTM/software; barrier (if any) is bridgeable | Needs sustained field ops or a genuinely unbridgeable credential |
| 9 | **Scalability optionality** | 4 | Can stay lifestyle OR scale into something larger/sellable | Inherently capped, no growth lever |

Weights sum to 100. **Must-have (25) and Competition (18) are the two heaviest
factors — together 43% — because they are where this thesis lives and where the
founders win.** Competition (dim 2) is backed by a dedicated Competitive Analyst
review (doc 06b) so it is judged on real, researched evidence, not a hand-wave.

### Legal / Regulatory RISK GATE (not a weighted dimension)
Regulatory and legal exposure is assessed as a **gate**, not as a weighted score
that drags attractive must-haves down. (Older versions made "amount of regulation"
a scored negative — which perversely punished exactly the forced-demand businesses
we like. That is fixed: regulation that *drives demand* is credited in dimension 1;
regulation as *risk* is handled here.) The Legal reviewer classifies the concept:

- **NONE / MINOR** — standard business/contract law; no special licensing; ordinary
  liability. (Most concepts.) No effect on tier.
- **SERIOUS BUT MANAGEABLE** — real licensing/compliance/liability work, but a
  known, navigable path exists (named regime, bridgeable, insurable). **This is
  fine and often expected for a forced-demand business — it does NOT lower the
  tier.** It becomes a due-diligence list, not a penalty.
- **FATAL** — an unbridgeable license/credential the founders can't scaffold, an
  uninsurable liability tail, or a legal prohibition. Only a **FATAL** rating gates
  a concept down (to a hard cap or disqualification per hard constraint #6).

So: a heavily-regulated business with a navigable compliance path scores HIGH on
must-have (forced demand), is unaffected on the weighted rubric, and merely carries
a documented compliance checklist. Regulation is a demand asset, a risk to manage,
and never a reason to deflate the score.

## Scoring discipline
Per 00-evaluation-stage.md: 5 = mediocre, 7 = good, 9+ = rare and earned — and
**equally**, do not deflate a genuinely strong, evidenced dimension. A genuinely
excellent concept should show a field of evidenced 8s and 9s; that is correct, not
"gaming."

## The council that produces the scores
Six independent expert reviews feed this scorecard (each via its own subagent, run
as a fresh parallel batch per concept, blind to each other and to any target):
**CFO** (02), **CMO** (03), **COO** (04), **Legal/Regulatory** (05, runs the risk
gate), **CTO** (06), **Competitive Analyst** (06b, web-enabled deep competitive
teardown). The **PM synthesizer** (07) integrates them, runs a two-directional
red-team, computes the Objectives Weighted Score, sets the tier, and produces the
plain-English deliverables (doc 14).

## Tiers & how the result is reported (replaces the old hard "pass bar")
There is no single numeric cliff to engineer toward. Score honestly, rank
everything, and label each concept by tier on the **Objectives Weighted Score**
(after the red-team), subject to the gates:

- **A — Advisor-Ready (Strong):** Objectives **>= 80**, must-have (dim 1) **>= 8**,
  competition (dim 2) **>= 7**, no FATAL legal gate, demand backed by named
  comparables/market signals. *"I would put this in front of expert advisors and
  defend the score."*
- **B — Promising (Deep-Dive):** Objectives **68–79**, OR >= 80 with one serious
  open risk. Worth manual validation before commitment.
- **C — Pass (Drop):** Objectives **< 68**, OR fails a hard constraint / FATAL
  gate, OR must-have (dim 1) **< 6**.

**The deliverable is the honest top 2–3 of the field, ranked, each with a full
plain-English dossier (doc 14)** — drawn from A and, if needed, the strongest B
concepts. If the best the field offers is B-tier, say so plainly and surface it
anyway: the founders will take the top 2–3 to real expert advisors and compare.
A run that honestly finds zero A-tier concepts is a valid, useful result — it is
NOT a reason to inflate. Equally, a strong concept that honestly earns A-tier
should be labeled A without hesitation.

## What this doc is NOT
Not a business plan or idea list. It is the filter and the yardstick. The
plain-English business write-ups are produced as deliverables per doc 14.
