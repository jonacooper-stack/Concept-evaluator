# Briefing #3 — SurplusLines
*(Council average: 7.02 / 10. Verdict: high-quality near-miss — great demand, but the work is messy and one rival is already modern.)*

## The big idea, in plain words
Some insurance is "normal" and some is "special." When a risk is unusual — a weird factory, a hard-to-insure building — a broker has to place it with a special kind of insurer called a **non-admitted** or **surplus-lines** carrier. When they do that, the **broker** (not the insurance company) becomes responsible for a pile of tax filings and reports to each state, on different schedules, plus keeping proof that they first tried the normal insurance market.

Each state has its own tax rate, its own forms, its own deadlines, and about 15 states have a special "stamping office" with its own website. If the broker misses a filing or gets the math wrong, they face penalties, interest, audits — and can put their **license** at risk.

This business does all of those tax filings and reports **for the broker**. Software figures out which states are owed money and how much, fills out the forms, and a specialist submits them on time and keeps the records audit-ready. Importantly, the broker still pays the actual tax from their own bank account — the service just prepares and files the paperwork, so it never touches the money (which keeps it legally simpler).

## Who buys it and why they're stuck
Small and medium "excess & surplus" (E&S) insurance brokers — from solo brokers up to ~50-person shops — that place this special insurance across many states. They're stuck because the paperwork is forced, recurring, and tied to their license, and it gets overwhelming as they write business in more states. This market has also been **growing fast** (more risks are pushed to special insurers when regular insurance gets expensive), so more brokers are getting pulled into the mess.

## How it makes money
- A **monthly subscription per brokerage**, priced by how many filings and states — roughly **$500 to $3,000 a month**.
- Plus a **setup fee** and per-filing fees.

To hit the income goal, the founders would need roughly **120–180 brokerages** out of tens of thousands of licensed surplus-lines brokers. Plenty of room.

A big plus: one of the founders has a **background in insurance**, so they understand this customer and can earn their trust quickly.

## Who else is out there (the competition)
- The huge insurance-tech companies (AgentSync, Vertafore) work on a **different** problem (agent licensing), not this tax-filing one — so they're not direct rivals.
- **Sleepy clerks and hourly consultants** do this today with spreadsheets — easy to beat on quality and marketing.
- **BUT** there is one modern, software-led company already in this exact lane (called **InsCipher**). So the claim "no modern player exists here" is only partly true. There's one to out-position, and the open space is specifically the smaller-broker, fully-done-for-you tier they don't focus on.

## Strengths
- **Forced, recurring, license-tied demand** — brokers can't skip it.
- **A founder already knows this world** (insurance) — a real edge for trust and sales.
- **Smart, clean money setup**: paid in advance, and it never holds the tax money (which removes a big legal/financial headache).
- **Easy ways to find customers**: there's an industry association (WSIA) and public broker lists, plus almost no good online content for the panicked "how do I file this?" searches.

## Weaknesses
- **The work is genuinely messy.** There are ~50 states plus ~15 stamping offices, and **most of their websites have no easy automatic connection** — a human often has to type filings in by hand. If that hand-work is heavy, this becomes a people-heavy business instead of a software business. This is the #1 concern.
- **Zero room for error.** A single missed deadline can risk a broker's license — the exact thing they paid you to protect — so every filing must be checked carefully, which adds human cost.
- **One modern rival already here** (InsCipher) — the lane isn't totally empty.
- **Small, cautious, slow-to-trust buyer group** — sales take time.

Four of five experts said "go"; the operations expert said "fix it first." Its lowest single score was a **4** (from Legal, for how complicated the 50-states-plus-stamping-offices maze is).

**What the experts liked most:** Strong forced demand, a founder who knows the industry, a clean money model that avoids touching the tax dollars, and cheap ways to reach customers.

**What worried them most:** The **messy manual filing** across dozens of government websites (is it software or a back-office?), the **no-room-for-error** pressure, and that there's already one modern competitor.

**The tests that could push it over the bar:** Measure the real minutes-per-filing across the busiest states, and start with just a few high-volume states instead of all 50. If the hand-work is manageable, the operations score rises and it gets close.
