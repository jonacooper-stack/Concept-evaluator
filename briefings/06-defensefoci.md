# Briefing #6 — DefenseFOCIdesk
*(Council average: 6.68 / 10. Verdict: near-miss — the strongest "can't skip it" demand, but the scariest delivery and legal risks.)*

## The big idea, in plain words
Some small companies do secret/classified work for the U.S. military. To do that, they need a **facility security clearance**. If such a company has any **foreign ownership or investors** (called "FOCI" — Foreign Ownership, Control, or Influence), the government (an agency called DCSA) requires them to set up and maintain a strict, ongoing security program to prove the foreign owners can't access secrets: special board rules, yearly self-inspections, security plans, training, and recurring reviews.

If they let this slip or fail a government review, their **clearance can be suspended** — which means they **can't do classified work anymore**, which can kill the company. So it's a forced, never-ending, life-or-death requirement.

This business runs that security-paperwork program for them: software keeps the calendar, plans, and records; a **cleared expert** handles the parts that legally require someone with a security clearance; everything is delivered remotely.

## Who buys it and why they're stuck
Small defense contractors with foreign ownership. They're stuck because keeping the clearance is the price of doing (very lucrative) classified work, and the rules are strict and constant. This has been growing because of more foreign investment in defense companies and tighter government enforcement.

## How it makes money
- A **yearly subscription per cleared facility**, plus a managed self-inspection package — roughly **$20,000 to $60,000 a year** (these are bigger contracts than most ideas).
- The founders would only need about **30 customers** to hit the income goal — but that's both good (few needed) and bad (the total market is small).

## Who else is out there (the competition)
Very sleepy, which is good: **retired security officers working solo**, billing by the hour, with no software and no marketing. The big consultancies ignore these small accounts, and modern compliance software can't touch a classified, government-specific niche. So no real modern competitor — the most "protected" open lane of any idea.

## Strengths
- **The single strongest "can't skip it" demand** in the whole study — lose the clearance, lose the business.
- **Sleepy, beatable competitors** and almost no modern rival.
- **Big-ticket contracts** mean few customers are needed.
- **Customers basically never quit.**
- Fits the founders' compliance background, and is the closest cousin to the "gold-standard" example business (managed CMMC readiness).

## Weaknesses
- **It needs a person with a security clearance — and the founders aren't.** Some parts legally require a cleared individual, so the whole business leans on **one hard-to-replace cleared expert**. You can't just "go buy" a clearance. That's a single point of failure for both delivery and credibility.
- **The scariest legal risk of all eight.** If a prepared document turns out false, there are criminal-law dangers (False Claims Act, export-control rules) that **insurance won't cover**. This is why the legal score was the lowest in the whole study (a 3 on some measures).
- **You'd be holding extremely sensitive data.** A database of many defense contractors' security weak spots is a target for foreign spies — so the company itself would need a heavy, expensive security program (the very thing it sells), which fights against being a lean startup.
- **A tiny, trust-gated market.** Maybe only a few thousand possible customers nationwide, and they're cautious and slow to trust outsiders.

**What the experts liked most:** The strongest forced demand in the field, sleepy competitors, big contracts, and customers who never leave.

**What worried them most:** (1) The **cleared-expert single point of failure** — you can't run it without that person. (2) The **uninsurable criminal-law risk** if something is wrong. (3) The **heavy security burden** of holding such sensitive data, which makes it hard to stay lean.
