# Briefing #5 — RetireFiler
*(Council average: 6.9 / 10. Verdict: near-miss with a split personality — best money score, worst marketing score. The catch: people may not think they need it.)*

## The big idea, in plain words
When a small company offers its employees a 401(k) retirement plan, the company has a long list of yearly government paperwork it must do — the biggest being **Form 5500**, plus mailing required notices to employees, keeping an insurance "bond," and updating the plan document when laws change. Miss these and there are **steep fines** (hundreds of dollars per day), and the **business owner is personally on the hook** as the legal "plan administrator."

Here's the twist: a lot of this is *supposed* to be handled by the company's existing helpers (the "TPA" and recordkeeper). But responsibilities are split among several parties, and things fall through the cracks — a notice never goes out, a deadline gets missed, a 5500 is filed late.

This business is an **independent watchdog** for the owner: software maps every deadline and obligation, a specialist double-checks that the existing helpers actually did each item, mails the notices, and keeps an "audit-ready file" proving everything was done. It does **not** replace the existing helpers — it watches over them.

## Who buys it and why they're stuck
Small businesses with a 401(k) (10–200 employees) that use the older, "split-up" setup (a separate recordkeeper and TPA), rather than the modern all-in-one providers. They're stuck because the obligations are forced by law, never-ending, fined if missed, and the owner is personally liable.

## How it makes money
- A **subscription per plan**, plus a setup fee and fees to fix late filings — roughly **$150 to $600 a month per plan**.
- To hit the income goal, the founders would need roughly **450–550 plans** — a tiny slice of the hundreds of thousands of small plans out there.
- The money side is excellent: paid in advance, cheap to start, and one expert can watch many plans because most plans need almost no attention in a given week.

## Who else is out there (the competition)
- **Modern all-in-one 401(k) companies** (Guideline, Human Interest, Vestwell) handle this — but only for plans **on their own platform**. This idea deliberately avoids competing with them and targets the older, split-up plans instead. Smart move.
- **The TPAs themselves** already file the 5500 — so they'll say "we already do this."
- **The sharpest rival:** "3(16) administrators" (like Pentegra) who offer a *stronger* version — they will **legally take the liability off the owner**. This idea chose *not* to do that, which is a marketing disadvantage.
- **Sleepy hourly ERISA consultants** — easy to beat, but unmarketed.

## Strengths
- **Forced, recurring, fined, personally-liable** demand — in substance, a real painkiller.
- **The best money model of all eight ideas** (the finance expert gave it the highest single financial score in the whole study).
- **Clean and easy to run** from a laptop; the software is straightforward; no special license required.
- **A clever free way to find customers**: the government publishes every 5500 filing, including which ones were **late** — so the founders can reach out to a company that *just filed late* and show them their own mistake.

## Weaknesses
- **The "do they even think they need it?" problem (the big one).** The owner usually believes "my TPA and advisor already handle this." So before you can sell, you have to **convince them they have a gap** — and our rules say if you have to *teach* a customer that they have a problem, it's usually the **wrong problem**. In plain terms: it's a painkiller in reality but feels like a "nice to have" to the buyer. This is why the marketing score was the lowest of any idea (5.7).
- **You're blamed for misses you can't fully control.** The whole pitch is "we make sure nothing slips," but the actual filing is done by the *other* helpers. If they drop the ball, the customer still blames you. The value promise and the legal risk are the same thing.
- **A stronger competitor offers more** (the 3(16) firms take on the legal liability; this idea doesn't).

This idea had the **biggest split** of the eight: the money/operations/tech experts loved it (7.3–7.7), but the marketing expert flagged the most serious concern in the whole study (5.7). Its lowest single score was a **4** (Legal). The report-card total is dragged down mostly by the "do they think they need it?" problem, which hurts the most important category (must-have demand).

**What the experts liked most:** Excellent money model, easy to run and build, no license needed, and a clever "show them their own late filing" sales trick.

**What worried them most:** That buyers **don't feel the gap** (they think it's already covered), the **blame-for-others'-misses** risk, and a **stronger competitor** that takes on the liability.

**The one test that decides it:** Reach out to 50–100 companies that *just filed late*, show them their mistake, and see if they'll pay. If that "wake-up call" sales approach works, the idea jumps. If even late-filers shrug, it stays a "nice to have."
