# Briefing #1 — ContractorPrevailingWage
*(Best of the eight. Council average: 7.12 / 10. Verdict: near-miss — fix one thing and it could pass.)*

## The big idea, in plain words
When a construction company works on a project that uses government money (a school, a road, a courthouse), the law says it has to pay its workers a set minimum wage called the "prevailing wage." And every single week, the company has to send the government a special report — called **certified payroll** — that proves it paid everyone correctly. They sign it under penalty of perjury (meaning lying is a crime).

Here's the catch: if they don't send that weekly report, or send it wrong, **the government stops paying them** for the work they already did. Money gets frozen. So contractors have to do this, every week, perfectly, or they don't get paid.

This business does that paperwork **for them**. It's a service plus software: the contractor sends over their payroll data, the software fills out the weekly report and checks it for mistakes, and a trained helper handles anything tricky and submits it to the right government website. The contractor never has to worry about it.

## Who buys it and why they're stuck
The customers are small and medium construction companies and their subcontractors (electricians, plumbers, site crews) that bid on government-funded jobs. They are stuck because:
- It's **forced** — the law requires it.
- It's **weekly** — not once a year, but constantly.
- It's **already in their budget** — they know it's a cost of doing public work.
- Missing it **hurts immediately** — withheld payments, fines, even being banned from future government jobs.

There are also more of these customers than before, because the government has been spending a lot on roads and bridges, which pulls more contractors into these rules.

## How it makes money
- A **monthly subscription per active job** — so the more government projects a contractor has running, the more they pay.
- Plus a **setup fee** when a new contractor signs up, and **fees for handling audits**.
- The price would be roughly **a few hundred to a couple thousand dollars per project per month**, so a mid-size contractor with several jobs might pay a few thousand a month.

The money math works: the founders would only need on the order of **35–55 customers** to hit their income goal, out of **tens of thousands** of contractors who do this kind of work. So there are plenty of potential customers.

## Who else is out there (the competition)
The current options are weak and old-fashioned, which is good:
- **Old "certified payroll" software** like LCPtracker and Points North — these exist but are clunky, and they make the contractor do the work themselves. They're somewhat established but not modern or loved.
- **Sleepy local payroll bureaus** that do it by hand.
- **Many contractors just do it themselves** in spreadsheets and live in fear of an audit.

Importantly, **no big modern tech company owns this**. That's the kind of "open lane" we want. The clever marketing trick: government bid results are **public**, so the founders can see exactly which contractor just won a covered job — and reach out the same week, when the pain is fresh.

## Strengths
- **Demand is about as strong as it gets** — weekly, forced, money-blocking if missed.
- **Customers almost never quit** — because dropping the service means risking frozen payments.
- **Cheap to start, runs from a laptop**, money comes in before costs go out.
- **A smart, free way to find customers** (public bid-win data).

## Weaknesses
- **The hidden labor question.** The promise is "software does it," but every state and government website is different and messy. If each customer secretly needs a lot of hand-work, the business turns into a "people-heavy" operation with thinner profits — more like a staffing agency than a software company. Nobody knows the real number yet.
- **Scary legal blame.** The reports are signed under penalty of perjury, and on federally funded jobs a false report can trigger a serious law called the False Claims Act. If the service makes a bad mistake, the consequences are large and hard to fully insure against. (This is managed by having the contractor be the one who signs, plus insurance — but it's the sharpest risk.)
- **The buyers are "offline."** Contractors don't shop online for this; they're reached one at a time, which makes growth slower.

Five experts reviewed it. Here are their scores out of 10:

| CTO | Is the software easy to build? | **6.7** |
| **Average** | | **7.12** |
| Report card | Out of 100 | **~78** |

**To pass, it needed an 8.0 average, no score under 7, and 80/100. It missed all three — but by the smallest margin of any idea.** Its lowest single score was a 5.

**What the experts liked most:** This is the closest match to the "perfect" kind of business — a forced, weekly, budgeted must-have, with sleepy competitors and a clever public-data way to find customers. The marketing and money sides both scored high.

**What worried them most:** Two things, and they're the same theme. (1) Is it really software, or secretly a lot of manual labor? (2) The "you signed off on it" legal blame if a filing is wrong. The reviewers couldn't tell, without a test, whether the profit margin is "great" (software-like) or "just okay" (people-heavy).

**The one test that could push it over the bar:** Run the real paperwork for a handful of contractors for a couple of months and **measure the minutes of hand-work per filing**. If it's low, this likely jumps over 8.0. If it's high, it's a lower-margin labor business.
