# Briefing #2 — VendorTrust
*(Council average: 7.04 / 10. Verdict: strong near-miss — the cleanest "open lane," but it must prove people will pay.)*

## The big idea, in plain words
When a small company sells products to a giant buyer — think a small food brand selling to Walmart or Kroger, or a parts maker selling to a big factory — the giant buyer makes them keep a pile of paperwork up to date all the time. Things like proof of insurance, safety and food-quality certificates, "we don't use forced labor" promises, and long questionnaires. Each of these expires and has to be re-done on a schedule.

If the small supplier lets any of it lapse, the big buyer can **put them "on hold"**: block their orders, stop paying them, or even drop them as a supplier. That's a direct hit to the small company's income.

This business keeps all of that paperwork current **for the supplier**, across **every** big buyer they sell to. Software tracks what's due and when; a helper fills out the questionnaires, collects the updated insurance certificate from the supplier's insurance agent, and uploads everything into each buyer's website on time.

## Who buys it and why they're stuck
Small and medium suppliers ($1M–$50M in sales) that sell to big retailers, factories, hospitals, or hotel chains. They're stuck because:
- It's **forced** by the big buyer (no paperwork, no orders).
- It's **recurring** (documents expire on rolling schedules).
- It **multiplies** — a supplier selling to five big buyers has five different websites, five sets of rules, five sets of deadlines.
- Missing it **cuts off real money** immediately.

This is getting worse, not better, because big buyers keep adding new requirements (sustainability, anti-forced-labor, data security).

## How it makes money
- A **monthly subscription per supplier**, priced by how many big buyers they serve and how much paperwork is involved — roughly **$200 to $1,000 a month**.
- Plus a **setup fee** and fees for extra questionnaires.

To hit the income goal, the founders would need a few hundred suppliers — a tiny slice of the hundreds of thousands of small suppliers who sell to big buyers.

## Who else is out there (the competition)
This is the best part: **the lane is basically empty on the supplier's side.**
- There are big, well-funded software companies — but they all work for the **buyer** (helping Walmart chase its suppliers), not for the small supplier.
- There are insurance-certificate tracking tools — again, built for the buyer side, and only for insurance, not the whole paperwork pile.
- The closest "supplier pays" example is in a different industry (construction-safety networks like Avetta and ISN) — and importantly, **they prove suppliers will pay** for this kind of thing. But they're in construction, not retail/food, so this tier is wide open.
- Today, suppliers just handle it with an overworked office manager.

So no big modern competitor owns the small-supplier side. That's exactly the open lane we want.

## Strengths
- **Forced, recurring pain with a direct money consequence** (lose the buyer = lose the income).
- **An empty competitive lane** — nobody serves the small supplier across all their buyers.
- **Cheap to start, runs from a laptop, money comes in before costs.**
- **Customers stick around** — because lapsing blocks their own sales.
- A **great low-cost sales partner**: insurance agents already handle the supplier's insurance certificate, hear about all these demands, and would happily refer the annoying rest of the work.

## Weaknesses
- **Will they actually pay? (The biggest unknown.)** In construction, suppliers pay because the buyer *forces* them onto a paid network. Here, **nobody forces them** — so a supplier might just keep dumping it on the office manager for free. Whether small suppliers will pay $200–$1,000/month is the make-or-break question, and it's unproven.
- **The hidden labor question (again).** Every big buyer's website is different and changes often, and many have no easy automatic connection. If filling them out stays manual, profits look more like a staffing agency than software.
- **Hard to find customers efficiently.** "Small supplier to a big buyer" is a scattered group with no single place they gather — so getting the first few hundred is a grind.
- **Easy to copy.** The advantage is being first and doing more, not a deep secret — a funded competitor could copy it in a year.

**It missed the 8.0 bar, but notably it had no score below 5** — the most "even" of the strong ideas. Three of the five experts said "go" (money, operations, legal); two said "fix it first" (marketing, tech).

**What the experts liked most:** The cleanest open lane of any idea, a forced/recurring/budgeted pain, an easy operation that runs from a laptop, no licensing headaches, and a ready-made referral partner in insurance agents.

**What worried them most:** Two things. (1) **Unproven willingness to pay** — because no buyer forces suppliers onto it, it could turn out to be a "nice to have" people won't pay for. This is the single heaviest worry. (2) The **manual-labor question** — is it software or a back-office? Both are cheap to test before building anything.

**The tests that could push it over the bar:** (1) Interview 15+ real suppliers and see how many, without being prompted, say "yes, I'd pay to make this go away." (2) Run 10 real accounts and measure the hand-work hours. If both come back strong, several scores rise and it could clear the bar.
