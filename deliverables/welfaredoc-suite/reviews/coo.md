# COO REVIEW — Welfare-Plan Compliance Documents & Testing Service

## ONE-PARAGRAPH OPERATIONS READ
This is operationally one of the most tractable shapes a two-founder bootstrapped team could pick: the unit of work is a document/test/filing that software generates from a structured intake, the delivery is fully remote and digital, there are no trucks, no installs, no site visits, and the channel (brokers/TPAs) does the customer acquisition and most of the hand-holding. The real operational risk is not labor or logistics — it is the thin but unavoidable wedge of human review and liability ownership on regulated work product (a wrong Form 5500 or a botched §105(h) test has consequences), which means the founders cannot be 100% self-serve on day one and need a defensible review-and-sign-off process plus an ERISA-competent reviewer in the loop. Net: this is a remote-first, software-leveraged, recurring operation that two adaptive generalists can run lean, with the bottleneck landing on regulated-work-product QA and 5500-filing-season concentration, not on headcount or geography.

## THE TUESDAY-IN-MARCH WALKTHROUGH
It's a Tuesday in March, roughly the 12-month mark. The business runs on a broker/TPA-facing portal. Say there are ~25 broker/agency/TPA partners live, and across their books they've onboarded ~600 employer plans (some documents-only, some on the full documents + 5500 + testing tier). March is meaningful: calendar-year plans (the majority) have a plan year that ended Dec 31, the §125/§105(h)/§129/§79 tests for the prior plan year want to be run early in the new year, open-enrollment-driven plan-design changes from Jan 1 are flushing through, and the Form 5500 clock (7 months after plan-year-end = July 31 for calendar plans) is ticking. So March is a "testing + document-refresh + early-5500-prep" day — useful, because it shows the work is spread, not a single November cliff.

Jon starts in the partner console. Overnight automated jobs flagged 14 plans whose carriers or plan designs changed at 1/1 renewal (pulled from the intake/renewal questionnaire), meaning 14 wrap SPDs and a handful of §125 POP documents need regeneration and re-issue. The software does the generation; Jon's job is exception handling: of those 14, the software auto-clears 11 cleanly; 3 have a weird answer (a self-insured component a broker mis-tagged, a stop-loss arrangement, an HRA that interacts with §105(h)) routed to a human review queue. Jon spends ~15 minutes each clearing those 3, pinging a broker for one clarification. He pushes regenerated documents to the employer-facing portal for e-sign and distribution; the system logs proof-of-distribution into the vault.

Mid-morning, Mike (or a part-time ERISA-competent reviewer by this stage) works the nondiscrimination-testing queue. ~40 employers uploaded census/payroll data via a guided template. The platform runs §125, §105(h), §129, and §79 tests and outputs dated pass/fail with the math shown. ~35 pass clean and emit the audit-ready packet automatically. ~5 fail something — where the human judgment lives: confirm it's not a data-mapping artifact (the #1 cause of false fails is a miscoded HCE list or wrong plan-year), attach the platform's pre-drafted correction guidance. Each genuine failure review is ~20–40 minutes → ~5 × ~30 min ≈ 2.5 hours of skilled review for the day's batch — concentrated but bounded.

Around lunch, a CFO at a 220-employee direct-channel employer emails: DOL information request, needs wrap SPD, POP, and last 5500 immediately. Because everything is in the vault, this is a 10-minute retrieval — exactly the moment the product earns its renewal. Early afternoon: Jon does a 30-minute Zoom onboarding with a new TPA partner's two account managers — the growth motor, repeatable and scripted. Late afternoon is 5500 pre-season prep: the platform built a per-plan 5500 worklist for July 31, pre-populating Schedule A data where available and flagging missing pieces. Mike triages ~10, sending broker requests for missing inputs so July isn't a wall. Support tickets across the day are light — "how do I map my census file," "the broker entered the wrong plan year" — ~8–12 tickets, an hour total. Nobody drove anywhere. Nothing was installed.

## CAPACITY MATH
Target framing: ~$300K/founder by mo12 (~$600K combined), ~$500K+/founder by mo24. [ASSUMED] blended ACV per employer plan of $800–$2,500/yr by tier; take a blended ~$1,200/plan/yr.

**Revenue side (to size unit volume):** channel-sold means a chunk of headline price is the broker's margin. [ASSUMED] if the platform nets ~$700–$1,000 per plan, then ~$1.0–$1.2M net platform revenue ≈ ~1,200–1,500 active plans. mo24 (~$1M+/founder) implies ~3,000–6,000+ plans.

**Throughput side (the part that's mine):** per-plan annual recurring work, software doing the bulk and humans the exceptions:
1. Document generation/refresh (wrap SPD + POP): [ASSUMED] ~70–85% auto-clear with zero human minutes in a year; the ~15–30% that change need ~10–20 min review. Blended ≈ 5–8 human min/plan/yr.
2. Form 5500 (subset — only 100+ participant/funded plans): software pre-populates; human verifies and authorizes the e-file. [ASSUMED] ~25–40% of plans are 5500-eligible at ~20–45 min each. Blended ≈ 8–15 human min/plan/yr.
3. Nondiscrimination testing (subset on testing tier): [ASSUMED] ~40–60% buy testing; ~85–90% pass clean (~0 min), ~10–15% fail needing ~20–40 min. Blended ≈ 6–12 human min/plan/yr.
4. Calendar/reminders/support: ~0.5–1 ticket/plan/yr at ~10 min → ≈ 5–10 human min/plan/yr.

**Total blended ~25–45 min/plan/yr; working midpoint ~35 min/plan/yr.** Honest caveat: most of that is concentrated in Q1 (testing) and June–July (5500 crunch), so the annualized average understates peak-week load — size to peak, not average.

**Headcount math at scale:**
- ~1,500 plans (mo12 zone): 1,500 × ~35 min = ~52,500 min ≈ ~875 hrs/yr ≈ ~17 hrs/week spread over the year — two founders absorb it AND run sales/product. Peak (~50% of labor in ~14 weeks): ~440 hrs / ~14 weeks ≈ ~31 hrs/week — inside two founders plus a fractional reviewer.
- ~5,000 plans (mo24+): 5,000 × ~35 min ≈ ~2,900 hrs/yr ≈ ~1.6 annualized FTE of delivery labor — but seasonal, so staff ~2–3 trained reviewers slammed in crunch windows. ERISA-literate analysts / former TPA filing staff at [ASSUMED] ~$60–95K loaded, or seasonal/contract capacity.

**Decisive finding:** the labor curve is sub-linear to revenue because software does generation/tests/pre-population; humans touch only exceptions and authorizations. The bottleneck is not "how many hands" — it's "who is qualified to put their name on a failed §105(h) test or authorize a 5500 e-file," i.e., supervised regulated-work-product QA concentrated into two annual crunch windows.

**Comparable anchor (supports the 8s):** this is a live, paid market — ERISAfire, Mineral (formerly ThinkHR/Mammoth), ERISA Pros, various TPAs, and compliance modules in bswift / Employee Navigator's compliance partners. Per-document wrap/POP ~$300–$750, welfare 5500 prep ~$300–$1,000, NDT ~$300–$1,500/plan/yr [ASSUMED]. Cheap test: pull ERISAfire and Employee Navigator partner pricing; call two regional TPAs as a broker to confirm wholesale rates.

## TOP 3 OPERATIONAL STRENGTHS
- Maximum logistics tractability: 100% remote, digital, recurring delivery — no trucks, installs, site visits, inventory, or FX; the entire operation runs from a portal and a vault.
- Sub-linear labor curve proven by capacity math: at ~35 human-min/plan/yr, two founders service ~1,500 plans in ~17 hrs/week and a tiny analyst team clears ~5,000 plans at ~1.6 annualized FTE.
- Channel leverage on acquisition AND first-line ops: selling through brokers/TPAs means the partner does customer acquisition and absorbs routine client questions.

## TOP 3 OPERATIONAL RISKS
- Regulated-work-product QA and liability ownership: someone qualified must stand behind every authorized 5500 e-file and every failed §105(h)/§125 test; errors carry daily-accruing penalties and fiduciary exposure — needs a hard sign-off gate, audit trail, and E&O coverage from day one.
- Seasonality concentration: testing (Q1) and the 5500 crunch (June–July) compress labor into two windows; annualized FTE math understates peak-week load, so the team must be sized to the crunch.
- Channel dependency and competitive response: GTM leans on brokers/TPAs, and the obvious incumbent counter is a benefits-admin platform (Employee Navigator, bswift, Mineral) bundling wrap/5500/testing as a near-free value-add.

## BIGGEST SINGLE RISK
The single operational issue most likely to bite is **liability-bearing quality control on regulated work product colliding with the seasonal crunch.** Everything is delightfully tractable until a human has to authorize the electronic filing of a Form 5500 or sign off that a §105(h) test "passes," because if that output is wrong the customer eats >$2,000/day penalties or loses tax-qualified status and turns around looking for who told them it was fine. That means the work is NOT purely software self-serve: there must be a competent ERISA-literate reviewer in the loop, a documented sign-off and audit trail, and E&O/professional-liability insurance — and that reviewer's scarce hours are exactly the hours slammed in the two annual peak windows. The failure mode is foreseeable: the platform scales plan count beautifully on the software side, then a crunch-week surge of 5500 verifications and failed-test reviews swamps the one or two qualified humans, and either a deadline slips (penalties for the client, reputational hit to the channel) or quality slips under pressure. This doesn't make me say "two people can't do this" — the annualized labor is light and the talent is hireable — but the founders must treat regulated-QA capacity and the crunch-window staffing plan as a first-class design problem, and decide early how much liability they carry on authorized filings versus positioning the tool as "prepares/you-authorize."

## QUESTIONS THE FOUNDERS MUST ANSWER
- Who authorizes a Form 5500 e-file and signs off on a failed/passed nondiscrimination test — the employer, the broker, or you — and where exactly does professional liability sit? (Cheap test: one E&O quote for ERISA-document/5500 work and one ERISA-attorney opinion on filing authorization.)
- What is the true peak-week load? Model the June–July 5500 crunch and Q1 testing surge at 1,500 and 5,000 plans: how many filings/tests land in the worst single week, how many human-hours at your exception rates, and what's the seasonal/contract staffing plan?
- What share of generated documents and run tests genuinely auto-clear with zero human minutes vs. route to manual review? Build a 50-plan dry run against real (anonymized) inputs and measure the true exception rate — the ~35 min/plan/yr estimate lives or dies on this number.
- How do you defend the channel and per-plan economics if Employee Navigator / bswift / Mineral bundle wrap + 5500 + testing as a near-free add-on?

## RECOMMENDATION: GO (operations)
From a pure operations standpoint this is a strong GO — the lean, remote-first, software-leveraged, recurring-revenue shape the objectives want, with no field operations, no physical supply chain, no geographic footprint, a sub-linear labor curve shown with concrete capacity math, and a hireable national talent pool for the one role that needs depth. The two caveats that keep me from a 9 are real but manageable, not structural: (1) the work is regulated and liability-bearing, so it requires a competent human QA gate, sign-off workflow, and E&O coverage rather than pure self-serve; and (2) demand is seasonally concentrated, so staffing must be sized to peak weeks. Neither threatens "can two people do this" — they threaten "can two people do this carelessly," which is a process-design instruction. Build the sign-off/audit-trail workflow and crunch-window staffing plan deliberately, validate the auto-clear-vs-manual-review exception rate with a 50-plan dry run, and the operational engine is genuinely excellent.
