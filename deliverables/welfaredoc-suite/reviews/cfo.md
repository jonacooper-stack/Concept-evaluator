# CFO REVIEW — Welfare-Plan Compliance Documents & Testing Service

## ONE-PARAGRAPH FINANCIAL READ
The numbers pencil, and they pencil well for a bootstrap. This is a recurring, deadline-forced, SaaS-margin compliance subscription with named, profitable comparables already charging real money for the exact same deliverables (wrap SPD, POP, Form 5500, nondiscrimination testing), and a broker/TPA channel that turns one signed agency into dozens-to-hundreds of employer plans without per-account selling. The financial fragility is not the unit economics (which are strong) — it is the channel: revenue is concentrated through a small number of broker partners who can churn, re-price, or in-source you, and the realistic path to ~$300K/founder leans heavily on landing and retaining channel partners, not on the per-plan economics, which are excellent. On absolute merit the financial profile is among the better shapes a bootstrap can take: low startup capital, annual upfront billing that self-funds growth, 80%+ software-leaning gross margins, and a renew-every-plan-year retention dynamic baked into the law itself.

## THE MATH

**Pricing anchors (named comparables, real money in this exact category):**
- Wrap SPD / plan documents: ERISA wrap document services (e.g., the long tail of TPAs and document vendors, plus platforms like Mineral/ThinkHR, Threads, and document generators) commonly retail in the **$150–$500/employer/yr** range for a maintained wrap+POP bundle. [ASSUMED midpoint ~$300; verify by pulling 5–10 broker-facing rate cards.]
- Form 5500 preparation/filing for a welfare plan: typically **$500–$1,500 per filing per year** at TPAs and ERISA filing shops; ftwilliam.com (Wolters Kluwer) and ASC are the back-end engines many of these shops run on. [ASSUMED ~$750–$1,000.]
- Nondiscrimination testing (§125/§105(h)/§129): commonly **$300–$1,000/yr** bundled, often sold alongside FSA/HRA TPA administration. [ASSUMED ~$500.]

So a single employer buying the **full slate** today already spends roughly **$1,000–$3,000/yr** across these line items, frequently split across multiple vendors. That existing, fragmented spend is the budget line — this is not market creation.

**Productized tiering [ASSUMED, ranged, testable via broker rate-card interviews]:**
- Tier 1 (Documents only — wrap SPD + POP): **$200–$350/plan/yr** → model **$250**.
- Tier 2 (Documents + nondiscrimination testing): **$500–$800/plan/yr** → model **$650**.
- Tier 3 (Documents + testing + Form 5500): **$1,200–$2,000/plan/yr** → model **$1,500**.
- Blended ARPU across a realistic mix (many small employers take docs-only or docs+testing; the 100+-participant plans that owe a 5500 take Tier 3): model **blended net-to-us ARPU of $600–$900/plan/yr → use $750**.

Note on channel economics: when sold white-label/referral through a broker, the broker keeps a markup. The relevant number to ME is **net-to-platform per plan**, which I've already modeled as the $750 blended figure (i.e., the broker's resale markup sits on top and is not my revenue). If priced as wholesale-to-broker, $750 net is realistic; if direct-to-employer, ARPU is higher but acquisition is slower.

**Gross margin:**
- Cost to serve per plan/yr is overwhelmingly software + a thin layer of human compliance QA/support: document generation and testing are templated/automated; 5500 e-filing is automated against DOL EFAST2. [ASSUMED COGS $75–$150/plan/yr including support, hosting, e-sign, and a fractional compliance reviewer.] That implies **gross margin ~80–90%** at the $750 ARPU. This is consistent with vertical compliance-SaaS comparables (e.g., Carta, Gusto's compliance modules, and ERISA-document SaaS) that run 70–85%+ gross margins.

**Startup capital:**
- Build is software (intake → document assembly → testing engine → 5500 generator → vault/calendar) plus content/templates authored with ERISA counsel. The single largest "capital" item is **founder build time + ~$15K–$40K of outside ERISA counsel** to author and bless the document templates and testing logic. Cash outlay to first revenue is plausibly **$20K–$60K** (counsel + tooling + e-sign/EFAST integration + entity/E&O insurance binder). No inventory, no facilities, no field ops. This is a low-four/five-figure-to-low-six startup — capital-light.

**Path to the founder-income ladder (target ~$300K/founder ≈ $600K combined by mo12; ~$1.0M+ combined by mo24):**

Two ways to hit ~$600K combined owner income by ~mo12:

*Model A — net-to-us $750 ARPU, ~85% gross margin:*
- $600K combined owner income ≈ requires roughly **$800K–$1.0M ARR** (after modest opex: a part-time compliance reviewer, support, software, counsel retainer).
- At $750 ARPU → **~1,100–1,350 active plans**.

Is ~1,200 plans by mo12 plausible through the channel?
- A single mid-size benefits agency carries **dozens to a few hundred employer clients** in the 50–500-employee band. Landing **10–25 producing broker/TPA partners**, each rolling out the service across **40–120 of their employer clients**, gets you to **400–3,000 plans**. So ~1,200 plans is **~15–25 partners at ~60–80 plans each** — aggressive but not heroic for a channel motion, and squarely in reach by mo18–24 even if mo12 is more like **$300K–$450K combined**.
- Conservative ramp (channel sales cycles are slow; brokers move on their renewal calendars): **mo12 ≈ 500–800 plans (~$375K–$600K ARR; ~$200K–$350K combined owner income)**, **mo24 ≈ 1,500–2,500 plans (~$1.1M–$1.9M ARR; ~$600K–$1.0M+ combined owner income)**. That straddles the ladder: month-12 is plausibly slightly under the $300K/founder rung on a slow channel ramp, month-24 clears ~$500K/founder comfortably. The ladder is reachable with conservative-to-moderate assumptions.

**CAC / LTV:**
- Channel CAC: acquisition cost is the cost to land a broker partner (founder-led sales, demos, co-marketing), then near-zero marginal cost to add each employer plan under that partner. Effective **per-plan CAC is low** because one partner amortizes across many plans. [ASSUMED fully-loaded blended CAC $150–$500/plan; lower under productive partners, higher direct-to-employer.]
- LTV: ARPU $750 × gross margin ~85% × **retention**. Retention is structurally high because the obligation is legal and annual — the wrap must be maintained, the 5500 re-filed, the tests re-run every plan year; switching means re-papering documents and re-onboarding. [ASSUMED gross logo retention 85–92% at the employer level.] At 88% retention, average life ≈ ~8 years → **LTV ≈ $750 × 0.85 × ~6–8 ≈ $3,800–$5,100 per plan**. Against $150–$500 CAC that is **LTV/CAC ≈ 8–30x** — excellent, with the heavy caveat that LTV concentrates at the partner level (lose a partner, lose their whole book at once).

**Bottom line of the math:** per-plan economics are strong and well-supported by named comparables; the business self-funds on annual upfront billing; the binding constraint is **how fast and how stickily you sign and retain broker partners**, not whether each plan is profitable.

## TOP 3 FINANCIAL STRENGTHS
- Legally-forced, annually-recurring revenue with structural retention: the wrap must be maintained, the 5500 re-filed, and the tests re-run every plan year — renewal is written into the statute, which underwrites high LTV and clean financeability.
- Excellent capital and working-capital profile: ~$20K–$60K to first revenue, annual upfront billing, ~80–90% gross margin, and channel-amortized CAC together produce a negative-working-capital, self-funding model that needs no raise.
- Channel leverage on the upside: one signed broker/TPA partner converts into dozens-to-hundreds of employer plans at near-zero marginal acquisition cost, so the revenue ceiling for two founders is high without a sales team.

## TOP 3 FINANCIAL RISKS
- Channel concentration and partner power: a small number of broker/TPA partners can carry a large share of revenue; any one churning, re-pricing, or in-sourcing removes a whole book at once.
- Net-pricing compression in the channel: brokers want a markup, several deliverables (POP docs, basic wrap) are near-commoditized with sub-$200 substitutes, and bundling pressure could pull net-to-platform ARPU below the $750 model.
- Compliance-error / E&O cost surprises: a wrong testing result or a missed/incorrect 5500 creates client penalty exposure and potential liability claims; E&O premiums, indemnities, and QA staffing could quietly compress the modeled margin.

## BIGGEST SINGLE RISK
The dominant financial risk is **channel concentration coupled with partner pricing power**. The entire attractiveness of the model — low CAC, fast scale, high LTV/CAC — rests on the broker/TPA channel amortizing acquisition across many plans. But that same structure means revenue is concentrated in a small number of intermediaries who control the customer relationship. If 5–10 partners represent the majority of plans, the loss, re-pricing, or in-sourcing of even two or three of them can erase a quarter's growth overnight, and the founders may never have a direct line to the underlying employers to defend the book. Brokers are also sophisticated resellers who will compress your net pricing toward the commoditized floor for the simpler deliverables (POP and basic wrap docs already have sub-$200 substitutes), and a large TPA or benefits-admin platform that decides this is strategic can simply build or buy the capability and cut you out of its book. So the unit economics that look like 8–30x LTV/CAC at the plan level can, at the portfolio level, behave like a handful of large, churnable, price-sensitive accounts. The mitigation is deliberately building partner diversity early, securing multi-year/auto-renew channel terms, and seeding a direct-to-employer book so no single partner is existential — but that mitigation is unproven and is the thing I'd want validated first.

## QUESTIONS THE FOUNDERS MUST ANSWER
- What is the realistic **net-to-platform ARPU after the broker's markup** for each tier, and how many partners would it take for the top 5 to represent under ~40% of revenue? (Validate with 8–10 broker rate-card interviews and a partner-concentration model.)
- What are **real-world retention/churn** numbers at both the employer-plan level and the partner level — how often do brokers switch compliance vendors, and what triggers it (price, M&A among agencies, a competing platform bundling it free)?
- What is the **fully-loaded cost and liability of compliance QA** — the human review needed per 5500 and per testing engagement, the E&O premium for testing/filing errors, and the indemnity terms brokers will demand — and how do those move the 80–90% gross margin?

## RECOMMENDATION: GO
The financial case is strong and well-supported by named comparables already charging real money for the identical deliverables: capital-light to launch, annual-prepay self-funding, 80–90% gross margin, legally-forced annual renewal, and a channel that gives two founders a high revenue ceiling without a sales org. The math clears the income ladder on conservative-to-moderate assumptions (mo24 comfortably above ~$500K/founder; mo12 plausibly near or modestly below the $300K/founder rung on a slow channel ramp). The one thing that keeps this from a clean 8+ overall is **channel concentration**, which is a real financial fragility rather than a stage artifact — so the GO is conditioned on the founders treating partner diversification, multi-year channel terms, and a seeded direct-to-employer book as first-order design choices, and on validating net-of-markup ARPU and broker-switching behavior before scaling.
