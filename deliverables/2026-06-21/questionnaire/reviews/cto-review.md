CTO REVIEW — Defense-Sub Security-Questionnaire Answer Engine ("auto-fill + interview-to-fill + learn")

ONE-PARAGRAPH TECHNICAL READ
This is a buildable, well-scoped RAG (retrieval-augmented generation) application with a thin custom wedge, and it sits squarely in the "duct-tape proven SaaS together" zone a two-founder bootstrap should live in — the only genuinely hard parts are matching-quality tuning and a non-technical-user interview UX, neither a research problem. The decisive de-risker is that one founder has built and operated this exact pattern in production before; the pattern (seed knowledge base, auto-fill inbound questionnaires, interview to fill gaps, re-feed completed forms) is a known quantity, not a bet, and the absence of reusable code costs dev-weeks, not feasibility. The two real technical hazards are not the build but the runtime: (1) wrong-but-confident LLM answers that a non-technical owner — who has no in-house CTO to sanity-check the hard controls — forwards to a prime or attests to the government, which is a liability surface, not a bug; and (2) strict per-customer data isolation so one sub's policies/answers can never bleed into another's, straightforward to engineer correctly but catastrophic if engineered lazily. Net: technically green-lit for a competent small team in roughly 12–18 dev-weeks to a revenue-earning MVP, with the engineering that matters concentrated in answer-confidence calibration, human-in-the-loop review gating, and tenant isolation rather than in any exotic capability.

ARCHITECTURE SKETCH
- Frontend: Next.js/React on Vercel. Standard SaaS dashboard — document upload, a questionnaire "workspace" (question list with auto-filled answers + confidence indicators), a plain-language interview flow (one question at a time, "answer this / route this to your IT provider"), and an export-to-the-prime's-format view. The hard UI problem is making the interview legible to a non-technical owner.
- Backend: A conventional API service (Python/FastAPI natural given the AI/embeddings ecosystem). Stripe for self-serve billing. Auth via a managed provider (Clerk/Auth0/WorkOS) — do not hand-roll.
- Data store: Postgres for tenant/user/questionnaire/answer records, plus a vector store for embeddings. The vector layer can be pgvector inside the same Postgres (preferred at this scale — one fewer system, trivial per-tenant row-level isolation) rather than a separate Pinecone/Weaviate. Object storage (S3) for uploaded policies/PDFs, encrypted, per-tenant prefixes.
- The wedge / real complexity (three places): (a) Document ingestion + chunking + embedding of heterogeneous, ugly inputs — policy PDFs, prior questionnaire spreadsheets in arbitrary formats, scanned audit reports (OCR for scans). (b) The matching/answer-generation core: embed an incoming question, retrieve the customer's most-similar stored Q/A and policy chunks, have an LLM draft an answer grounded in retrieved context, WITH a calibrated confidence score and a "no good basis found — must interview" fallback. (c) The learning loop: writing completed, human-approved answers back into the per-tenant knowledge base as new canonical Q/A pairs so the next form matches more. (a) and (b) are where quality is won or lost.
- Integrations: LLM API + embeddings API on the critical path. Stripe. Email. The SPRS "score" and gap-analysis are computed in-app against the 110 NIST 800-171 controls — there is no SPRS API to integrate; the customer enters their own score into the government system manually, which REDUCES integration risk (no fragile federal-system dependency).
- Async/background: A job queue for ingestion/OCR/embedding of large uploads. Lightweight; not Kafka-grade.
- ML/AI: No model training. Retrieval + prompt-engineering + off-the-shelf foundation models and embeddings — the correct low-risk posture. Per-customer "learning" is data accumulation in the knowledge base, NOT fine-tuning — keeps it boring and safe.
- Auth/hosting/tenancy: Multi-tenant, but with hard row-level / prefix-level tenant isolation as a first-class constraint because confidentiality is the product's whole promise. Retrieval queries MUST be tenant-scoped at the data layer, not just the app layer.

CRITICAL ASSUMPTIONS
- [ASSUMED] An LLM + retrieval over the customer's OWN documents can auto-fill ~80% of a questionnaire at a quality the owner will trust. Plausible (the founder did exactly this before) but 80% is a load-bearing marketing number. Cheap verify: 5–10 real prime flow-downs, seed a KB from a realistic sub policy set, measure actual auto-fill rate and acceptability by hand before production code. A weekend spike answers this.
- [ASSUMED] Per-customer corpora are large/structured enough to retrieve against. A 35-person shop may upload very little; if the seed corpus is sparse, auto-fill collapses toward "interview everything," weakening the wedge for the smallest customers. Verify: inventory what a real target sub possesses day one; design the interview-first cold-start path deliberately.
- [ASSUMED] Confidence/abstain can be made reliable enough to keep the LLM from confidently emitting wrong control attestations. The safety crux. Verify: prototype an abstain threshold and measure false-confidence rate (confident + wrong) on the test forms; if it can't be driven low, lean harder on mandatory human review of every security-control answer.
- [ASSUMED] The questionnaire formats are ingestible. Primes send Excel, Word, PDF, portal exports — heterogeneous, sometimes locked. Verify: collect 10–15 real specimens and confirm parseability.
- [ASSUMED] CMMC/NIST 800-171 content and primes' forms change at a pace two founders + one advisor can keep current (800-171 rev 2 → rev 3, CMMC phase-in). Verify: estimate annual content-maintenance hours with the advisor; decide founder task vs. contracted.

INFORMATION SECURITY POSTURE
Data collected: customer security policies, prior questionnaire responses, audit reports, gap-analysis answers — a detailed map of each small sub's security weaknesses. Critically, the design EXCLUDES the customer's CUI and live system data — the single best security decision: it keeps the company out of the CUI-handling regime, drastically shrinks the breach blast radius and the founders' own compliance burden, and means a breach exposes "how secure is this shop" rather than actual controlled defense data. That self-imposed boundary must be enforced in product (warn/strip if a user pastes CUI) and stated in terms, because a non-technical owner may upload sensitive things anyway.
Threat model: (1) Cross-tenant leakage — the gravest risk; one sub seeing another's policies/answers, or a retrieval query pulling another tenant's chunks, is both a confidentiality breach and a competitive-intelligence disaster among competing suppliers. Mitigation: tenant_id on every record, tenant-scoped vector queries enforced at the data layer, per-tenant S3 prefixes, automated isolation tests. (2) Account takeover exposing a sub's full weakness map — MFA, managed auth, least-privilege. (3) Prompt-injection/exfiltration via malicious uploaded documents — sanitize, scope retrieval strictly to the requesting tenant, never include cross-tenant context. (4) Vendor/sub-processor exposure — customer data flows to the LLM/embeddings provider; use enterprise/no-train tiers, sign DPAs, disclose sub-processors.
Controls at launch: encryption at rest and in transit; managed auth + enforced MFA; secrets in a manager; audit logging of access to customer documents; documented backups; an enterprise LLM tier with no-training terms. At scale: SOC 2 Type II (the founders have run SOC 2 II before — a genuine advantage; their buyers operate in a security-conscious supply chain and will ask "are YOU secure?"), incident-response runbook, key rotation, pen testing, sub-processor page. SOC 2 attainability is high because the data model is deliberately narrow. A breach of weakness-maps for defense suppliers is reputationally severe even though not CUI — specify incident response early.

BIGGEST SINGLE RISK
The single most dangerous issue is confidently-wrong answer generation in the hands of a non-technical owner with no in-house technical staff to sanity-check it. The product's value is auto-filling ~80%, but security-control questions are exactly where a fluent, plausible, WRONG answer is most dangerous: the owner has no CTO and limited ability to judge whether "Yes, we enforce FIPS-validated cryptography on CUI at rest" is actually true for their shop. If the tool drafts an over-confident affirmative and the owner forwards it to a prime — or worse, it feeds a self-assessment score they attest to in a federal system — the customer has made a false attestation with potential False Claims Act exposure, and the blame conversation points at the tool that produced the draft. The "drafts/estimates clearly labeled, customer is responsible" disclaimer is necessary but not sufficient: the engineering answer is a strict design where security-control answers default to abstain-and-interview unless grounded in the customer's own documented evidence, where affirmative control claims require the user to actively confirm the underlying fact (not just accept a pre-filled "Yes"), and where the riskiest attestation-grade questions are gated behind explicit human review and routed to the IT provider. This is a solvable product-and-prompt-engineering problem the prior build presumably touched — but it is THE thing that determines whether this tool is a trusted assistant or a liability generator, and it is harder here than at the founders' prior company precisely because the customer cannot backstop a bad answer.
