# LEGAL / REGULATORY REVIEW — Evidence-of-Insurability (EOI) Orchestration for Voluntary Benefits

*Standard disclaimer, stated once: I am not a licensed attorney and this is not formal legal advice. I am an experienced operator with strong regulatory pattern-recognition, flagging the issues the founders must brief real counsel on — not resolving them.*

## ONE-PARAGRAPH RISK READ
This is a B2B/B2B2C insurance-adjacent SaaS workflow sitting on top of a genuinely regulated activity: it touches individual health/medical-history information (EOI is, by definition, a medical-history attestation a carrier underwrites), it operates inside the life/disability insurance distribution chain, and it makes representations to employees about when their coverage is and is not in force. None of those facts is fatal, and most of the heavy-sounding regimes here are actually navigable because of a single structural design choice: the carrier is the underwriter and the data controller of record, and the platform is best built as a HIPAA Business Associate / service-provider/processor and a technology vendor — not as an insurance producer, not as a TPA making underwriting decisions, and not as the entity that "tells employees they are covered." The two real risks are (a) accidentally drifting into activities that trigger insurance-producer or third-party-administrator (TPA) licensing in many states, and (b) the disclosure/attestation trail being a double-edged sword — it is the platform's best liability shield if it is precise and carrier-approved, and its single biggest liability source if the platform ever originates a wrong statement about effective dates. The founders' actual HIPAA/HITECH and regulated-insurance-enterprise background makes this one of the more bridgeable regulated concepts I would expect to see, provided they retain insurance-regulatory counsel early to draw the producer/TPA line and keep the platform on the right side of it.

## RISK-GATE RATING: SERIOUS BUT MANAGEABLE
This earns SERIOUS BUT MANAGEABLE, not FATAL and not merely MINOR. It is more than MINOR because three real regimes apply with real consequences — HIPAA/health-data privacy on the medical-history data, state insurance-producer/TPA licensing on the activity, and direct liability for representations about coverage status — and because the wrong corporate/contractual structure could pull the company into licensing it does not want. It is not FATAL because every one of those has a named, well-trodden, bridgeable path: operate as a HIPAA Business Associate and CCPA/CPRA service-provider under signed BAAs/DPAs; structure deliberately as a technology/administrative-services vendor that routes and chases EOI rather than solicits insurance or adjudicates underwriting; and make the carrier — not the platform — the source of truth for what coverage is in force, with platform messaging limited to carrier-approved, status-accurate language. Per the rubric this rating is a due-diligence checklist, NOT a tier penalty; the forced, already-elected demand is the asset and is credited elsewhere.

## REGULATORY MAP
- **HIPAA (Privacy, Security, Breach Notification) — HHS OCR.** The likely posture is favorable: a fully-insured employer-sponsored group life/disability arrangement and the carrier's underwriting of EOI sit inside the HIPAA universe via the carrier (a covered entity/health plan). The defensible design is that the platform is a HIPAA **Business Associate** of the carrier (and/or the group health plan), handling PHI under a signed BAA. Burden: a HIPAA Security Rule program (access controls, encryption, audit logging, risk analysis, breach procedures) plus BAAs with every carrier and subcontractor — squarely within the founders' demonstrated HIPAA/HITECH experience. [ASSUMED medium-high confidence] EOI medical-history data is PHI in the carrier's hands and the platform is a BA.
- **State insurance data-security law — state DOIs.** The NAIC **Insurance Data Security Model Law** (Model #668, ~20+ states) and NY's **23 NYCRR 500** impose a written security program, third-party oversight, and breach notification on "licensees," flowing to vendors contractually. Burden: maps onto SOC 2 + HIPAA controls the founders already know.
- **State insurance producer licensing — state DOIs.** A producer license is required to **solicit, negotiate, or sell** insurance, or to be **compensated based on the sale/placement**. Pure administrative orchestration (ingesting elections already made, routing EOI, reminders, status) is administrative/technology work, not solicitation. BUT the proposed **per-approved-application success fee tied to in-force premium** is the single feature most likely to be re-characterized as compensation contingent on placement, implicating producer licensing or anti-rebating/fee-sharing rules. The item to brief counsel on first.
- **State Third-Party Administrator (TPA) licensing — state DOIs.** Many states license entities performing "administration" of insurance (collecting premiums, adjudicating claims, determining eligibility, handling enrollment/underwriting administration). Routing and chasing EOI and maintaining records *could* be read as administrative services in broad states; **making/transmitting underwriting decisions, determining eligibility, or handling money** would much more clearly trigger TPA registration. If the platform never adjudicates, never touches premium, and the carrier remains decision-maker, TPA exposure is reduced; in the broadest states, registration is paperwork + surety bonds + filings — manageable but real.
- **CCPA/CPRA and state privacy laws.** Health data is "sensitive personal information." The platform is most cleanly a **service provider/processor** under a DPA on documented instructions from the carrier/employer. Several newer laws (e.g., Washington's **My Health My Data Act**, with a private right of action) treat consumer health data expansively — worth a targeted look, though the employer-benefits/HIPAA context likely carries exemptions.
- **ADA and GINA (employment-law boundary) — EEOC.** The ADA restricts employer access to employee medical information; GINA prohibits acquiring/using genetic information (including family medical history, which EOI questionnaires sometimes ask). The protective design: medical-history data flows employee → platform → **carrier/underwriter**, and the platform shows the employer only **status** (pending/approved/denied), never the underlying health answers. Built that way, the platform actually *reduces* the employer's exposure. If it ever surfaces health answers or denial reasons to the employer, it manufactures ADA/GINA liability for its customer. A permissioning/architecture rule, not a license.
- **ERISA — DOL.** Voluntary/supplemental benefits are often structured outside ERISA (voluntary-plans safe harbor) or with employer/carrier as fiduciaries. The platform is a vendor, not a plan fiduciary, and should be contractually positioned as such.
- **TCPA / CAN-SPAM / state consumer-communication law — FCC/FTC/state AGs.** The product's engine is **automated reminders to employees** by email/SMS/voice. SMS is TCPA-sensitive (consent, time-of-day, opt-out; A2P 10DLC). Consent posture is defensible (employees elected the benefit) but must be engineered.
- **E-SIGN / UETA.** The EOI attestation is a signed medical statement with legal weight (misrepresentation can void coverage). The platform must capture legally valid e-signatures and tamper-evident records — solved technology, but the audit trail must be defensible at claim time.
- **Unauthorized practice / advice boundary.** The "plain-language guided questionnaire" must not advise employees on how to answer underwriting questions or how much coverage to buy (veers toward producer activity + misrepresentation/fraud exposure). A content/UX guardrail.

## LICENSING SUMMARY
The honest answer is "it depends on how the model is scoped, and that scoping decision is the single most consequential legal question in the concept." Three postures:
1. **Pure technology/administrative orchestration (target state).** The platform ingests already-made elections, routes EOI, sends reminders, surfaces status; the **carrier underwrites and decides**, the carrier/enrollment system communicates binding coverage facts, and the platform never solicits, determines eligibility, or touches premium. In this posture, **no producer license is required**, and TPA licensing is avoided or minimized — the company needs ordinary entity formation, a business license, and (critically) carrier-facing contracts and BAAs/DPAs. The reason this is not FATAL.
2. **TPA-style administration.** If the model includes adjudication support, eligibility determination, premium handling, or deep enrollment administration, several states require **TPA registration** (license, often a surety bond, annual reports). Moderate per-state paperwork; manageable, not preferred.
3. **Producer-style compensation.** The **success fee tied to in-force premium** is the feature most likely to require a resident/non-resident producer license (and to run into anti-rebating/fee-splitting rules). Bridgeable via NIPR non-resident reciprocity, a licensed principal, or a licensed channel partner — but it converts a clean SaaS into a partly-licensed insurance business across many states. The cleaner fix is to re-paper the success fee as an administrative/workflow-completion fee paid by the carrier for EOIs resolved (decoupled from premium amount) — whether that holds is a substance-over-form question for counsel.

No license collides with an unbridgeable founder gap. The target model needs none; where licensing is unavoidable, the founders' regulated-insurance background and standard scaffolding paths make it bridgeable. No FATAL credential here.

## LIABILITY POSTURE
The defining liability fact is that the platform inserts itself into the **communication of coverage status** to employees. That is simultaneously the value proposition, the liability shield, and the liability source. As a **shield**: a complete, timestamped, tamper-evident disclosure-and-attestation trail (what was elected, what is pending, that above-GI coverage is NOT in force until approved, the actual effective dates, the signed medical attestation) is the platform's best defense and a genuine product benefit — at claim time it proves the employee was accurately told their above-GI amount was pending/denied. As a **source**: if the platform ever **originates an inaccurate statement** (telling an employee they are covered when coverage is pending/denied, or showing stale "approved" status), it creates exactly the coverage-gap harm the concept exists to prevent, and a plaintiff (a denied beneficiary at claim time) will name the platform. Mitigation architecture: (1) the **carrier is the system of record** for in-force status, the platform mirrors carrier data; (2) messaging uses **carrier-approved language** with conservative defaults ("pending — not yet in force"); (3) **contractual allocation** — scope limits, carrier-as-underwriter disclaimers, mutual indemnities, liability caps; (4) **Tech E&O / Professional Liability + Cyber insurance**, both readily available and reasonably priced for this profile; (5) form the entity before any data flows. The residual, hardest-to-contract-away tail is the third-party-beneficiary/consumer-harm claim from a relying employee — which is why messaging accuracy and carrier-as-source-of-truth are non-negotiable and E&O is mandatory. Net: the liability is real, identifiable, and **insurable**, not catastrophic.

## TOP 3 LEGAL/REGULATORY STRENGTHS
- The structure naturally keeps the platform OUT of the regulated core: the carrier underwrites and decides, so the platform can be a HIPAA Business Associate and technology/administrative vendor rather than an insurer, producer, or underwriter — the single biggest reason this is navigable rather than fatal.
- The founders' real compliance-operator background (HIPAA/HITECH, SOC 2 Type II, GDPR/CCPA) and regulated-insurance experience map almost exactly onto the controls and contracts this requires — the hardest-sounding regime (health-data privacy) is their home turf.
- The disclosure/attestation audit trail, if built carrier-accurate, is a liability *shield* and a product feature at once, and the residual liability is squarely insurable (Tech E&O + Cyber) and contractually allocable.

## TOP 3 LEGAL/REGULATORY RISKS
- The success-fee-tied-to-in-force-premium could be re-characterized by a state DOI as producer/placement-contingent compensation (or run into anti-rebating/fee-splitting rules), dragging the company toward multi-state producer licensing — a model-design risk.
- Coverage-status representation liability: the moment the platform originates a wrong statement about effective dates, it creates the exact harm it exists to prevent and becomes a named defendant in a sympathetic claim-time dispute.
- Heavy, gating contractual dependency on the very incumbents it routes between: carrier and enrollment-platform data-exchange agreements (plus BAAs/DPAs) are required for the product to function — negotiated, slow, and giving counterparties leverage.

## BIGGEST SINGLE RISK
The biggest single risk is the interaction between the **per-approved-application success fee** and **state insurance-producer/anti-rebating law**, because it is the one issue that can quietly convert a clean, scalable B2B SaaS into a partially-licensed insurance business across dozens of states — changing the cost structure, go-to-market, and corporate form. Regulators assess substance over labels: a fee that rises with the in-force premium the platform helps "secure" looks, to a skeptical DOI examiner, like compensation contingent on the placement of insurance, the classic definition of producer compensation; some states' anti-rebating and fee-sharing statutes also restrict paying placement-contingent compensation to non-licensed entities. If that sticks, the company faces a choice between abandoning its most differentiated pricing lever or scaffolding producer licensing state-by-state — a real operational tax even though bridgeable. The likely fix is to re-paper the performance component as an **administrative/workflow-completion fee paid by the carrier for EOIs resolved** (decoupled from premium dollars) and keep the carrier as underwriter/decision-maker — but whether that holds is exactly the substance-over-form judgment only insurance-regulatory counsel can make, and it must be made before the pricing model is locked and sold.

## QUESTIONS FOR REAL COUNSEL BEFORE LAUNCH
1. **Producer / TPA line.** Given a model that ingests already-made elections, routes EOI, sends reminders, and surfaces carrier-decided status: in which states does this require a producer license or TPA registration? Does a per-approved-application success fee tied to in-force premium constitute producer/placement-contingent compensation or violate anti-rebating/fee-splitting rules, and can it be re-papered as an administrative/workflow fee that does not?
2. **HIPAA status and the BAA chain.** Confirm whether EOI medical-history data is PHI in the carrier's/plan's hands such that the platform is a HIPAA Business Associate, and define the BAA/DPA chain with carriers, employers/plans, ben-admin systems, and subcontractors — including for fully-insured voluntary arrangements that may sit partly outside ERISA.
3. **ADA/GINA exposure on the employer side.** Validate that routing employee health/family-history answers to the carrier while exposing only pending/approved/denied **status** (never the underlying answers or denial reasons) to the employer keeps the platform and its employer customers clear of ADA confidentiality and GINA restrictions.
4. **Coverage-representation liability and messaging.** What contractual scope-limits, carrier-as-underwriter disclaimers, indemnities, and liability caps, plus carrier-approved messaging standards and conservative defaults, are needed so the platform's status communications do not create third-party-beneficiary or negligent-misrepresentation liability at claim time?
5. **State data-security and privacy patchwork.** Which states' NAIC Insurance Data Security adoptions, NY DFS 23 NYCRR 500 flow-down, CCPA/CPRA service-provider terms, and consumer-health-data laws (e.g., WA My Health My Data) impose direct obligations, and how is breach/event notification (HIPAA + state DOI + state AG) sequenced?
6. **Messaging compliance (TCPA/SMS/CAN-SPAM).** What consent capture, opt-out handling, quiet-hours, and A2P 10DLC registration are required for automated employee reminders?
7. **E-signature/record admissibility.** Confirm the e-signature and audit-trail design satisfies E-SIGN/UETA and is admissible if relied upon to prove what an employee attested and was told.
