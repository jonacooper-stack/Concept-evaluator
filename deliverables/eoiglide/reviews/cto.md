# CTO REVIEW — Evidence-of-Insurability (EOI) Orchestration for Voluntary Benefits

## ONE-PARAGRAPH TECHNICAL READ
The application layer here is unremarkable, well-scoped, off-the-shelf SaaS — a guided questionnaire, a state machine that tracks each EOI case to resolution, a nudge/reminder engine, role-scoped dashboards, and an audit log. That core is a 6–10 dev-week build for one competent full-stack developer on a boring stack (Next.js + Postgres + a job runner + Stripe/transactional email). The entire technical risk of this business lives in exactly one place: the integration surface. To deliver the promised value you must (a) reliably learn which employees elected above-GI coverage — data that lives in ben-admin/enrollment platforms and partly in the EDI 834 feed, and is sometimes computed rather than transmitted — and (b) hand each case into, and pull decision status back out of, many carriers' underwriting/EOI intake, which ranges from "has a partner API" to "flat-file SFTP" to "a human-only web portal." This is not a research problem and not a science problem; it is a long, grinding, many-to-many integration-and-partnership problem against inconsistent and partly-closed endpoints, and it is the difference between a 6-week MVP and an 18-month slog. The health-data handling raises the security bar but is a well-trodden path. For two founders with one developer, the honest read is: the software is easy, the data plumbing is the whole game, and the speed-to-revenue depends almost entirely on whether you can launch on a thin, mostly-manual integration footprint (file feeds + carrier portal hand-off + human-assisted status entry) rather than waiting for clean APIs from every carrier.

## ARCHITECTURE SKETCH
- **Frontend:** A thin web app — employee-facing guided EOI questionnaire (plain-language, mobile-first, conditional branching), plus separate role-scoped dashboards for broker/enrollment firm, employer HR, and carrier ops. Next.js/React on Vercel. The questionnaire branching is the only mildly complex view.
- **Backend / core logic:** A case-management state machine — each EOI application is an entity with states (elected → EOI-required → in-progress → submitted-to-carrier → pending-underwriting → approved/denied/modified → in-force-confirmed). This is the actual product. Node/TypeScript or Python; Postgres as system of record.
- **Data store:** Postgres for case/state/audit data; an append-only audit/disclosure ledger (what was elected, what was disclosed, effective dates, what the employee was told) — must be immutable-by-design and a first-class table, not an afterthought. Object storage (S3) for uploaded documents/PDF attestations, encrypted.
- **Async / background work:** A reminder/nudge scheduler (the "chase-to-completion" engine) — cron/queue-driven email+SMS nudges, stall detection, escalation timers. Operationally important but technically simple (BullMQ/Sidekiq/Temporal). Temporal is worth considering precisely because EOI cases are long-running, multi-week workflows with timeouts and human steps.
- **Integrations (where ALL the complexity hides):**
  - *Inbound (elections that trigger EOI):* EDI 834 file ingestion over SFTP/AS2 (batch flat files), plus per-platform APIs/webhooks where they exist (Employee Navigator, Ease, bswift, PlanSource, Workday/ADP feeds [ASSUMED API availability varies widely]). You need an 834 parser and a normalization layer that maps inconsistent real-world feeds into your case model, including deriving the "above-GI / EOI-required" flag when it isn't explicitly transmitted.
  - *Outbound + status (carrier underwriting/EOI intake):* a per-carrier connector layer — API where a carrier offers one, file feed where it doesn't, and a clean hand-off into the carrier portal plus human-assisted status reconciliation where the carrier is closed. The MIB/EOI rails and carrier-proprietary EOI portals are the relevant endpoints. Expect a connector zoo, not a single API.
- **ML/AI:** None required, and that is good. No model is on the critical path. (Optional later: an LLM to translate dense medical-questionnaire language into plain English and to draft nudge copy — convenience, not dependency; never let an LLM make an underwriting or coverage-status determination.)
- **Auth / access:** Multi-tenant RBAC with strict tenant isolation; SSO/SAML for carrier and large-broker users; separation between who can see health-disclosure data (very few) and who can see case status (many). Auth0/WorkOS/Clerk off-the-shelf.
- **Hosting:** Single-region managed cloud (AWS/GCP); a HIPAA-eligible configuration (AWS with BAA, encrypted RDS/S3, VPC) because of the health data.

The real complexity is the integration normalization layer and the carrier-status round-trip. Everything else is commodity.

## BUILD PLAN TO REVENUE-EARNING MVP
Total for a revenue-earning MVP with ONE design-partner carrier or broker and a thin (file-feed + portal-handoff + human-assisted status) integration footprint: roughly **12–18 dev-weeks** of one developer's time. The gap between "demoable" (the app alone, ~6 weeks) and "earns revenue" (the app plus at least one real, working data round-trip) is where the weeks go.
1. **Core case engine + audit ledger (3–4 weeks).** The state machine, the immutable disclosure/attestation trail, the data model. The durable spine and the part most under your control. Do this first.
2. **Guided EOI questionnaire + nudge/escalation engine (3–4 weeks).** Conditional questionnaire, reminders over email/SMS (Resend/Postmark + Twilio), stall detection, role dashboards. Straightforward.
3. **Inbound election ingestion (3–5 weeks, high variance).** 834 parser + normalization + the EOI-required derivation logic, AND at least one ben-admin connection. The variance is real because 834s differ per sender and the above-GI flag may not be present.
4. **Outbound carrier hand-off + status reconciliation (3–6 weeks, highest variance, partly punt-able).** For MVP, the minimum: a clean structured hand-off into ONE design-partner carrier's intake and pull status back; where status can't be pulled programmatically, a human-assisted reconciliation screen. Do NOT try to integrate many carriers for v1.
5. **Tenant/auth/billing/security baseline (2–3 weeks).** RBAC, tenant isolation, encryption config, SSO, Stripe billing, audit logging on access to health data. Some overlaps Chunk 1.
**Punt to v2:** multi-carrier connector breadth (the single biggest time sink — add carriers one at a time as customers demand), advanced escalation, SOC 2 formal attestation, automated bidirectional API status for closed carriers (start human-assisted), and the LLM plain-language layer.

What two non-engineer-heavy founders can realistically ship: the app, questionnaire, nudge engine, audit ledger, and ONE end-to-end integration via file feed + portal hand-off + human-keyed status — yes, comfortably, in a quarter. What they should NOT promise day one: turnkey API connectivity to "many carriers and many enrollment systems" — that phrase is the multi-year version of this company, not the MVP. The right wedge is to land one carrier or one large broker, prove the premium-recapture, and add integrations transaction by transaction.

## CRITICAL ASSUMPTIONS
1. [ASSUMED] The "above-GI / EOI-required" trigger data is obtainable. The 834 may carry the election but not the GI threshold or the EOI-required flag — sometimes made by the ben-admin platform, sometimes the carrier. Range: derivable in maybe 40–70% of feeds; the rest needs a side feed or carrier rules. Cheap test: get 3–5 real de-identified 834 files plus the plan's GI rules from one friendly broker and try to compute "EOI required" by hand. If you can't, you have a data-source problem, not a coding problem.
2. [ASSUMED] At least one ben-admin platform and one carrier will give a small vendor data access without a year of BD. Large platforms (Workday/ADP) — slow/closed; mid-market (Employee Navigator, Ease, PlanSource) — more partner-friendly, weeks-to-months [ASSUMED]. Cheap test: email the partner/developer programs of Employee Navigator and Ease and ask, in writing, what access a third-party EOI vendor can get.
3. [ASSUMED] Carriers will accept a third party in the EOI workflow at all. EOI is underwriting; carriers are protective of the attestation and the decision. Some will welcome a completion-rate lift (it's their leaked premium too); some will insist EOI runs only in their rails. Cheap test: a 30-minute call with one group-life carrier's enrollment/underwriting ops lead.
4. [ASSUMED, partly verifiable] The health-disclosure data can be handled as health PII without an unbridgeable compliance blocker. A HIPAA-eligible cloud config with a BAA and strict access control is a known path — confirm your regulatory posture (Business Associate vs. under carrier's umbrella vs. standalone). Cheap test: one consult with a benefits-data privacy attorney plus reading the carrier's vendor-security requirements.
5. [ASSUMED] Status can be reconciled even when carriers are closed. The MVP leans on human-assisted status entry. Workable up to maybe low-hundreds of pending cases per cycle with one ops person; beyond that you need real status feeds. Cheap test: estimate pending-EOI volume for one mid-market employer and time how long one manual reconciliation takes.

## INFORMATION SECURITY POSTURE
Data collected: beyond ordinary PII (name, DOB, employer, identifiers in 834s), the EOI questionnaire collects self-reported medical history — health information. Even where not strictly HIPAA PHI in your hands, it must be treated as regulated health data: exactly the class that turns a breach into a reportable, reputation-ending, possibly carrier-contract-terminating event. There is no "we'll fix security later" version of this business. It lives in Postgres (case + audit) and S3 (documents), in a HIPAA-eligible cloud (AWS with BAA, encrypted RDS, encrypted S3, KMS, private VPC). Health-disclosure fields should be field-level encrypted and access-segregated from ordinary case-status data so broker/employer dashboards physically cannot read medical answers. Threat model: (1) broad-access dashboard users over-seeing health data — strict field-level RBAC + tenant isolation; (2) the inbound/outbound file pipeline (SFTP/AS2) as a leak surface — encrypted transport, per-partner credential isolation; (3) multi-tenant data bleed — row-level/tenant-scoped access in the data layer; (4) insider/ops access during human-assisted reconciliation — audit logging on every read of a health record. Launch controls: encryption at rest/in transit, signed BAA, RBAC with tenant isolation, field-level segregation, immutable audit logging, secrets manager, MFA. At scale: SOC 2 Type II (a sales gate the moment you sell to a carrier or enterprise broker), pen testing, key-rotation, IR runbook, data-retention/disposal policy. Budget 6–12 months + Vanta/Drata for SOC 2 as a growth milestone, not a launch blocker.

## SCORES (1–10)

2. Build vs. buy posture: **8** — Almost everything is off-the-shelf (auth, email, SMS, billing, workflow via Temporal, an 834 parser library); the only true custom wedge is the case-orchestration logic and per-carrier normalization.
3. Architecture cleanliness: **6** — The app is clean, but the many-to-many integration layer against inconsistent/partly-closed endpoints (834 variants + per-carrier connectors + human-assisted status) is inherently a connector zoo that grows messier with each carrier.
4. Time to revenue-earning MVP: **7** — A revenue-earning MVP with one design-partner integration is ~12–18 dev-weeks; well under a year to first dollar, but more than the <8-week ideal because the first real data round-trip is where the time goes.
5. Technical-assumption risk: **5** — No unproven capability, but it bets heavily on external data access (whether the EOI-trigger is derivable; whether ben-admins/carriers grant access) — unverified and partly outside the founders' control.
6. Third-party dependency risk: **5** — Success sits squarely on others' rails: ben-admin platforms and carriers who own the underwriting endpoint and could embrace, refuse, or close the loop themselves; many endpoints are closed portals with no API and no obligation to open.
7. Data architecture quality: **7** — The model is simple, owned, portable (Postgres + an append-only audit ledger you control), and the disclosure trail is a genuine asset; loses points because inbound data is messy and health-data sensitivity makes the store as much a liability as a moat.
8. Information security posture: **7** — The required posture is achievable from day one with named standard controls (HIPAA-eligible AWS + BAA, field-level encryption, RBAC, immutable audit logging, Vanta/Drata); good-but-not-exceptional because the bar is high (health PII), the consequence of error severe, and SOC 2 a real cost.
9. Scaling headroom: **7** — Compute/data scale is trivial (low-volume, long-running workflow data, not high-QPS); the real "scaling" constraint is integration breadth and human-assisted status volume, which scale with effort, not a rewrite.
10. Maintenance burden: **6** — The app runs itself, but a multi-carrier/multi-ben-admin integration estate is a permanent maintenance tax: feeds break, portals change, formats drift, each carrier added is ongoing surface — a real recurring oncall load, manageable while carrier count is small.

## TOP 3 TECHNICAL STRENGTHS
- The product core is genuinely simple and off-the-shelf: a case state machine, guided questionnaire, nudge engine, and audit ledger — buildable in ~6–10 dev-weeks on a proven stack with no AI/ML on the critical path.
- No bet-the-company novel technology: the value (chase-to-completion + auditable disclosure trail) can be delivered with a thin integration and a human in the loop initially, so the founders can earn revenue before the hard multi-carrier API breadth exists.
- Defensible data asset that you own: the append-only disclosure/attestation ledger and the cross-carrier case data live in your Postgres, are portable, and accrete into a record neither the carrier portal nor the ben-admin system currently owns — a real, ownable moat that compounds.

## TOP 3 TECHNICAL RISKS
- The integration surface is the entire business and it is many-to-many against inconsistent, partly-closed endpoints — 834 feeds that may not even carry the EOI-trigger flag, ben-admin platforms with uneven API access, and carrier EOI intake that is frequently a human-only portal with no status API.
- Heavy dependency on parties who own the underwriting endpoint and may not want a third party in it: a carrier can refuse to return status, insist EOI runs only in its own rails, or simply build the completion-chase itself once it sees the leaked premium it's recovering.
- Sensitive health-disclosure data sets a high, unforgiving security bar from day one (HIPAA-eligible infra, BAAs, field-level segregation, immutable audit logging, eventual SOC 2) — manageable, but a breach here is reputation- and contract-ending.

## BIGGEST SINGLE RISK
The single risk most likely to break this business is integration access and trigger-data availability at the two endpoints you don't control — and it is a business/partnership risk wearing a technical costume. The promised value requires reliably knowing which employees elected above-GI coverage and reliably handing each case to, and pulling status back from, the carrier's underwriting intake. Both ends are problematic. On the inbound side, the EDI 834 feed is the de facto standard but is implemented inconsistently and frequently does not carry the GI threshold or an explicit "EOI-required" flag — that determination is often computed inside the ben-admin platform or by the carrier — so you may not be able to derive your own trigger from the data you can get, forcing you into a deeper (and slower) partnership with whoever does own that determination. On the outbound side, EOI is underwriting, and carriers are protective of both the medical attestation and the decision; many run EOI only through their own portals, expose no status API, and have no obligation to open one to a small third party. If carriers decline to return status or insist the chase happen only in their rails, the product degrades from "automated cross-carrier orchestration" to "a nicer questionnaire plus a human keying status off carrier portals" — which can still earn some revenue but is a far smaller, more operationally heavy business. The deepest version is competitive: the leaked premium is the carrier's premium too, so a carrier that sees you proving the recapture has both the data access and the incentive to build the completion-chase internally. None of this is technically unbuildable — it is a grind of integrations and BD — but it determines whether this is a 12-week launch into a real wedge or an 18-month effort to pry open endpoints owned by counterparties who may prefer to keep them shut.

## QUESTIONS THE FOUNDERS MUST ANSWER
- Can you compute "EOI required / above-GI" from data you can actually obtain? Given a real de-identified 834 plus a plan's GI rules from one friendly broker, can you reconstruct which elections trigger EOI without depending on the ben-admin platform or carrier to tell you? If not, who owns that determination — your design partner or your competitor?
- Will at least one carrier let a third party run the EOI chase and return decision status — and on what terms? Have you had a concrete conversation with a group-life/disability carrier's underwriting/enrollment-ops lead?
- What does data access cost in time from the ben-admin platforms you'd target first (Employee Navigator, Ease, PlanSource) — API, sandbox, or only file feeds — and is that access available to a small vendor in weeks, or a multi-quarter BD effort?
- What is your regulatory data posture for the medical-history attestations (Business Associate under a BAA, under the carrier's umbrella, or standalone), and have you confirmed the path is bridgeable before ingesting a single real medical record?
- At what pending-case volume does human-assisted status reconciliation stop scaling, and which carriers must have a real status feed before you can serve a multi-thousand-employee employer profitably?

## RECOMMENDATION: REFINE
The technology is feasible and the app core is a clean, ownable, ~6–10-dev-week wedge with no research risk and no AI dependency — that part is genuinely good and I do not want to deflate it. But the concept as written ("sit between many carriers and many enrollment systems") describes the multi-year endgame, and the entire near-term risk concentrates in two endpoints the founders don't control: deriving the EOI trigger from obtainable feed data, and getting carriers to accept a third party in the underwriting hand-off and return status. Refine in three ways before committing build dollars. First, run the three cheap, one-to-two-week de-risking experiments above (reconstruct "EOI-required" from a real 834 + GI rules; one carrier conversation about third-party hand-off and status return; written API/feed-access terms from two mid-market ben-admin platforms). Second, narrow the MVP scope explicitly to ONE design-partner carrier or one large broker plus a thin file-feed-plus-portal-handoff-plus-human-status footprint, and stop promising turnkey many-carrier API connectivity for v1; add carriers one at a time. Third, design the health-data security in from day one and confirm the regulatory posture with one attorney consult before ingesting real medical data. Do those, and this becomes a buildable, defensible orchestration business; skip them, and you risk an 18-month integration slog against counterparties who may prefer the gap stay broken — or who close it themselves.
