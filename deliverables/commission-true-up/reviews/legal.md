# LEGAL / REGULATORY REVIEW — Carrier Commission Reconciliation & Recovery Service

*Standard disclaimer, stated once: I am not a licensed attorney and this is not formal legal advice. I am an experienced operator with strong regulatory pattern-recognition. The items below are issues the founders must brief real counsel on — I flag where the landmines live; I do not pretend to clear them.*

## ONE-PARAGRAPH RISK READ
This is a B2B financial-data/revenue-assurance product sold to insurance intermediaries, and the dominant legal question is narrow and well-defined: where does the line sit between "software that helps an agency audit and self-collect its own contractually-owed money" (lightly regulated, ordinary commercial law) versus "a third party acting as the agency's agent to demand and collect money from carriers" (which can drag in insurance-producer licensing analysis, third-party-administrator (TPA) statutes, debt-collection licensing, and — most acutely — the contingency-fee structure, the single sharpest edge). The good news is the customer is the principal/owner of the money being recovered, the agency submits its own recovery package, and the founders have genuine insurance-industry and compliance-operator backgrounds — so a clean, navigable "software + the agency acts as claimant" design is plainly achievable. The risk is real but it is a structuring problem, not a prohibition.

## RISK-GATE RATING: SERIOUS BUT MANAGEABLE
This earns SERIOUS BUT MANAGEABLE, not FATAL and not MINOR. Above MINOR because three regimes plausibly attach — producer/TPA licensing analysis, debt-collection law, and the contingency-fee "recovery agent" question — and because the data is non-public personal financial information governed by GLBA. Firmly short of FATAL because every one of those is bridgeable by design choice: position the product as the agency's own self-service/self-collection tool with the agency as the named claimant against its own carriers, keep the vendor out of the role of "demanding payment from the carrier on the agency's behalf," and the licensing triggers largely fall away. Per 01-objectives.md, SERIOUS BUT MANAGEABLE is a due-diligence checklist and does NOT lower the concept's tier. The contingency-fee structure is the one item that must be cleared with counsel before any revenue is taken.

## REGULATORY MAP
- **State insurance-producer licensing (50 state DOIs; NAIC model).** The relevant question is whether the VENDOR, by re-deriving and helping recover commissions, is "transacting insurance" or "sharing in commissions" in a way that requires a producer license. [ASSUMED — to be confirmed] As a software vendor charging a service fee for reconciliation, the vendor is not receiving a commission and not transacting insurance; the risk concentrates in the contingency-fee structure. Burden: low-to-moderate if structured as software/service. Cheap test: a single multi-state producer-licensing memo keyed to the 2–3 largest target states.
- **Contingency / "found money" recovery fee — the sharpest edge (state DOI + state debt-collection + money-transmission adjacency).** A percentage-of-recovery fee paid to a third party for collecting insurance money owed by a carrier is the structure most at risk of recharacterization — as (a) impermissible commission-sharing with an unlicensed entity, (b) acting as an unlicensed collection agency, or (c) acting as the agency's agent in the business of insurance. Mitigation: bill the contingency fee as a service fee for the audit work (not a share of the commission), have the AGENCY collect from the carrier into the agency's own account, then the vendor invoices the agency. Cheap test: structure-and-fee opinion letter scoped specifically to this question.
- **Third-Party Administrator (TPA) statutes (NAIC TPA model act).** Reconciling/recovering commissions for the AGENCY (producer side), not adjusting claims or administering benefits for the CARRIER, is generally outside the classic TPA definition, but definitions vary. Burden: low if the customer is clearly the producer/agency and the vendor never administers anything for a carrier.
- **Debt-collection licensing (state collection-agency acts; FDCPA largely inapplicable — commercial, not consumer, debt).** ~25–30 states license "collection agencies," and some definitions reach a third party that collects commercial debts owed to another. Avoidable if the agency, not the vendor, demands and receives payment from the carrier. Cheap test: a 10-state survey.
- **GLBA / Safeguards Rule + state privacy (FTC; NAIC Insurance Data Security Model Law ~25 states; CCPA/CPRA carve-outs).** Commission statements contain non-public personal information (policyholder names, policy numbers; sometimes producer SSNs/tax IDs). As a service provider you are under GLBA's Safeguards Rule and likely a "third-party service provider" under carrier data-security addenda. Moderate, and squarely in the founders' wheelhouse (SOC 2 / WISP). GLBA-regulated data is partially exempt from CCPA/CPRA, simplifying the overlay.
- **Carrier distribution contracts — anti-assignment, audit-rights, confidentiality, dispute clauses (private contract law).** The agency's right to audit, the carrier's stated comp terms, anti-assignment, mandatory-arbitration/forum clauses, limitations periods, and "statement is final unless disputed within N days" all live in the carrier-agency agreement. The practical battlefield — diligence per carrier, not licensing.
- **Unauthorized practice of law (UPL) — low but worth a guardrail.** Keep outputs factual reconciliation/documentation, not legal advice or representing the agency in a proceeding. Route genuine disputes to the agency's own counsel.
- **TCPA / CAN-SPAM (outbound marketing to agencies).** Standard B2B; low burden.

## LICENSING SUMMARY
For the **default, recommended structure — software-plus-service where the AGENCY is the named claimant and self-collects, and the vendor charges a service/subscription fee — I assess that NO insurance-producer license, NO TPA license, and NO collection-agency license is required of the vendor in most states** [ASSUMED ~70–85% confidence pending counsel; confirm in the 3–5 largest target states first]. The agency already holds whatever producer licenses it needs; the vendor sells software and back-office analysis. The licensing exposure concentrates in two design choices: (1) if the **contingency fee** is characterized as a share of the commission rather than a fee for services, a minority of states could treat the vendor as impermissibly sharing insurance commissions; and (2) if the vendor itself **contacts carriers to demand payment**, a subset of states' collection-agency acts could attach. Both are avoidable by structure, not by obtaining a license. No exam, no CE, no per-state producer-license stack under the recommended design. If the founders later want the vendor to act as an outsourced recovery agent that directly dunns carriers, THAT variant would likely require collection-agency licensing in some states and a fresh producer analysis — a separate, more-regulated product.

## LIABILITY POSTURE
Comparatively benign: recovering money already owed, not handling client funds, not giving investment/tax/legal advice, not touching consumers. Exposure vectors: (1) **errors-and-omissions** — the vendor computes a wrong shortfall, the agency disputes a carrier on bad data, the relationship sours, or a wrongful "you owe us" claim damages the agency's carrier standing; (2) **data breach** — exfiltration of policyholder NPI/producer data triggering GLBA/state breach-notice duties and carrier-contract indemnities; (3) **contractual indemnity flow-down** from carrier data-security addenda. None catastrophic or uninsurable. Tech-E&O / professional-liability plus cyber covers (1) and (2) at ordinary premiums [ASSUMED $5K–$20K/yr blended early; verify with a broker]. The vendor should NOT take custody of recovered funds — keep money flowing carrier→agency, vendor invoices agency — removing any money-transmission/fiduciary-custody tail entirely. Personal founder exposure is the standard pre-entity gap (form the entity before the first customer/statement). Contracts do the rest: a customer MSA with clear scope ("reconciliation and documentation; agency is solely responsible for what it submits to and disputes with carriers"), an E&O/limitation-of-liability cap tied to fees, a DPA, and disclaimers that the vendor does not represent the agency before carriers or provide legal/tax advice. The leftover residue is reputational/relationship risk if recoveries are wrong — managed by accuracy, conservative flagging, and a human-review step.

## TOP 3 LEGAL/REGULATORY STRENGTHS
- **The customer owns the money — the cleanest possible posture for a recovery product.** Because the agency is recovering its OWN contractually-owed commissions and is itself the licensed producer, the vendor can be designed as the agency's tool rather than its agent, keeping producer/TPA/collection licensing largely off the vendor.
- **No consumer, no fund custody, no field ops** — three of the most expensive liability/compliance categories are simply absent (high scores on dims 4, 5, 8).
- **Founder fit to the actual compliance work is excellent** — GLBA Safeguards, NAIC data-security, SOC 2, B2B MSAs plus insurance-industry deal experience are exactly the founders' demonstrated competencies.

## TOP 3 LEGAL/REGULATORY RISKS
- **Contingency-fee recharacterization.** A percentage-of-recovered-dollars fee paid to an unlicensed vendor is the single piece most exposed to being recast as impermissible commission-sharing or unlicensed collection/insurance activity — must be opinion-lettered and structured as a service fee before the first dollar.
- **The "who contacts the carrier" boundary.** If operations drift into the vendor directly demanding payment from carriers, it can trip state collection-agency acts and re-open the producer-licensing question; the self-collection boundary must be enforced in contract and workflow.
- **Carrier-contract terms can blunt the value and create dispute friction** — anti-assignment, short "statement is final" windows, mandatory arbitration, and confidentiality restrictions may limit recovery, and a wrongful claim on bad data could damage an agency's carrier standing (an E&O vector).

## BIGGEST SINGLE RISK
The contingency / "found money" fee is the one issue most likely to seriously slow this concept, and it must be resolved with insurance-regulatory counsel before any revenue is taken. The entire go-to-market hook — "you pay nothing out of pocket; we take a cut of what we recover" — is also the structure regulators are most prone to recharacterize. In a meaningful minority of states, a third party that receives a percentage of an insurance agency's commissions can be viewed as (a) sharing in insurance commissions while unlicensed, (b) operating as an unlicensed collection agency for commercial debt, or (c) acting as the agency's agent in the business of insurance — any of which converts a clean software business into one with a licensing problem in those jurisdictions. The fix is structural and well-understood: characterize and document the fee as a service fee for the audit/reconciliation work (priced by reference to results, but not framed or paid as a share of the commission itself); keep the carrier paying the AGENCY, with recovered funds landing in the agency's own account; and have the vendor invoice the agency afterward, so the vendor never receives money from a carrier and never holds the commission. Done that way, the contingency wedge survives in most states. Done casually — vendor takes its cut directly out of carrier remittances, or the contract literally assigns a slice of the commission — it is the thing most likely to draw a cease-and-desist or a producer-licensing inquiry. Gating only if ignored; with a written structuring opinion it is a solved problem, which is why the rating is SERIOUS BUT MANAGEABLE rather than FATAL.

## QUESTIONS FOR REAL COUNSEL BEFORE LAUNCH
1. **Fee characterization:** May an unlicensed software/service vendor charge a percentage-of-recovered-dollars fee for re-computing, documenting, and helping an insurance agency recover its OWN commission/override/bonus/renewal entitlement — and in which target states (start TX, FL, CA, NY, and one NAIC-model state) does that risk recharacterization as commission-sharing, unlicensed collection, or transacting insurance? What contract language and money-flow (carrier→agency→vendor invoice) makes it defensible?
2. **Licensing applicability (producer / TPA / collection):** Under the recommended self-collection design, does the vendor need any producer, TPA, or collection-agency license in any target state — and precisely what workflow boundary (vendor never contacts the carrier; agency is sole party that disputes/collects) must we hold?
3. **Carrier-contract constraints:** Across 3–5 carrier-agency agreements, what do the audit-rights, dispute-window/limitations, anti-assignment, mandatory-arbitration, and confidentiality clauses permit and forbid regarding re-deriving comp and submitting recovery claims — and does encoding/using a carrier's comp grid, or sharing statements with us, breach any confidentiality or assignment provision?
4. **Data and security obligations:** Given NPI on statements, what are our exact obligations under GLBA's Safeguards Rule, the NAIC Insurance Data Security Model Law in adopted states, and carrier data-security flow-down addenda — and what breach-notification and indemnity exposure do we inherit?
5. **Liability/contract package:** What MSA scope, limitation-of-liability cap, E&O disclaimer, DPA, and insurance stack (tech-E&O + cyber limits) cap our exposure for an incorrect computed shortfall, a wrongful chargeback dispute, or a data incident?
6. **UPL / agency boundary:** Where is the line between assembling a factual reconciliation/recovery package (permissible) and representing the agency in a dispute or giving legal advice (UPL), and what guardrails keep our deliverables on the safe side?

## RECOMMENDATION: GO
This is a structurally clean, navigable concept whose regulatory exposure is real but bounded, named, and bridgeable — and bridgeable specifically by founders with insurance-industry and SOC 2/HIPAA-grade compliance experience. The legal posture rests on one decisive design principle (the agency owns and self-collects its own contractually-owed money; the vendor is its software/service tool, not its collection agent), and on getting the contingency-fee mechanics opinion-lettered before taking revenue. With those handled — a multi-state structuring/licensing memo and a clean MSA/DPA/insurance package — there is no FATAL gate, no unbridgeable credential, and no uninsurable liability tail. I would advance this without hesitation, GO, with the explicit pre-launch condition that the contingency-fee structure and the producer/TPA/collection-licensing applicability are cleared by insurance-regulatory counsel in the first one to three target states before the first dollar of recovery fee is invoiced; that is a due-diligence checklist item, not a reason to refine or down-tier.
