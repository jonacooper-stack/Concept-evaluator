# SYNTHESIS — Carrier Commission Reconciliation & Recovery Service ("Commission True-Up")
**Date:** 2026-06-23

## ONE-PARAGRAPH PM READ
This is a genuinely strong *demand* concept attached to a genuinely contested *market*, and that tension is the whole story. The forcing function is as clean as they come — the agency's own revenue is the thing being mispaid, it recurs every cycle, it is already "budgeted" in the sense that it is money already owed, and the contingency "found-money" wedge means the buyer takes zero risk to say yes. The CFO, CMO, COO, and Legal all correctly see a self-funding, capital-light, vertical-SaaS-grade bootstrap with a forcing function most concepts would envy. But the web-enabled Competitive Analyst did the job the founders' charter demands and found that this exact category is one of the hottest, best-funded corners of insurtech right now — Comulate (~$25M raised, 8-figure revenue, marquee logos) and Fintary ($12.8M, already explicitly in life & annuity) are on-thesis pure-plays doing the core job, AgencyBloc Commissions+ already ships a 4.8/5-rated "Commission Not Received Report" inside thousands of the exact target agencies at $109/seat, and eNoah already runs the comp-grid recovery service as a BPO. That is a direct collision with hard constraint #5 (avoid well-funded, strong-tech incumbents) and it is the load-bearing fact of this evaluation. My call: the analyst's evidence is more decisive than the four GO votes' optimism, because competition is an 18-point rubric factor judged on exactly the kind of named, researched evidence the analyst supplied, and the GO votes were each scoring their own slice (money, message, ops, law) without weighing the funded field. This lands as a solid **B (Promising)** — a real niche play worth validating, not the clean-air A-tier the founders are hunting for.

# PLAIN-ENGLISH DELIVERABLES (for Mike)

## CONCEPT DOSSIER — Carrier Commission Reconciliation & Recovery ("Commission True-Up")
**Tier: B (Promising — worth a real test before we commit) | Objectives Score: 72.6/100 | 2026-06-23**

### 1. IN ONE PARAGRAPH
Insurance agencies that sell life insurance, annuities, and Medicare plans get paid by the carriers in commissions — first-year, yearly "renewal" commissions, bonuses, and overrides. That commission income is the agency's *entire* business. The problem: carriers pay through messy old computer systems and routinely get it wrong — skipping renewal payments on active policies, miscalculating bonuses, and sometimes "clawing back" money they shouldn't. Nobody re-checks the math against what the agency's contract actually says it's owed, so the agency quietly loses money every month. Our business is software-plus-service that re-does that math: we pull in the carrier statements, encode what the agency's contracts actually entitle them to, find the missing money, help them claim it back, and keep checking every month. We get in the door by doing the first historical audit for free and taking a cut of whatever we recover (so the agency risks nothing), then convert them to a monthly subscription for the ongoing checking.

### 2. WHO PAYS — AND WHY THEY CAN'T SAY NO
The buyer is the **owner or CFO of a small-to-midsize insurance agency** ($1M–$50M of commission income, placing life/annuity/Medicare across many carriers). The **forcing function** is the strongest kind: it's their **own revenue being underpaid** (an "operational/financial continuity + critical-input" must-have). What breaks if they don't buy? Nothing explodes — but they keep silently leaking 2–8% of income every month, forever. And because we lead with "we'll find it for free and you only pay us out of money we recover," there's no reason to say no. The competitors literally publish case studies of agencies finding $40,000 and $69,000 in missing money.

### 3. THE MONEY (PLAIN TERMS)
Two streams. **First**, the one-time "found money" audit: for a typical agency we'd find ~$100K–$190K of missing money in their last 12–18 months, collect maybe half to two-thirds, and keep ~25% — roughly **$27K–$45K per agency, one time** (this alone pays for landing the customer). **Second**, the real business: a **monthly subscription** (~$2,500/mo, ~$30K/year mid-size). The math: **about 40–70 agencies on subscription gets each of us to ~$500K/year by year two** — a tiny number in a market of thousands. (All estimates until we test them; the biggest one to nail is how much we can actually recover.)

### 4. WHY WE WIN
Our edge is the *way* we sell it and the *depth* of what we check. The sleepy, beatable players are **eNoah** (old-school outsourcing, weak brand) and **ComTrack** (smaller tool). Against them, our marketing skill and the zero-risk "found money" offer are a real advantage. We also do something most tools *don't*: check against what the **contract actually owes** (not just "did a payment look low?") and fight **wrongful clawbacks** — two things the big players under-build for this kind of agency.

### 5. THE COMPETITION (FROM THE ANALYST — read this carefully)
This is where the concept gets its B instead of an A. This is **not a sleepy market** — it's one of the hottest, best-funded corners of insurance software:
- **Comulate** — raised ~$25M, 8-figure revenue, big-name customers, already does our core job. The most dangerous. One gap: their customers are big property-and-casualty brokerages, *not* the small life/annuity/Medicare agencies we'd target.
- **Fintary** — raised ~$13M and **already serves life & annuity agencies** specifically. The closest match to our exact customer, with fresh money.
- **AgencyBloc Commissions+** — software thousands of our target agencies *already use and pay for* ($109/seat), rated 4.8/5, and **already markets a "find your missing commissions" report.** We'd wedge between a funded competitor above and a tool the customer already owns below.
- **eNoah** and **ComTrack** — the beatable ones, but they prove the service already exists.

Bottom line: the *demand* is fantastic, but we'd enter a **funded race**, not an empty field — and our charter specifically says avoid well-funded strong-tech competitors. We can still win a *narrow corner* (small life/annuity/Medicare agencies the big P&C-focused players ignore), but a funded player could copy our angle in 1–2 quarters if we prove it works.

### 6. THE BIGGEST RISKS (TOP 3, PLAIN)
1. **The funded players reach down into our niche before we lock it up.** Our only defense is speed, a sharper niche, and a proprietary library of carrier contracts they'd have to rebuild.
2. **There might be less recoverable money than we think.** "2–8%" is an unverified estimate; if it's more like 1%, the free-audit hook stops paying for itself and the subscription gets hard to justify.
3. **The hard part isn't the software — it's getting the data.** Two unknowns could turn this into a labor-heavy grind: can we encode each agency's contract terms quickly, and can we reliably match the carrier's payment lines to the agency's policies?

### 7. WHAT WE'D TEST NEXT (cheap, before building anything)
1. **Free historical audit on 3–5 real agencies** — answers how much is recoverable AND how hard the data is, and even makes money.
2. **Sit with those agencies and have them show their current tool's output** line by line — proves (or kills) our "we catch what others don't" claim.
3. **Get a lawyer's opinion on the "take a cut" fee** in a couple of states before charging a dollar.
4. **Test whether agencies keep paying monthly** after we've found their back-money.
5. **Prove the niche is too small/annoying for the big funded players** to bother with for a couple of years — or admit our only moat is "we got there first."

### 8. THE SCORECARD (plain)
- **Must-have (8/10):** the agency's own revenue is underpaid; nobody needs convincing.
- **Competition (4.5/10):** the one thing holding this back — a funded, heating race, not a sleepy field.
- **Path to our income (8/10):** ~40–70 subscribed agencies → ~$500K/year each by year 2.
- **Recurring revenue (7/10):** sticky once installed, but whether they keep paying after the windfall is unproven.
- **Demand evidence (8/10):** competitors publish real recovered-dollar case studies.
- **Capital efficiency (9/10):** cheap to start; the first audit pays for landing the customer.
- **Operations (7.5/10):** fully remote, no field work; the hard part is the upfront onboarding.
- **Founder fit (8/10):** marketing, positioning, software, insurance/compliance background.
- **Scalability (8/10):** lifestyle-capable or acquirable.
- **Legal risk gate: SERIOUS BUT MANAGEABLE** — real but navigable; the "take a cut" fee needs a lawyer's sign-off first. Does **not** lower the tier.
- **What moves it to A:** prove with 3–5 real agencies that (a) recoverable money is genuinely there, (b) our contract-level audit catches dollars existing tools miss, and (c) the small life/annuity/Medicare niche is one the funded players leave alone long enough for us to own it.

## APPENDIX: CFO REVIEW

# CFO REVIEW — Carrier Commission Reconciliation & Recovery Service

## ONE-PARAGRAPH FINANCIAL READ
The numbers pencil well, and unusually so for a bootstrap: this is a software-leveraged business sold on found money, where the entry offer pays for itself out of recovered dollars and the durable revenue is a sticky, data-moated subscription on top. The economics rhyme closely with proven adjacent categories — contingency/SaaS revenue-recovery (medical/insurance underpayment recovery, retail freight-audit, telecom expense management, SaaS-spend reconciliation) where vendors routinely take 15–35% of recovered dollars and then convert to recurring monitoring. The two real financial questions are not "do the unit economics work" (they clearly can) but (1) is the leakage rate big and real enough at the small-agency tier to fund attractive contingency checks, and (2) does the recurring subscription hold its price once the historical "windfall" is harvested and the monthly catch shrinks to steady-state. Both are testable cheaply before a line of code is written.

## THE MATH

**Target customer profile and the leakage pool:**
- Target agency: $1M–$50M annual commission revenue. Representative early target at ~$5M annual commission revenue [ASSUMED midpoint; real distribution heavily weighted to $1M–$10M — cheap test: pull state-level counts of licensed agencies and a sample of BGA/IMO public profiles].
- Silent leakage estimate: 2%–8% of commission revenue per cycle [ESTIMATE — to be validated]. I will NOT take the 8% top end. I use a conservative 1.5%–4% recoverable-and-collectible band, because some leakage is real but uncollectible (genuine lapses, contractual ambiguity, statute-barred), and recovery success is never 100%.

**LAYER 1 — The contingency wedge (initial historical audit)**
- Lookback: 12–24 months before records/relationships go cold.
- Recoverable pool on a $5M/yr agency at 2.5% recoverable leakage over an 18-month lookback ≈ $5M × 1.5 yr × 2.5% = ~$187K identified shortfall.
- Realized recovery (carriers contest, some fail): 50%–70% lands ⇒ ~$94K–$131K collected. Call it ~$110K [ASSUMED].
- Contingency take-rate: revenue-recovery norms 15%–35%. Use 25% [ASSUMED, anchored to named comparables] ⇒ ~$27K one-time contingency revenue per onboarded $5M agency.
- Lumpy, one-time "harvest" revenue. NOT the business — it is the customer-acquisition mechanism that funds itself.

**LAYER 2 — The recurring subscription (the durable business)**
- Steady-state ongoing leakage is smaller than the historical backlog but recurs every cycle. On a $5M agency, ongoing catch might be 1%–2.5% of commissions = $50K–$125K/yr identified.
- Subscription pricing by size/volume, anchored to value: protecting $50K–$125K/yr of otherwise-lost income justifies $1.5K–$4K/month = $18K–$48K/yr [ASSUMED — value-based, 15–40% of dollars protected]. Use ~$2.5K/mo = $30K/yr ARPU as the planning midpoint for a $5M agency, scaling to ~$6K–$12K/yr for $1M agencies and ~$60K–$120K/yr for $20M+.

**Gross margin:**
- Cost to serve = software (ingestion/normalization/recompute) + light human review per cycle. Build-once, run-many. Per-customer marginal cost at steady state ~$3K–$8K/yr at ~$30K ARPU ⇒ gross margin ~73%–90%. Model 78%–85% blended [ASSUMED — comparable to ops-heavy vertical SaaS like freight-audit/TEM at 65–80%, not pure 90% SaaS]. **PATH TO THE FOUNDER-INCOME LADDER (recurring is the spine; contingency is fuel)**
Target: ~$300K/founder by mo12 (~$600K combined), ~$500K+/founder by mo24 (~$1M+ combined).
- Recurring ARPU ~$30K/yr, ~80% GM: ~$600K of gross profit ≈ ~$600K / (30K × 0.80) ≈ **25 recurring agencies**. Plus contingency: 25 onboardings × ~$27K ≈ $675K of additional (lumpy) first-year cash. So in year one the contingency harvest alone can fund or exceed the mo12 rung while you build the recurring base.
- mo24 at ~$1M+ combined GP on recurring alone: ~$1M / (30K × 0.80) ≈ **~42 recurring agencies**, i.e., roughly 40–50 agencies under subscription. A very small absolute customer count for a category with thousands of BGAs/IMOs/FMOs.
- Pessimistic case (~1% recoverable): contingency checks ~halve (~$13K/onboarding) and subscription compresses to ~$12K–$18K/yr → mo24 needs ~70–110 recurring agencies — still small share; CAC efficiency and onboarding throughput become the binding constraint, not demand. The model survives the pessimistic case; it just needs more logos.

**CAC / LTV:**
- CAC: founder-led, list-buildable segment (state DOI licensing data, NAILBA/IMO rosters, LinkedIn). The contingency offer ("we audit your last 18 months free, you pay only from money we recover") collapses sales friction to near zero. Blended CAC $2K–$8K/agency [ASSUMED]. The first audit (~$13K–$27K) typically EXCEEDS CAC → customer acquisition is cash-positive on day one before any subscription. Rare and excellent.
- LTV: $30K ARPU, 80% margin, assumed 85%–92% annual gross retention (sticky — wired into their statements, protects their own revenue) → gross-profit LTV ≈ $200K–$300K per recurring agency [ASSUMED]. LTV/CAC comfortably > 10 even after haircuts; payback immediate (contingency) to <6 months (subscription). The strongest single quantitative fact in the file.

**Bottom line:** at conservative-to-midrange assumptions the recurring business reaches the mo24 rung at ~40–70 agencies, acquisition is self-funding (often cash-positive), and gross margins are vertical-SaaS-grade. The open questions are the size/collectibility of the leakage pool and the durability of recurring price after the backlog is harvested.

## APPENDIX: COO REVIEW

# COO REVIEW — Carrier Commission Reconciliation & Recovery Service

## ONE-PARAGRAPH OPERATIONS READ
This is a remote-first, software-plus-light-services operation with no field component whatsoever — no trucks, no installs, no on-site visits — which puts it squarely inside what two versatile, software-capable founders can actually run. The repeated unit of work is data: ingest carrier commission statements, encode each agency's comp grid once, re-compute expected pay, reconcile against actual, and assemble a dispute package. The genuine operational hazard is not delivery scale — it's the *onboarding cliff*: building a reliable parser/normalizer across dozens of incompatible carrier statement formats and accurately encoding each agency's contractual comp terms is real, judgment-heavy human labor that does not self-serve at the start, and it is front-loaded onto exactly the two people who also have to sell and build. The business is operable by two founders plus 2–3 hires, but only if the founders ruthlessly narrow the initial carrier set, treat the comp-grid build as a structured intake (not bespoke consulting), and let recurring-cycle reconciliation — which IS highly automatable once the data pipeline exists — become the scalable spine.

## THE TUESDAY-IN-MARCH WALKTHROUGH
It is a Tuesday in March, ~12-month mark. The business has ~18 agencies live on recurring reconciliation [ASSUMED] plus 2 historical-audit onboardings in flight. Founder A (operations/product) starts with the cycle queue: 11 of the 18 agencies receive carrier statements monthly, and a batch of February-cycle statements landed over the weekend — Lincoln Financial, Nationwide, a couple of aggregator feeds, two BGA carrier-portal CSV exports, and three PDFs from smaller annuity carriers. The ingestion pipeline auto-parsed ~80% of the lines cleanly. The other 20% kicked out to an exceptions queue: a carrier silently changed its PDF column layout in February, a renewal block came through with policy numbers that don't match the agency's book-of-record IDs, and one override statement is missing the bonus-tier breakout. Founder A spends ~90 minutes triaging exceptions, mapping the changed PDF template (a one-time fix that auto-applies going forward), and flagging the ID-mismatch policies for the agency to confirm.

By mid-morning, the reconciliation engine produced this cycle's variance reports for the 11 agencies. Most are clean. But three are interesting: one shows ~$14K of renewal commissions that stopped paying on a block of policies still in-force (a classic carrier "trail just quietly fell off the statement"), another shows a wrongful chargeback of ~$6K on a policy the agency can prove didn't lapse, and a third shows an override mis-tiered one band low because the carrier didn't credit a production threshold the agency crossed. The Recovery Specialist (first hire, ~$60–80K, started month 7) assembles the documented recovery packages — the contractual comp-grid entitlement, policy-level evidence, the specific statement lines — into the format each carrier's commission-accounting team will accept, and submits through the carrier's broker-comp channel. She also chases two recovery claims from last cycle; carrier commission departments are slow, so follow-through cadence is the actual work — persistence, not genius.

Meanwhile Founder B (GTM/build) is half on a new-agency onboarding and half on sales. The onboarding agency placed business across 22 carriers; the team prioritized the top 9 carriers (~85% of the agency's commission dollars) and is encoding those comp grids first — reading the actual carrier-agency agreements, override schedules, bonus addenda, and entering terms. This is the labor-intensive step: ~15–25 hours of structured intake per agency [ASSUMED], part judgment and part data entry that a trained Onboarding Analyst (second hire, ~$50–65K) handles under review. Founder B fields two support pings, runs one sales demo with a $12M-commission BGA, and reviews the analyst's comp-grid entries before they go live (a wrong comp grid produces wrong variances and erodes trust fast).

Where it breaks on a bad Tuesday: a carrier reformats its statement and the parser silently mis-maps a column, so a batch of "underpayments" are actually parsing artifacts — if that reaches an agency as a recovery claim, the agency submits garbage to its carrier and the vendor's credibility takes the hit. That is why the exceptions queue and a human QC gate on first-of-cycle outputs per carrier are non-negotiable. The second break point is recovery-claim limbo: a carrier slow-walks a claim, the contingency fee doesn't convert, and the founders eat the onboarding labor with no payday on that line. Neither is fatal, but both are why this is "software-plus-service," not pure SaaS, in year one.

## CAPACITY MATH
**Target.** mo24 binding test: ~$1.0–1.2M of founder income, which on a lean services-light SaaS at ~45–55% owner margin implies roughly **$2.2–2.6M annual revenue** by mo24. Two streams: (A) one-time contingency fees on historical audits, (B) recurring subscription.

**Unit inputs [ASSUMED — testable, ranged]:** target agency ~$6M commission revenue; conservative 2–4% of one year's commissions recovered = $120K–$240K found, say ~$150K recovered; contingency 25–30% = ~$40–45K one-time per agency; recurring ~$1,500–$4,000/mo, model ~$2,500/mo = $30K/yr.

**How many agencies to hit ~$2.4M by mo24:** ~50–70 recurring agencies = $1.5–2.1M recurring ARR, plus $1.5–2.5M of historical-audit contingency across the ramp. Blended, **~55–65 live agencies** clears ~$2.2–2.6M. Even halving the recurring price, ~90–110 agencies gets there — still a *small-account-count* business. Two-founders-plus-a-handful tractable.

**Recurring-cycle delivery load (scalable spine):** 60 live agencies, ~70% monthly → ~52 cycle-events/month ≈ 12/week. ## APPENDIX: LEGAL / REGULATORY REVIEW

# LEGAL / REGULATORY REVIEW — Carrier Commission Reconciliation & Recovery Service

*Standard disclaimer, stated once: I am not a licensed attorney and this is not formal legal advice. I am an experienced operator with strong regulatory pattern-recognition. The items below are issues the founders must brief real counsel on — I flag where the landmines live; I do not pretend to clear them.*

## ONE-PARAGRAPH RISK READ
This is a B2B financial-data/revenue-assurance product sold to insurance intermediaries, and the dominant legal question is narrow and well-defined: where does the line sit between "software that helps an agency audit and self-collect its own contractually-owed money" (lightly regulated, ordinary commercial law) versus "a third party acting as the agency's agent to demand and collect money from carriers" (which can drag in insurance-producer licensing analysis, third-party-administrator (TPA) statutes, debt-collection licensing, and — most acutely — the contingency-fee structure, the single sharpest edge). The good news is the customer is the principal/owner of the money being recovered, the agency submits its own recovery package, and the founders have genuine insurance-industry and compliance-operator backgrounds — so a clean, navigable "software + the agency acts as claimant" design is plainly achievable. The risk is real but it is a structuring problem, not a prohibition.

## RISK-GATE RATING: SERIOUS BUT MANAGEABLE
This earns SERIOUS BUT MANAGEABLE, not FATAL and not MINOR. Above MINOR because three regimes plausibly attach — producer/TPA licensing analysis, debt-collection law, and the contingency-fee "recovery agent" question — and because the data is non-public personal financial information governed by GLBA. Firmly short of FATAL because every one of those is bridgeable by design choice: position the product as the agency's own self-service/self-collection tool with the agency as the named claimant against its own carriers, keep the vendor out of the role of "demanding payment from the carrier on the agency's behalf," and the licensing triggers largely fall away. Per 01-objectives.md, SERIOUS BUT MANAGEABLE is a due-diligence checklist and does NOT lower the concept's tier. The contingency-fee structure is the one item that must be cleared with counsel before any revenue is taken.

## REGULATORY MAP
- **State insurance-producer licensing (50 state DOIs; NAIC model).** The relevant question is whether the VENDOR, by re-deriving and helping recover commissions, is "transacting insurance" or "sharing in commissions" in a way that requires a producer license. [ASSUMED — to be confirmed] As a software vendor charging a service fee for reconciliation, the vendor is not receiving a commission and not transacting insurance; the risk concentrates in the contingency-fee structure. Burden: low-to-moderate if structured as software/service. Cheap test: a single multi-state producer-licensing memo keyed to the 2–3 largest target states.
- **Contingency / "found money" recovery fee — the sharpest edge (state DOI + state debt-collection + money-transmission adjacency).** A percentage-of-recovery fee paid to a third party for collecting insurance money owed by a carrier is the structure most at risk of recharacterization — as (a) impermissible commission-sharing with an unlicensed entity, (b) acting as an unlicensed collection agency, or (c) acting as the agency's agent in the business of insurance. Mitigation: bill the contingency fee as a service fee for the audit work (not a share of the commission), have the AGENCY collect from the carrier into the agency's own account, then the vendor invoices the agency. Cheap test: structure-and-fee opinion letter scoped specifically to this question.
- **Third-Party Administrator (TPA) statutes (NAIC TPA model act).** Reconciling/recovering commissions for the AGENCY (producer side), not adjusting claims or administering benefits for the CARRIER, is generally outside the classic TPA definition, but definitions vary. Burden: low if the customer is clearly the producer/agency and the vendor never administers anything for a carrier.
- **Debt-collection licensing (state collection-agency acts; FDCPA largely inapplicable — commercial, not consumer, debt).** ~25–30 states license "collection agencies," and some definitions reach a third party that collects commercial debts owed to another. Avoidable if the agency, not the vendor, demands and receives payment from the carrier. Cheap test: a 10-state survey.
- **GLBA / Safeguards Rule + state privacy (FTC; NAIC Insurance Data Security Model Law ~25 states; CCPA/CPRA carve-outs).** Commission statements contain non-public personal information (policyholder names, policy numbers; sometimes producer SSNs/tax IDs). As a service provider you are under GLBA's Safeguards Rule and likely a "third-party service provider" under carrier data-security addenda. Moderate, and squarely in the founders' wheelhouse (SOC 2 / WISP). GLBA-regulated data is partially exempt from CCPA/CPRA, simplifying the overlay.
- **Carrier distribution contracts — anti-assignment, audit-rights, confidentiality, dispute clauses (private contract law).** The agency's right to audit, the carrier's stated comp terms, anti-assignment, mandatory-arbitration/forum clauses, limitations periods, and "statement is final unless disputed within N days" all live in the carrier-agency agreement. The practical battlefield — diligence per carrier, not licensing.
- **Unauthorized practice of law (UPL) — low but worth a guardrail.** Keep outputs factual reconciliation/documentation, not legal advice or representing the agency in a proceeding. Route genuine disputes to the agency's own counsel.
- **TCPA / CAN-SPAM (outbound marketing to agencies).** Standard B2B; low burden.

## LICENSING SUMMARY
For the **default, recommended structure — software-plus-service where the AGENCY is the named claimant and self-collects, and the vendor charges a service/subscription fee — I assess that NO insurance-producer license, NO TPA license, and NO collection-agency license is required of the vendor in most states** [ASSUMED ~70–85% confidence pending counsel; confirm in the 3–5 largest target states first]. The agency already holds whatever producer licenses it needs; the vendor sells software and back-office analysis. The licensing exposure concentrates in two design choices: (1) if the **contingency fee** is characterized as a share of the commission rather than a fee for services, a minority of states could treat the vendor as impermissibly sharing insurance commissions; and (2) if the vendor itself **contacts carriers to demand payment**, a subset of states' collection-agency acts could attach. Both are avoidable by structure, not by obtaining a license. No exam, no CE, no per-state producer-license stack under the recommended design. If the founders later want the vendor to act as an outsourced recovery agent that directly dunns carriers, THAT variant would likely require collection-agency licensing in some states and a fresh producer analysis — a separate, more-regulated product.

## LIABILITY POSTURE
Comparatively benign: recovering money already owed, not handling client funds, not giving investment/tax/legal advice, not touching consumers. Exposure vectors: (1) **errors-and-omissions** — the vendor computes a wrong shortfall, the agency disputes a carrier on bad data, the relationship sours, or a wrongful "you owe us" claim damages the agency's carrier standing; (2) **data breach** — exfiltration of policyholder NPI/producer data triggering GLBA/state breach-notice duties and carrier-contract indemnities; (3) **contractual indemnity flow-down** from carrier data-security addenda. None catastrophic or uninsurable. Tech-E&O / professional-liability plus cyber covers (1) and (2) at ordinary premiums [ASSUMED $5K–$20K/yr blended early; verify with a broker]. The vendor should NOT take custody of recovered funds — keep money flowing carrier→agency, vendor invoices agency — removing any money-transmission/fiduciary-custody tail entirely. Personal founder exposure is the standard pre-entity gap (form the entity before the first customer/statement). Contracts do the rest: a customer MSA with clear scope ("reconciliation and documentation; agency is solely responsible for what it submits to and disputes with carriers"), an E&O/limitation-of-liability cap tied to fees, a DPA, and disclaimers that the vendor does not represent the agency before carriers or provide legal/tax advice. ## APPENDIX: CTO REVIEW

# CTO REVIEW — Carrier Commission Reconciliation & Recovery Service

## ONE-PARAGRAPH TECHNICAL READ
This is a financially deterministic problem wrapped in a genuinely hard data-ingestion problem. The reconciliation math (re-derive expected comp from a grid, diff it against what was paid, line by line) is boring, provable, off-the-shelf arithmetic — that part is not where the risk lives. The risk lives almost entirely in the front door: ingesting and normalizing hundreds of incompatible carrier commission statements (PDFs, spreadsheets, EDI/flat files, portal scrapes) into one clean ledger, and in entity-resolving each paid line to the agency's book of policies so "expected vs. paid" is comparable at all. This is buildable by a two-founder team with one strong developer — but it is a per-carrier extraction grind that is service-heavy at the start, not a clean SaaS you flip on; the honest plan is to ship a narrow, semi-automated, human-in-the-loop MVP against a handful of carriers and a few design-partner agencies, earn contingency revenue immediately, and let coverage compound. The biggest non-obvious bomb is data acquisition: the agency's *contractual* comp terms (the grid) and a clean policy book are the two inputs the whole engine depends on, and both are messy, partly tribal knowledge, and not sitting in a queryable system.

## ARCHITECTURE SKETCH
- **Frontend:** a thin web portal for the agency principal/finance team — upload statements, view a normalized ledger, review flagged discrepancies, approve/edit, download recovery packages. Next.js + React on Vercel, or Retool/Refine for the internal-ops console early. Tables, review queues, PDF/Excel exports — no exotic UI.
- **Backend:** a standard Python service (FastAPI) — Python because the document-extraction and data-wrangling ecosystem (pandas, openpyxl, pdfplumber/camelot, regex/template parsers) is richest there. Postgres as the system of record (a normalized commission ledger, a policy book, a comp-grid store, a discrepancy/case table). Object storage (S3) for raw statements and generated recovery packages. A job queue (Celery/RQ or managed equivalent) for async ingestion and recompute runs each cycle.
- **The ingestion layer is the real system, and it is a per-source adapter farm, not one parser:** (a) spreadsheets/CSV/EDI flat files — deterministic, column-mapped parsers, high reliability; (b) PDF statements — table extraction (pdfplumber/Camelot/AWS Textract or Azure Document Intelligence) plus per-carrier templates, with an LLM as a *fallback structuring/field-mapping assistant* over already-extracted text, never as the source of truth for dollar amounts; (c) carrier-portal downloads — ideally human-pulled files dropped into the portal at the start, NOT brittle Playwright scrapers behind MFA logins (scraping carrier portals is a ToS and reliability minefield and should be explicitly out of scope for the MVP).
- **Comp-grid engine:** a data-modeled rules store (first-year %, renewal/trail schedule by policy year, override tiers, bonus tiers, keyed by carrier × product × contract). Configuration data plus a deterministic evaluator, not ML. Versioned and effective-dated, because comp schedules change and you must recompute against the terms in force at the transaction date.
- **Reconciliation/matching engine:** deterministic. The subtle part is entity resolution — joining each carrier-paid line to the right policy and the right expected-comp rule when carriers use inconsistent policy numbers, producer IDs, product names, and dates. Fuzzy-match + rules + a human review queue for low-confidence joins; not a research problem, but where the engineering hours actually go.
- **ML/AI:** deliberately on the periphery — LLM/OCR assists *extraction and field mapping over text*; all money math is deterministic and reproducible. The architecture must be auditable: every flagged dollar traces to "this paid line, this expected rule, this delta," because the output is a dispute you hand a carrier.
- **Auth/hosting:** managed auth (Auth0/Clerk/Cognito) with SSO-readiness, single managed cloud (AWS or GCP), encryption in transit and at rest from day one. Where the real complexity lives: ingestion coverage + matching accuracy + acquiring the comp grid. Everything downstream is conventional.

## BUILD PLAN TO REVENUE-EARNING MVP
Estimate: roughly **10–16 weeks of one strong developer's time** to a *contingency-revenue-earning* MVP against a narrow carrier set and 1–3 design-partner agencies, with the second founder running onboarding/comp-grid capture and recovery submission by hand. This MVP *earns* (you can run a real historical audit and submit real recoveries) — not a full self-serve SaaS, which is quarters away.
1. **Ledger + comp-grid data model and the deterministic reconciliation/recompute engine (~2–3 wks).** The spine and the easy, provable part — build it first so the math is trustworthy before you fight the documents.
2. **Spreadsheet/CSV/EDI ingestion for the first handful of carriers your design partners actually use, plus the policy-book import (~2–3 wks).** Start with structured formats; they cover real dollars immediately and de-risk the thesis fastest.
3. **PDF table extraction + per-carrier templates for the top 3–6 statement formats your design partners actually receive (~3–4 wks, and this never fully "ends").** Human-in-the-loop review queue so a low-confidence extraction is corrected, not silently wrong.
4. **Discrepancy detection + recovery-package generator + the review/approval UI (~2–3 wks).** This converts found money into a claim and therefore into your contingency fee.
5. **Recurring-monitoring loop, multi-tenant hardening, audit logging, onboarding tooling (~2–3 wks)** — partly punt-able to v2; the contingency historical audit can be delivered semi-manually before the recurring layer is polished.
**Punt to v2:** portal scraping/automation, broad carrier coverage, self-serve onboarding, downline/producer-payment features, SOC2 formalization. Reality check for two non-engineer-heavy founders: one capable dev can build this spine; the *coverage* and *comp-grid capture* are ongoing labor the non-dev founder absorbs as a service motion early — the right bootstrap shape, but "automation %" is low at launch and improves per carrier over time.

## CRITICAL ASSUMPTIONS
1. **The comp grid is obtainable and machineable.** The entire engine assumes the agency's contractual comp terms can be captured into structured rules. In practice these live in carrier contracts, addenda, and the principal's head, and can be ambiguous. If grids can't be reliably encoded, "expected" is undefined. Cheap test: take 3 real agencies, attempt to fully encode the comp grid for their top 5 carriers in a spreadsheet, time it / log ambiguities — before writing any engine code.
2. **A clean, matchable policy book exists.** Reconciliation needs the agency's in-force/sold policies to join paid lines against. If the book lives only in carrier portals or a thin AMS with bad keys, matching quality collapses. Cheap test: pull one agency's policy export and one month of statements and hand-measure what % of paid lines auto-join.
3. **Structured-format coverage carries enough dollars early.** The bet is that spreadsheet/EDI/DTCC-style feeds plus a few PDF templates cover enough commission volume to find recoverable money in phase 1. Cheap test: for design partners, tally structured vs. PDF-only format share.
4. **Recoverable money actually exists at the claimed rate.** The 2%–8% figure is unvalidated; the contingency model dies if real leakage is <0.5% or dominated by items carriers won't honor. Cheap test: a manual spreadsheet-only audit of one agency's last 12 months against 3–5 carriers — the single highest-value spike, needs no product.
5. **Carriers will pay on a documented dispute.** Technically you can flag a shortfall; commercially the carrier must accept the package and remit. If carriers stonewall or the dispute process is adversarial, found money isn't collectible. Cheap test: interview 2–3 principals on their actual dispute experience and hit rate.

## INFORMATION SECURITY POSTURE
Data is sensitive but bounded: carrier commission statements, the agency's policy book, comp-grid/contract terms, recovery correspondence. Financial and commercial data, very likely containing PII (insured names, producer SSNs/tax IDs on some statements, policy numbers) — treat as PII/financial-data from day one. Single managed cloud: Postgres for structured data, S3 for raw statements/packages, encrypted at rest (KMS) and in transit (TLS). Threat model: (a) multi-tenant data leakage — one agency seeing another's commissions is catastrophic to trust; enforce strict tenant isolation (RLS / tenant-scoped queries) and test it explicitly; (b) credential/secrets exposure — secrets manager, never embed carrier-portal creds, prefer human-pulled files over storing carrier-portal logins at all (storing carrier MFA credentials would be a meaningful liability to avoid in the MVP); (c) raw-statement store as crown jewels — least-privilege, no public buckets, signed URLs with expiry. Launch controls: managed auth with MFA, RBAC, encryption everywhere, audit logging of who-viewed/exported-what (table stakes for financial-dispute data), automated encrypted backups, lightweight written incident-response plan. At scale / first enterprise-ish ask: SOC 2 Type II is attainable but a deliberate 6–12 month program; design now (logging, least privilege, change control) to avoid retrofit. Not HIPAA, but producer SSNs mean minimize/mask/avoid ingesting fields you don't need.

## TOP 3 TECHNICAL STRENGTHS
- The core economic computation is deterministic, auditable, and provable — re-deriving expected comp from a versioned grid and diffing against paid lines is plain arithmetic, so "is this number right?" risk is low and the output is defensible to a carrier.
- The proprietary data assets compound: a growing library of per-carrier statement templates, encoded comp grids, and normalized historical ledgers is a real, owned, portable moat that gets cheaper-per-carrier and harder to replicate over time.
- Revenue can start before the full product exists — the contingency historical audit is deliverable semi-manually on structured formats in the first ~4–6 dev-weeks, so the founders are not staring down a 12-month pre-revenue build.

## TOP 3 TECHNICAL RISKS
- Ingestion/matching long tail: hundreds of carriers × evolving PDF layouts × inconsistent policy/producer keys means extraction and entity resolution are a perpetual grind, and accuracy directly determines whether flagged discrepancies are real or noise.
- Input-data availability: the comp grid and a clean, matchable policy book are the two required inputs, and both are messy, partly tribal, and not sitting in queryable systems — if they can't be captured reliably, "expected" is undefined.
- Carrier-portal dependency and credential handling: if the model drifts toward scraping portals behind MFA, you inherit ToS exposure, brittle automation, and a serious security liability (storing carrier logins) — must be deliberately bounded to human-pulled files in the MVP.

## BIGGEST SINGLE RISK
The biggest single risk is not the code — it's the data acquisition the code depends on, specifically the comp grid combined with a matchable policy book. The entire value proposition is "we re-compute what you were *owed* and compare it to what you were *paid*." That requires, per agency, a faithful machine-readable encoding of every relevant compensation rule (first-year, renewal/trail by policy year, override tiers, bonus thresholds, by carrier and product, effective-dated as schedules change) and a clean policy book whose keys actually join to the carrier's paid lines. In reality, comp terms are scattered across carrier contracts and addenda and live partly in the principal's memory, with genuine ambiguity about edge cases; and many small agencies' books are fragmented across carrier portals and a weak AMS with inconsistent identifiers. If encoding a single agency's grid takes days of expert interpretation and the policy book auto-joins at, say, 60%, the system either produces low-confidence noise (false "underpayments" that embarrass the agency with its carriers) or requires so much human reconciliation per client that the unit economics collapse into a labor-bound consultancy rather than a software-leveraged product. This is testable for near-zero cost before a line of engine code — encode 3 real agencies' top-5-carrier grids by hand and measure the auto-join rate on one month of statements — and that single spike should gate whether this is a software business or a services business.

## QUESTIONS THE FOUNDERS MUST ANSWER
- For a representative design-partner agency, how long does it take to fully encode their comp grid for their top 5 carriers, where does ambiguity arise, and how often do comp schedules change in ways that force re-encoding and historical recompute?
- When you take one agency's policy-book export and one cycle of real statements, what percentage of paid lines auto-join to the right policy + expected rule on available keys, and what does the residual require to resolve?
- What share of a target agency's commission dollars arrives in structured/EDI/DTCC-style formats vs. PDF-only vs. portal-only, and is structured-format coverage alone enough to surface collectible recoveries in phase 1?
- For the contingency wedge, when a real shortfall is documented and packaged, will carriers actually accept and remit — what is the realistic dispute hit rate and time-to-collection?
- Are you committing to keep carrier-portal credential storage and scraping out of scope (human-pulled files only) for the MVP, given the ToS and security liability — and if not, who owns that risk?

## RECOMMENDATION: REFINE
The technology is feasible and well-shaped for a two-founder bootstrap, and the revenue-before-full-product structure is genuinely attractive — but two cheap, decisive spikes must run before committing engineering: (1) hand-encode 3 real agencies' top-5-carrier comp grids and measure effort + ambiguity, and (2) measure the policy-book-to-statement auto-join rate on real data, ideally alongside a manual spreadsheet-only historical audit to confirm collectible leakage actually exists at a meaningful rate. Scope-wise: build the deterministic ledger + comp-grid engine first (the provable spine), start ingestion with structured/EDI formats and the top 3–6 PDF templates the design partners actually receive (not broad coverage), keep portal scraping and carrier-credential storage out of the MVP, and run the contingency audit human-in-the-loop so a low-confidence extraction is corrected rather than shipped as a false dispute. If the two data spikes come back favorable (grids encodable in hours not days, auto-join in the 80%+ range, real collectible dollars), this converts to a confident GO as a software-leveraged product; if they come back poorly, it's a labor-bound services business wearing a SaaS costume, and the model needs rethinking before code is written.

## ONE-PARAGRAPH COMPETITIVE READ
The pain is real, the forcing function is genuine (the agency's entire revenue is the thing being mispaid), and the leakage estimate checks out against third-party data — but this is NOT the sleepy, fragmented, marketing-led-entrant market the founders' thesis wants. Commission reconciliation has become one of the hottest, best-funded corners of insurtech in the last 24 months: Comulate raised a $20M Series B in Feb 2025 (total ~$25M, 8-figure revenue, customers like The Baldwin Group, Hilb, IMA), Fintary raised a $10M Series A in Nov 2025 (total $12.8M, explicitly serving life & annuity / EB / health / P&C), AgencyBloc's Commissions+ already ships a "Commission Not Received Report" rated 4.8/5 on Capterra, ComTrack already markets "AI-powered detection of missing renewal commissions" across 300+ L&H carriers, and eNoah already runs a done-for-you BPO that ingests carrier statements and re-computes expected comp against a client-supplied "Master Grid" for BGAs/IMOs. Two marketing-strong founders can absolutely sell into this segment, but they would be entering a crowded, venture-funded race where the headline value prop ("we find your missing commissions") is already the standard pitch from at least five named players — so the only defensible angle is the narrow, under-served wedge (true contract-entitlement audit + wrongful-chargeback recovery for small/mid L&A BGAs/IMOs, sold as found-money), not the category itself.

## MARKET STRUCTURE
This market is consolidating and heating, not fragmented and sleeping — the opposite of the founders' ideal. Three structural facts: (1) Venture capital is actively flowing into this exact category — Comulate $20M Series B (Feb 2025), Fintary $10M Series A (Nov 2025) — simultaneously demand validation and a danger signal; well-funded competitors are a stated thing-to-avoid in the founders' charter. (2) The broader insurance-agency-management software market is large and growing (~$4–4.8B in 2025–2026, ~8–9% CAGR), and insurtech funding rebounded +19.5% YoY to $5.1B in 2025 with ~two-thirds going to AI plays — capital and attention pointed straight at AI-on-carrier-statements. (3) Real layering: horizontal distribution platforms (AgentSync, a $1.2B unicorn, $161M raised) own producer onboarding and could extend into compensation; dominant AMS platforms (Applied, Vertafore, AgencyBloc) own the book of record and bolt reconciliation on as a module; pure-plays (Comulate, Fintary, ComTrack) attack the niche directly; and BPOs (eNoah) do it as a service. The customer relationship is mostly owned by whoever holds the AMS/book of record — for this target buyer, frequently AgencyBloc, an IMO's own portal (Ritter Platform), or an AMS — a meaningful distribution disadvantage for an independent entrant. Switching costs are real once installed (statement-format mappings, comp-grid encoding, AMS write-back, monthly workflow) — a moat for an incumbent already in the seat and a barrier for a new entrant trying to displace one. Net: the structure favors funded specialists and platform owners, with one genuinely under-served pocket — the small/mid L&A and annuity BGA / Medicare FMO whose payee-side contract-entitlement audit (not just payment recording) nobody is doing well.

## WEDGE & DIFFERENTIATION
The differentiation that is real and defensible is narrow, and it is not "we reconcile carrier statements" (commodity) — it is the combination of three things almost no one packages together for this buyer: (a) **true contract-entitlement auditing** — re-deriving what the agency's signed carrier contracts actually owe (comp grid: FYC/renewal schedules, override and bonus tiers by product and carrier) and comparing line-by-line, rather than inferring "expected" from prior payments or simply flagging zero-pay policies (what most tools do); (b) **wrongful/duplicate chargeback recovery** — auditing clawbacks against contract lookback windows and actual lapse/attribution facts, an area with genuine contractual/legal teeth (PIA, AgentSync, EverQuote document that chargebacks are governed by agency-agreement clauses and that wrongful/excessive chargebacks are disputable) that the payment-recording tools largely ignore; and (c) **the found-money go-to-market** — a contingency-fee historical audit that converts to subscription, removing the "why buy another tool" objection because the agency pays only out of recovered dollars. That packaging is a real wedge. The problem: each individual piece is copyable. Comulate already says "guide the recovery of missing and underpaid commissions," ComTrack already detects "missing renewals," eNoah already encodes a Master Grid, and contingency recovery-audit is a well-worn model in adjacent fields (AP recovery audits at 10–40% of findings; healthcare underpayment recovery). A funded incumbent could bolt a "contract-entitlement audit + contingency historical audit" onto an existing reconciliation engine in roughly one to two quarters. The durable moat, if any, is not the software — it is the proprietary library of carrier-contract structures and statement-format parsers built up across many agencies, plus the recovery playbook (knowing which carriers underpay what, and how to win the dispute), which compounds with volume. That is a data/expertise moat that builds with traction, but it is bridgeable by anyone with capital and time, and two of the most capable someones already have the capital.

## DEFENSIBILITY & SWITCHING COSTS
Once won, a customer is reasonably sticky: the comp grid is encoded, every carrier's statement format is mapped, historical data is loaded, and the monthly reconciliation becomes embedded in the finance team's close — ripping that out is painful, and the ongoing-monitoring subscription is exactly the recurring spine the founders want. Defensibility builds with traction in two ways: the carrier-contract-and-format library gets broader and cheaper to extend with each new agency, and a track record of recovered dollars becomes the proof asset that sells the next agency. However, the switching costs protect whoever is in the seat first — and for this buyer an incumbent (AgencyBloc, an IMO portal, or an AMS) is frequently already in the seat, so the founders must overcome an existing lock-in, not just win a greenfield. And the moat is data/relationship, not structural IP: no network effect across customers, no regulatory license barrier, no exclusive carrier data feed. So defensibility is "decent and compounding," not "wide and durable."

## COMPETITIVE-RESPONSE WAR-GAME
At $1–3M revenue, the most likely reactions: (1) Comulate or Fintary notices the L&A/annuity BGA segment converting on a "contract-entitlement audit + found-money" pitch and adds an entitlement-audit module and a contingency on-ramp — they have the engine, capital, and AMS integrations; the central threat, plausibly within 2–4 quarters of the wedge being proven. (2) AgencyBloc, sitting on the customer relationship and the comp grid for thousands of target agencies, deepens Commissions+ from "flag mismatches" to "audit against contract terms" and markets recovery harder — they have the logos, the 4.8/5 reviews, and the $109/seat beachhead, so they can bundle the wedge into a product the customer already pays for. (3) A BPO like eNoah, which already encodes Master Grids, repackages its service as a contingency recovery audit to defend its BGA accounts. (4) Encroachment/reach-down risk is HIGH-MODERATE: this concept sits adjacent to capabilities multiple funded horizontal/specialist platforms already own; it is not a standalone problem insulated from them — it is a sharper, better-targeted version of a job several well-capitalized players are already doing. The concept's best defense is speed into the specific under-served segment, the contract-audit + chargeback depth, and the recovery-fee wedge — and ideally a beachhead in the annuity/Medicare BGA niche the P&C-skewed incumbents are not focused on. But "we got there first in a niche" is a fragile moat against players with $13–25M in the bank and the same engine.

- On wrongful-chargeback recovery specifically: do you have evidence that disputing carrier clawbacks against contract lookback windows yields recoverable dollars at a rate worth a contingency, and that carriers will actually pay on those disputes without litigation — or is that line more legal-services than software, with collection risk you can't control?

## RECOMMENDATION: REFINE
The forcing function and demand are genuinely strong (an 8 on timing, corroborated leakage, documented recoveries), and there is a real under-served wedge — but the competitive structure is the wrong shape for the founders' "out-position sleepy incumbents in a fragmented market" thesis: this is an actively-consolidating, venture-funded category with at least two capitalized on-thesis pure-plays (Comulate, Fintary), an entrenched well-reviewed AMS module (AgencyBloc Commissions+), and an existing BPO (eNoah) that already does the comp-grid service. I would not greenlight the broad "commission reconciliation & recovery platform" as framed — it walks straight into funded competitors and hard constraint #5. The refine that could make this defensible: narrow hard to the single segment the funded P&C-skewed players neglect (small/mid L&A, annuity, and Medicare BGAs/FMOs), lead with the two genuinely under-built capabilities (true contract-entitlement auditing and wrongful-chargeback recovery, not generic statement reconciliation), and enter through the contingency "found-money" historical audit as a services-led wedge that builds a proprietary carrier-contract/format library before converting to subscription — explicitly accepting that you are racing the clock against Comulate/Fintary reaching down. If the founders can show, with three to five real target BGAs, that (a) the entitlement audit catches dollars existing tools miss, (b) those agencies are not already locked into an AMS module that does enough, and (c) the wrongful-chargeback recoveries are real and collectible, this becomes a credible B-grade niche play. Without that proof, the differentiation is positioning over substance against better-funded competitors running the same playbook.
