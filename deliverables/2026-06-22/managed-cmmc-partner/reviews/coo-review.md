COO REVIEW — Managed CMMC Level 2 Readiness & Continuity for Small Defense Contractors

ONE-PARAGRAPH OPERATIONS READ

This is an operationally favorable shape for a two-founder lean team: the unit of work is a knowledge/document deliverable (a scoped boundary, an SSP, a POA&M, an evidence package, an annual affirmation) rather than a truck, an install, or an on-site repeat visit — so it is squarely a remote-first, software-leveraged services model, not field ops. The real operational question is not "can it be delivered remotely" (it can) but "how heavy is the human hour per certification, and how much of that can AI genuinely absorb so one success manager can carry a portfolio that pencils to the income ladder." The two genuine operational dependencies that make or break the model are external, not internal: the network of independent accredited assessors (a known capacity bottleneck industry-wide) and the implementation partners who do the actual technical remediation the founders are explicitly outsourcing. The model is operable by a lean team, but the throughput math hinges on how much of the 110-control implementation is "orchestrated" versus "done," and that is the single number I most need validated.

THE TUESDAY-IN-MARCH WALKTHROUGH

Picture the business at roughly month 12. Assume [ASSUMED] a delivery team of the two founders plus one CMMC-credentialed delivery lead and two compliance success managers (call them SMs), with AI tooling and a roster of implementation partners and assessors on standby. The portfolio at this point is, say, 25–35 active first-time certification projects in various stages plus a small but growing book of post-certification continuity retainers. Here is the actual Tuesday.

8:30 a.m. — Onboarding a new client. A 40-person precision machine shop in Ohio signed last Thursday after coming in through an APEX Accelerator referral. SM Maria kicks off onboarding. This is not a sales call — the buy is already forced and closed — it is a kickoff: she sends a pre-built intake packet, schedules the discovery scan, and collects the firm's contract requirements (which CUI types, which primes, which deadline). The owner's only homework is to grant the discovery tool read access to file shares, M365/Google Workspace, and to point at the engineering CAD repository. Maria's leverage here is that the intake is productized — same checklist every time — so this consumes maybe 60–90 minutes of her day, not a week.

9:30 a.m. — Running discovery on a different client. The CUI-discovery scan for last week's client (a specialty electronics firm) finished overnight. This is the highest-value, most defensible step and where the human judgment concentrates. The software has flagged ~4,000 files as candidate CUI, including drawings buried in a shared "old projects" folder and PDFs in an estimator's personal mailbox. The tool does the heavy lifting (scanning CAD/PDF/scanned images that generic DLP misses), but a human must adjudicate scope: is this folder in-boundary or can it be quarantined out, can we shrink the enclave to 12 seats instead of 40, is this email actually CUI or just ITAR-adjacent marketing. Getting this wrong is the most expensive mistake in the whole process, so the delivery lead (not the SM) reviews the boundary decision. Call it 2–3 hours of senior time on this client today.

11:00 a.m. — AI-generated documentation review. For a third client now past discovery, the AI has drafted the gap analysis against all 110 controls, a tailored SSP, and a POA&M. The SM's job is not to write these from scratch — that is the AI leverage — it is to review, correct, and contextualize: confirm the AI didn't hallucinate a control as "met," align the POA&M dates to the client's contract deadline, flag the five controls that need real technical remediation. This is editing, not authoring — perhaps 2 hours.

1:00 p.m. — Remediation orchestration (the crux). A fourth client needs the actual technical fixes: stand up a GCC High or compliant enclave, enforce MFA, turn on logging/monitoring, deploy endpoint protection, build incident-response capability. The founders' model is explicitly that they orchestrate rather than personally configure — so today the SM is coordinating a government-cloud implementation partner who is doing the enclave build, and an MSP partner handling endpoint deployment, while the AI generates the runbooks and the SM tracks the POA&M items to closure. The operational reality check: the SM is now a project manager across two outside vendors on someone else's network, on the client's schedule, with the client's existing IT contractor sometimes in the way. This is where elapsed time balloons (weeks of partner lead time) even if internal hours stay modest. Call it 1.5 hours of coordination today, but it is recurring across the multi-week project.

2:30 p.m. — Mock assessment / evidence packaging. A fifth client is nearly audit-ready. The SM and delivery lead run a mock assessment against the assembled evidence, the AI cross-references each of the 110 controls to its evidence artifact, and they identify three weak spots to shore up before the real C3PAO assessor arrives. Then the prepared package is handed to the independent accredited assessor from the partner network — and here is the scheduling dependency: that assessor is booked six to ten weeks out [ASSUMED — verify against current C3PAO availability], so this hand-off had to be reserved well in advance. The provider performs no assessment and issues no certification; they prepare and support.

4:00 p.m. — Managed continuity (the recurring engine). For the back book of already-certified clients, the continuity platform pings: one client added a new file share that may contain CUI (drift detected), another has an annual affirmation due in 30 days, and a prime just sent one of them a flow-down security questionnaire. The SM's value here is largely automated — the system auto-answers the questionnaire from stored evidence, queues the affirmation, and flags the drift for a quick remediation ticket. This is the lowest-hour, highest-margin work in the business, and it is the part that should scale beautifully because the heavy lifting was done during certification and the evidence is already stored.

Where things break on this Tuesday: (1) the enclave partner slips two weeks and a client's contract deadline is now at risk — the founders own the "you will pass" promise but don't control the partner's calendar; (2) the assessor reschedules and the whole prepared package sits idle; (3) a discovery scan under-scoped a client six months ago and a new CUI repository surfaces, forcing rework the firm sold as "done." Notice that none of the break points are field-ops or manual-labor — they are dependency-management and scope-accuracy problems, which is a far healthier operational profile for these founders.

CAPACITY MATH

First-time certification project — internal delivery hours. Industry consultants commonly describe a small-firm CMMC L2 readiness engagement as a multi-month effort. - Discovery scan oversight + boundary adjudication (senior): ~6 hours
- AI-doc review/correction (SSP, POA&M, gap analysis): ~10 hours
- Remediation orchestration / partner & client PM across the project: ~25 hours
- Mock assessment + evidence packaging + assessor hand-off + assessment support: ~16 hours
Total internal hours ≈ 60 hours per first-time certification [ASSUMED range: 40–100 hours depending on how much remediation is truly partner-delivered vs. SM-managed]. Cheap verification: run two paid pilots and time-track every task category; the single most important number to measure is the remediation-orchestration line, because that is the widest range.

What one success manager + AI can carry (first-time projects). A productive SM has ~1,400–1,500 billable/delivery hours/year after overhead. At 60 internal hours per project, one SM carries ≈ 1,450 / 60 ≈ 24 first-time certifications per year, but these run concurrently over 3–6 month windows, so the steady-state concurrent load is the binding constraint, not the annual count. If a project runs ~4 months and an SM can actively manage ~8–10 concurrent projects without quality slipping [ASSUMED — verify in pilot], that is consistent with ~24/year throughput. Two SMs + delivery lead spillover ≈ 45–55 first-time certs/year capacity.

Continuity retainer — internal hours. This is the recurring engine and it is light because the heavy work is automated post-certification: drift monitoring, annual affirmation, auto-answered questionnaires, re-cert prep. Estimate ~2–4 hours of human time per client per MONTH [ASSUMED range; verify in pilot]. At ~3 hrs/client/mo = 36 hrs/client/yr, one SM's ~1,450 hours could nominally carry ~40 continuity clients — but you would never load an SM 100% on continuity; realistically blend it. The key point: continuity hours are roughly 1/15th of a first-time project's hours per unit of revenue, so the portfolio gets MORE operationally efficient as it matures. That is the margin engine.

Revenue tie-out to the income ladder. Using the one-pager's [ASSUMED] pricing — $15K–$40K upfront (midpoint ~$27K) and $1.5K–$5K/mo retainer (midpoint ~$3K/mo = $36K/yr):
- 50 first-time certs/year × ~$27K ≈ $1.35M one-time revenue.
- A continuity book that accumulates: if ~70% convert and stick, after ~18–24 months you have 60–80 clients on retainer × ~$36K ≈ $2.2M–$2.9M ARR.
- Combined run-rate in the $3.5M–$4M range against a team of ~5 (2 founders + lead + 2 SMs) plus partner pass-through costs. Net to two founders comfortably clears the ~$300K-each mo-12 rung and is on a credible path to the ~$500K+ mo-24 rung, assuming retainer retention holds and partner margins aren't crushed. This is a SHOWN bottoms-up calc, not an adjective — and it supports the throughput score below.

Where the bottleneck lands first. Not internal labor. The first binding constraints are external: (1) assessor availability — a documented industry-wide C3PAO capacity shortage means your "you will pass" promise can be gated by a calendar you don't own; (2) implementation-partner capacity and reliability — you are renting the riskiest, most schedule-sensitive part of the deliverable; (3) scope-accuracy at discovery — the one place a human error creates expensive, reputation-damaging rework. The internal SM model scales fine; the partner and assessor ecosystem is the throttle.

BIGGEST SINGLE RISK

The single operational issue most likely to hurt this business is that the founders are selling an absolute outcome — "you will pass, and you will stay compliant" — while the two most failure-prone steps in delivering that outcome are owned by third parties they cannot control. The actual certification is issued by an independent accredited assessor (a C3PAO) whose availability is a documented, industry-wide bottleneck, and whose pass/fail judgment the provider does not control; and the technical remediation — standing up a compliant enclave, MFA, logging, endpoint protection — is executed by implementation partners on the client's own network, on the client's schedule, often with the client's existing IT contractor in the loop. So the provider has put its brand and its money-back-implying promise on a result that is gated by a calendar it doesn't own and engineering work it doesn't perform. The reason this is the biggest risk rather than a fatal one is that it is manageable with operational design — building deep redundancy in the assessor and implementation-partner networks, pricing in schedule buffers, defining "pass" obligations carefully in the contract, and keeping the one truly proprietary, in-house-controlled step (CUI scoping) excellent so that what the firm IS accountable for is also what it does best. But if the founders under-invest in partner-network depth and partner SLAs, a single assessor backlog or a botched enclave build can blow a client's contract deadline, trigger a refund, and — worst of all in a tight-knit DIB referral market — poison the word-of-mouth channel the entire partner-led GTM depends on. The fix is contractual and operational, not technical, and it must be designed in from day one.

QUESTIONS THE FOUNDERS MUST ANSWER BEFORE I'M COMFORTABLE
- What are the true internal human hours per first-time certification, broken out by task category — and specifically, how many of the ~110 controls' remediation are genuinely partner-delivered (you orchestrate) versus SM-delivered (you do the work)? This is the single number that decides whether one SM carries 8 concurrent projects or 3. Verify with two paid, time-tracked pilots before scaling headcount.
- Exactly how does the "you will pass" promise read in the contract, and how is partner failure handled? If an assessor backlog or a partner's slipped enclave build blows a client's deadline, who eats the cost, what's the SLA with the partner, and how deep is the redundancy in both networks (how many assessors and how many enclave/MSP partners are signed before you sell the promise)?
- Are you building or buying the differentiating CUI-discovery scanner that reads CAD/PDF/scanned drawings — and if buying, who owns that vendor relationship, what does it cost per scan, and what is your exposure if they raise prices or get acquired? The entry-point wedge depends on this capability being reliable and economically yours.
- What is the realistic continuity-retainer attach and retention rate after the one-time certification is delivered, and what is the actual monthly human-hour load per retained client? The recurring engine is the whole long-term thesis; verify attach/retention assumptions against the first cohort of certified clients.

RECOMMENDATION: GO (with focused operational de-risking)

Operationally this is a strong fit for a lean, remote-first, AI-leveraged two-founder team: the unit of work is a standardized knowledge deliverable against a fixed standard, there is no field-ops core, the logistics profile is near-ideal, and the recurring continuity layer is a genuinely scaling margin engine whose capacity math ties out to the founders' income ladder on a ~5-person team. My GO is qualified, not unconditional: the model's operational center of gravity is dependency management, and the founders are putting an absolute outcome promise on assessor and implementation-partner ecosystems they don't control, against a backdrop of documented assessor scarcity. The specific de-risking I want before scaling is (1) two paid, fully time-tracked pilots to nail the per-cert internal-hours number and the orchestrate-vs-do remediation split, (2) signed depth and SLAs in BOTH the assessor and implementation-partner networks before the "you will pass" promise is marketed at volume, and (3) clarity on whether the CUI-discovery scanner is owned or rented. None of these are field-ops disqualifiers; they are the right operational homework for a services model whose risk lives in its dependencies. Get those three answered and this is a clean, repeatable, lean operation.
