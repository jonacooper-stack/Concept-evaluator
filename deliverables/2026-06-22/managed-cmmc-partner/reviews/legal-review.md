LEGAL / REGULATORY REVIEW — Managed CMMC Level 2 Readiness for Small Defense Subcontractors ("You run your shop; we run your compliance")

Standard disclaimer, stated once: I am not a licensed attorney and this is not formal legal advice. I am an experienced operator with strong regulatory pattern-recognition flagging the issues the founders must take to real counsel. Treat everything below as a due-diligence map, not a legal opinion.

ONE-PARAGRAPH RISK READ

This is a regulated-adjacent services business operating in one of the cleanest, most explicitly-mapped compliance regimes the federal government has ever published — CMMC is defined by 32 CFR Part 170 (the program rule, effective December 16, 2024), 48 CFR / DFARS 252.204-7021 (the contract clause), and a measurable technical standard (NIST SP 800-171 Rev. 2, 110 controls). The founders are deliberately staying on the preparation side of the industry's bright-line independence rule (they will be a Registered Provider Organization, an RPO, and will NOT become the accredited assessor / C3PAO), which is the single most important architectural decision in the whole concept and is exactly the right one. The genuinely novel legal exposure here is NOT "is CMMC regulated" — it is the liability shift the business voluntarily takes on: it sells an outcome ("you will pass"), it touches/orchestrates the implementation of the controls being audited, it runs automated discovery of where a customer's CUI lives, and it helps the customer prepare a federal affirmation the customer's senior official signs under penalty of the civil False Claims Act. That bundle is insurable and contractable, but it is real professional-liability and FCA-adjacent exposure that must be firewalled deliberately rather than waved away with a terms-of-service line. Net: a regulated, navigable, bridgeable concept with a well-named path and a manageable — not trivial — liability tail.

RISK-GATE RATING: SERIOUS BUT MANAGEABLE

The regime is explicitly published, the founders' chosen role (RPO, not C3PAO) avoids the one credential they cannot scaffold and the one independence rule that could be fatal, and the residual exposure (E&O, FCA flow-down via the customer's affirmation, custody-of-CUI risk) is insurable and contractable with a named, well-trodden path. Per 01-objectives.md this rating is a due-diligence checklist and does NOT lower the concept's tier — it is, in fact, exactly the kind of forced-demand, navigable-moat business this thesis favors. I reserve FATAL for an unbridgeable credential, an uninsurable tail, or a prohibition, and none of those is present: the assessor accreditation the founders lack is one they have deliberately chosen not to pursue, and the demand-driving mandate (the thing that makes this a must-have) is credited elsewhere, not penalized here.

REGULATORY MAP

The specific regimes that apply, each with regulator, scope, and rough compliance burden:
- CMMC Program Rule — 32 CFR Part 170 (DoD / Office of the CISO; administered through The Cyber AB as the accreditation body). Defines CMMC levels, the C3PAO / assessor ecosystem, the RPO and Registered Practitioner (RP) designations, and the independence rules. Scope for this business: it governs what role the provider may play. Burden: LOW-to-MODERATE — registering as an RPO is an administrative process through The Cyber AB (background/agreement, listing, code of conduct), not an exam-gated license. This is the load-bearing rule, and it is published, stable as of the final rule, and explicitly contemplates the "advisor who is not the assessor" role the founders want.
- DFARS 252.204-7021 (CMMC clause) and 252.204-7012 (safeguarding CDI / 72-hour cyber-incident reporting) — DoD, 48 CFR. Scope: these bind the customer (the defense contractor), not the provider directly, but they define the deliverable the provider is selling readiness against and the incident-reporting obligations the provider's "managed continuity" component touches. Burden: the provider must understand them cold; they are public and finite.
- NIST SP 800-171 Rev. 2 (110 controls) and the associated assessment methodology (NIST SP 800-171A); NIST SP 800-172 for higher tiers. Scope: the technical standard the whole service is built around. Burden: this is competence, not licensure — it is exactly the kind of explicit, enumerated standard the founders' SOC 2 / HIPAA / GDPR operator background makes bridgeable.
- DFARS 252.204-7019 / 7020 (SPRS self-assessment scoring and DoD access to systems). Scope: the interim self-assessment scoring posted to the Supplier Performance Risk System; relevant because the provider may help customers post scores and because a knowingly false SPRS score is a documented False Claims Act theory (see DOJ Civil Cyber-Fraud Initiative enforcement). Burden: MODERATE attention — this is where the provider's outputs touch a representation the government relies on.
- Civil False Claims Act, 31 U.S.C. §§ 3729–3733 (DOJ Civil Cyber-Fraud Initiative). Scope: the affirmation a senior company official signs (and SPRS scores) are statements to the government; a knowing misrepresentation of compliance is an active, named enforcement priority. The provider does not sign, but it prepares the package the affirmation rests on. Burden: this is the most important risk vector to firewall — see LIABILITY POSTURE.
- Controlled Unclassified Information regime — 32 CFR Part 2002 (NARA) and the underlying agency markings. Scope: the discovery component scans for CUI; the provider stores CUI metadata and locations and is architected NOT to take custody of the CUI itself. Burden: MODERATE, and it is the second key firewall — handling, even incidentally, of actual CUI (e.g., a CAD file pulled into the provider's environment during a scan) implicates FedRAMP-Moderate-equivalent / DFARS 7012 handling expectations and ITAR if any technical data is export-controlled.
- ITAR (22 CFR 120–130, DDTC / State Dept.) and EAR (15 CFR, BIS / Commerce). Scope: defense-contractor CAD drawings and technical data are frequently ITAR-controlled "technical data." If the provider's discovery tool, enclave partner, or any team member who is a non-U.S. person ever accesses such data, that is a potential export ("deemed export") even without it leaving the country. Burden: MODERATE-to-SERIOUS if not designed around; LOW if the architecture genuinely never touches the data and staffing is U.S.-persons for any data-adjacent role.
- Standard data-privacy baseline — CCPA/CPRA, state breach-notification laws (all 50 states), and the provider's own SOC 2. Scope: the provider holds customer compliance documentation, evidence, and CUI location metadata — sensitive, but largely B2B business data, not consumer PII or special categories. Burden: LOW-to-MODERATE; squarely in the founders' demonstrated competence.
- FTC Act §5 / advertising-substantiation and state UDAP statutes. Scope: the marketing claim "you will pass" and "stay compliant" is an outcome guarantee; if a client fails or is found non-compliant, an unsubstantiated or absolute claim is a deceptive-practices and breach-of-contract exposure. Burden: LOW if the claim is carefully framed ("we prepare you to pass / we get you assessment-ready," with defined remedies), SERIOUS if marketed as an unconditional guarantee.
- Federal anti-kickback / referral-fee considerations on the assessor handoff. Scope: the possible "referral / qualified-handoff fees" from partner C3PAOs. The federal Anti-Kickback Statute (42 U.S.C. § 1320a-7b) is healthcare-specific and does NOT apply here; this is a commercial referral arrangement, not a government-payment one. The real constraint is CMMC assessor-independence and conflict-of-interest rules under 32 CFR Part 170 / The Cyber AB code of conduct — a C3PAO must be independent of the entity that prepared the client. Burden: MODERATE — the fee can flow in the prep-to-assessor direction with disclosure, but a fee that compromises (or appears to compromise) the assessor's independence, or any payment contingent on a passing result, is the danger zone.

LICENSING SUMMARY

No occupational or state professional license is required to run this business, and that is a real strength — say so confidently. The business is a B2B advisory/managed-services firm; it needs only ordinary formation (an LLC or C-corp), a local business license, and standard registrations. The relevant "credential" landscape is voluntary and tiered, and the founders' plan threads it correctly:
- RPO (Registered Provider Organization) — the founders' stated plan. An administrative registration through The Cyber AB: organizational agreement, code of conduct, listing as an authorized provider of CMMC consulting/implementation. No exam gate at the org level. This is the right and sufficient credential for a preparation business and is fully bridgeable. Must-do before launch.
- RP / CCP / CCA (Registered Practitioner / Certified CMMC Professional / Certified CMMC Assessor) — individual credentials. The plan to "add a CMMC-credentialed delivery lead" maps to hiring or training someone holding CCP/CCA. This is a hire/learn item, not a barrier — it is exactly the kind of scaffolding 01-objectives.md and 08-founder-profile.md treat as bridgeable. Must-do before scale; partially before launch for credibility.
- C3PAO accreditation — DELIBERATELY NOT PURSUED. Becoming an accredited assessor requires CMMC Level 2 certification of the assessor org itself plus accreditation through The Cyber AB / A2LA, and — critically — would prohibit the firm from both preparing and assessing the same client under the independence rule. The founders' decision to remain on the prep side is the single best legal decision in the concept. The credential they "lack" is one they correctly chose not to need.
- No multi-state license multiplication. Unlike a contractor or broker, this business does not re-license per state. It is one federal standard applied to clients nationwide. This is a major navigability advantage versus, say, a 50-state insurance or home-services play.

Insurance "licensing" is not applicable. The genuine pre-launch paperwork is: entity formation, RPO registration, E&O/cyber/tech-E&O coverage bound (see below), and a templated MSA with the liability firewalls built in.

LIABILITY POSTURE

This is where the concept earns its "SERIOUS BUT MANAGEABLE" rating, and where the founders must spend their counsel budget. The business voluntarily concentrates four liability vectors that a pure tool-vendor or a pure assessor would not carry together:

1. Outcome-guarantee / professional-liability (E&O) exposure. Selling "you will pass" is selling a result the provider does not fully control (the independent assessor, and the customer's own follow-through, decide the actual outcome). If a client fails its assessment, loses a contract, and blames the prep, that is a professional-negligence / breach claim. Handled by: Technology / Professional Errors & Omissions insurance (tech-E&O) is standard, available, and affordable for advisory firms — [ASSUMED ~$5K–$20K/yr in early-stage premium for the coverage levels a 15–100-employee-serving advisory would carry; verify with two specialty brokers, e.g., a Coalition/Embroker-class tech-E&O quote]. Plus: the contract must convert "you will pass" into a defined, bounded commitment — a pass-readiness warranty with a specific remedy (re-remediation at no charge, fee credit, or a capped refund), an explicit disclaimer that the independent assessor and the client's cooperation determine the result, a limitation-of-liability clause capping damages at fees paid, and a waiver of consequential/contract-loss damages. The marketing must match the contract: "we get you ready to pass," not an unconditional "guaranteed certification." What's left over after E&O + contract caps is reputational, not existential.

2. False Claims Act / affirmation flow-down — the sharpest vector. The customer's senior official signs an annual affirmation of continued compliance, and posts SPRS scores; these are representations to the government, and the DOJ Civil Cyber-Fraud Initiative is actively prosecuting knowingly false cyber-compliance claims. The provider "helps prepare" the package those representations rest on. The provider is not the signer and (on a negligence theory) is insulated, but a knowing misrepresentation theory could try to reach a preparer. Handled by: (a) the customer, not the provider, always makes and signs the affirmation — keep that bright line; (b) the SSP/POA&M and evidence must reflect the actual environment (the discovery + evidence-of-control design supports this — accuracy is the defense); (c) contractual representations that the client is responsible for the truth of its submissions and for maintaining controls between engagements; (d) documented refusal to paper over gaps (no "just attest and we'll fix it later"). What's left over is the residual risk that a client lies using the provider's documents — mitigated by accurate-evidence discipline and an engagement-termination right.

3. CUI-custody and export-control (ITAR) exposure. The discovery scanner reads the customer's environment, including CAD/PDF/scanned/image files that may be CUI and may be ITAR-controlled technical data. The design intent — store metadata and locations, NOT custody of the CUI — is the correct firewall, but "scanning" can incidentally ingest content (OCR of a scanned drawing, image classification, indexing). If actual CUI/technical data transits or rests in the provider's environment, the provider has inherited DFARS 252.204-7012 / FedRAMP-Moderate-equivalent handling obligations and, for ITAR data, a potential deemed-export problem if any non-U.S. person can access it. Handled by: (a) architect the scanner to classify-in-place and persist only metadata/hashes/locations, with content processing happening inside the customer's boundary or a customer-controlled enclave (the implementation-partner enclave model supports this); (b) restrict any data-adjacent role and any subprocessor to U.S. persons / U.S. soil; (c) a DPA/data-handling addendum and a written ITAR technology-control plan; (d) carry cyber liability insurance covering breach of the metadata the provider does hold. What's left over is the engineering discipline to ensure "we never touch the CUI" is true in fact, not just in the marketing — this is a CTO/architecture obligation as much as a legal one.

4. Assessor referral-fee / independence exposure. A C3PAO must be independent of the party that prepared the client (32 CFR Part 170 / Cyber AB code of conduct). A referral fee flowing from the assessor to the prep firm, or any arrangement where compensation turns on the client passing, can be argued to compromise that independence and could jeopardize the assessment's validity (the worst outcome for the client) and the assessor's standing. Handled by: (a) keep referral fees, if any, flat / per-qualified-handoff and explicitly NOT contingent on the assessment result; (b) disclose the relationship to the client in writing; (c) maintain a network of multiple independent assessors and let the client choose, rather than a single tied assessor; (d) confirm the structure against the current Cyber AB COI guidance with counsel before taking a dollar. What's left over is small if the fee is non-contingent, disclosed, and the client retains assessor choice; it becomes serious only if the fee looks like a kickback for a passing grade.

Personal exposure pre-entity: standard and easily closed — form the entity before signing any client or partner agreement, keep the corporate veil (separate accounts, capitalization, formalities), and the founders' personal exposure drops to the ordinary minimum. General liability + tech-E&O + cyber is the insurance stack; the MSA + DPA + assessor-disclosure are the contracts; the residual is reputational and FCA-adjacent, both of which are mitigated by accuracy discipline.

BIGGEST SINGLE RISK

The single risk most likely to seriously slow or wound this business is the False Claims Act flow-down created by the combination of the outcome guarantee and the customer's signed affirmation. The structure is: the provider promises "you will pass and stay compliant," builds and orchestrates the actual controls, prepares the SSP/POA&M and evidence package, and then the customer's senior official signs an annual affirmation of continued compliance and posts SPRS scores — representations the DoD relies on to award and retain contracts. The DOJ's Civil Cyber-Fraud Initiative has made knowingly false cybersecurity-compliance representations a named, active enforcement priority, with settlements already on the board. The provider is not the signer and, on an ordinary negligence theory, sits behind the customer's own attestation — but the provider's deep, hands-on involvement in producing the very documents the affirmation rests on creates a plausible argument that a relator or the government could try to reach the preparer on a knowing-misrepresentation or causation theory, and even an unsuccessful attempt is expensive and reputationally toxic in a small market where assessors and primes talk. The mitigation is real and within the founders' control — keep the bright line that the customer makes and signs every government representation; ensure the SSP/POA&M and evidence reflect the actual environment (accuracy is the complete defense to an FCA knowledge element); contractually place responsibility for the truth of submissions and for between-engagement control maintenance on the client; refuse in writing to paper over gaps; and carry tech-E&O. Done right, this is a managed risk, not a fatal one — but it is the issue that most warrants real counsel before the first contract is signed, and the one that, if handled sloppily (e.g., "just attest now, we'll remediate the POA&M later"), could turn a good business into a defendant.

QUESTIONS FOR REAL COUNSEL BEFORE LAUNCH
1. FCA/affirmation firewall: How do we contractually and operationally insulate the firm from False Claims Act exposure when we prepare the SSP/POA&M/evidence and the client signs the affirmation and posts SPRS scores? What specific representations, disclaimers, and "client is responsible for the truth of all government submissions" language do we need, and does our refusal-to-paper-over-gaps policy need to be documented in the engagement letter?
2. Outcome-guarantee framing: How do we word "you will pass / stay compliant" in both the contract and the marketing so it is a bounded, defined-remedy warranty (re-work / fee credit / capped refund) rather than an unconditional guarantee that creates FTC §5 advertising-substantiation and breach exposure? What is the right limitation-of-liability cap and consequential-damages waiver for a firm serving 15–100-employee shops?
3. Assessor referral-fee vs. independence: Under the current 32 CFR Part 170 / Cyber AB conflict-of-interest and assessor-independence rules, what referral or qualified-handoff fee structure with partner C3PAOs is permissible? Specifically, can the fee flow from the assessor to us, must it be non-contingent on a passing result, what disclosure to the client is required, and does maintaining a multi-assessor network (client chooses) sufficiently protect both the assessment's validity and our standing?
4. CUI custody / ITAR architecture: Given the discovery scanner reads CAD/PDF/scanned/image files, at what point does "metadata only" become "custody of CUI," and what triggers DFARS 252.204-7012 / FedRAMP-Moderate-equivalent handling duties for us? If any scanned data is ITAR-controlled technical data, what is our deemed-export exposure, and what technology-control plan and U.S.-persons staffing restrictions do we need for any data-adjacent role or subprocessor?
5. Insurance adequacy: What tech-E&O and cyber-liability limits and policy language (does the policy cover claims arising from a client's certification failure or a client's false affirmation that used our work product?) do we need, and will a specialty broker write coverage for an outcome-guaranteeing CMMC prep firm at the early-stage scale we're starting at?
6. Partner liability allocation: In the MSAs with the cloud-enclave and CUI-discovery technology partners, how do we allocate responsibility if a partner's enclave or tool causes a control failure or a data exposure that costs a client its assessment — indemnities, flow-down of DFARS obligations, and insurance requirements on the partners?
