# CTO REVIEW — Self-Serve CMMC / NIST 800-171 Compliance Software for Small Defense Subcontractors

ONE-PARAGRAPH TECHNICAL READ
Technically this is a well-scoped, mostly-off-the-shelf SaaS build: a multi-tenant web app over Postgres, Stripe for billing, and an LLM (Claude/GPT-4-class) doing retrieval-augmented document grading and questionnaire auto-fill against a fixed, knowable corpus (the 110 NIST 800-171 r2 control objectives in NIST SP 800-171A, plus the DoD SPRS scoring methodology). The founders' explicit design decision to hold ONLY compliance documentation (policies, control status, questionnaire answers) and NOT the customer's CUI or operational security telemetry (configs, logs, scans) is the single smartest technical and security choice in the one-pager — it removes the CUI/ITAR data-residency nightmare that would otherwise force FedRAMP-Moderate-equivalent / GovCloud hosting and effectively kill a bootstrap. The real risk is not buildability; it is the accuracy and liability of LLM-generated scores and policy text in a domain where being wrong has a concrete consequence for the customer, and it is the upgrade-path trap if the product is ever pulled (by customers or by its own roadmap) toward ingesting actual CUI/CMMC evidence.

ARCHITECTURE SKETCH
- Frontend: Next.js (React) on Vercel — marketing site, free-assessment funnel, authenticated app, progress dashboards, document editor/viewer. Off-the-shelf; no novel UI.
- Backend / API: a single application service (Next.js API routes or a thin Node/Python service on Render/Fly/AWS) — stateless request handling, business logic for scoring, remediation workflow state, questionnaire assembly.
- Data store: Postgres (managed — Supabase/RDS/Neon) for tenants, users, control status per tenant, document metadata, questionnaire answers, score history; object storage (S3) for uploaded policy files and generated PDFs; a vector store for retrieval — and here the right bootstrap call is pgvector inside the same Postgres rather than a separate Pinecone/Weaviate service, since the per-tenant corpus is small (tens to low hundreds of short documents) and this keeps the data architecture to one durable system.
- LLM / AI layer: a hosted frontier model API (Anthropic Claude or OpenAI) behind a thin orchestration layer doing (a) document-to-control-objective grading, (b) gap explanation generation, (c) questionnaire auto-fill from the stored per-tenant knowledge base via RAG. The "single private knowledge base per customer that grows with every answer" is, concretely, per-tenant rows + per-tenant embeddings; this is normal RAG, not a research problem. NO model fine-tuning is required at launch and none should be attempted early.
- The grading "rubric" itself (the 110 control objectives, the SPRS point weights, mapping logic) is authored content, not learned — it should live as version-controlled structured data/prompts curated by the founders + DoD/DIB advisor, with the LLM doing extraction and matching against it, not inventing the standard. This is the architectural key to keeping scores defensible.
- Integrations: Stripe (billing), an email/notification service (Postmark/SendGrid) for re-assessment reminders, an auth provider (see below). Notably there is NO required integration into SPRS itself (SPRS has no public write API for this; the customer enters their own score) and no integration into customer infrastructure — which dramatically shrinks the integration failure surface.
- Async / background: light. Scheduled re-assessment reminders, possibly queued LLM jobs for batch document grading on upload. A simple job runner (cron + a queue like Inngest/QStash, or even Postgres-backed jobs) suffices; no Kafka, no streaming, no heavy pipeline.
- Auth: off-the-shelf (Auth0/Clerk/Cognito/Supabase Auth) with email/password + TOTP MFA. Multi-tenant isolation enforced at the data layer (row-level tenancy) is where the security attention must go.
- Where complexity actually hides: (1) the LLM grading/auto-fill prompts and the evaluation harness that proves they're accurate enough to publish a number; (2) strict per-tenant data isolation; (3) keeping the 110-control content + SPRS methodology current as CMMC rules phase in (Nov 10, 2026) and as the standard moves from 800-171 r2 toward r3. None of these are research problems; all are disciplined-engineering and content-maintenance problems.

Estimate for one developer, with the second founder authoring compliance content/prompts in parallel: roughly 10–14 dev-weeks to a paid MVP. Chunks, in order:
1. Scaffolding + auth + multi-tenant Postgres + Stripe subscription + marketing/landing — ~2–3 weeks. Almost entirely boilerplate on the named stack (Next.js + Stripe + managed Postgres + Clerk/Auth0).
2. The free assessment engine: document upload, RAG over the per-tenant docs, LLM grading against the 110 control objectives, estimated SPRS score + plain-English gap list — ~3–4 weeks, and this is the technically load-bearing chunk. Most of the calendar risk lives here, not in the line count, because it's the accuracy harness, not the plumbing.
3. Paid remediation guidance: per-gap explanation + template library + step tracking to "done" + document storage — ~2–3 weeks. The template/worked-example library is content work (the second founder + advisor), not engineering; the app side is CRUD + workflow state.
4. Questionnaire auto-fill — ~2–3 weeks. Mechanically the same RAG-from-stored-answers pattern as chunk 2, plus a "draft to export" (PDF/DOCX) step. Genuinely punt-able to v2: the free score + paid remediation alone is a chargeable product; auto-fill is the retention sweetener and can ship a release later.
5. Recurring re-assessment + reminders — ~1 week. Scheduled jobs + email. Trivially punt-able to v2.
So a defensible revenue-earning MVP = chunks 1–3 (free assessment + paid remediation + storage), roughly 7–10 dev-weeks, with chunks 4–5 as fast-follows. That comfortably beats the "can't spend 12 months before revenue" constraint.

CRITICAL ASSUMPTIONS
1. ASSUMPTION: A frontier LLM with RAG can grade a small subcontractor's policy documents against the 110 NIST 800-171 control objectives and produce an estimated SPRS score that is consistently close enough to what a human assessor/RPO would conclude that customers trust it and don't get burned. If false (the score is materially wrong often enough), the free front door becomes a liability magnet and word-of-mouth turns negative. CHEAP VERIFY: assemble 15–25 real or realistic SSP/policy sets with known/expert-scored outcomes, run the grading prompt, and measure agreement against the advisor's manual scoring before building anything else. This is the single most important spike.
2. ASSUMPTION: The grading + remediation content can stay current through a moving target — CMMC Level 2 phasing in from Nov 10, 2026, plus the standard's drift from 800-171 r2 to r3 and SPRS methodology updates. If false, the product silently gives stale advice that costs a customer a PO. CHEAP VERIFY: confirm with the DoD/DIB advisor the realistic change cadence and the rev-control plan; price the content-maintenance hours into the model.
3. ASSUMPTION: Customers will accept "self-attestation, estimates/drafts, not an accredited assessor, no certification" and the legal disclaiming holds up when a customer's self-reported SPRS score is later disputed by a real C3PAO assessment. If false, the disclaimer doesn't shield against "your software told me I was a 95" claims. CHEAP VERIFY: counsel review of the attestation/ToS language and an explicit, in-product "you self-attest; we estimate" gate the user must acknowledge.
4. ASSUMPTION: The product can genuinely stay out of CUI. The risk is scope creep — owners will want to upload real evidence (actual configs, scan outputs, screenshots), and primes/auditors may ask for evidence the platform then holds. If the product drifts into storing CUI or controlled technical data, hosting, ITAR, and FedRAMP-equivalent obligations land hard and the bootstrap economics break. CHEAP VERIFY: define an explicit, enforced "no-CUI" upload policy + content scanning/guardrails on uploads, and confirm with the advisor whether SSP/POA&M text itself is ever treated as CUI in practice (it generally is not, but confirm).
5. ASSUMPTION: One frontier-model vendor on the critical path is acceptable risk. Model price/behavior/ToS can change, and grading behavior can drift between model versions. CHEAP VERIFY: pin model versions, keep prompts model-portable, and validate that a second provider (e.g., swap Claude<->GPT-4-class) reproduces grading within tolerance, so no single vendor is a hostage situation.

INFORMATION SECURITY POSTURE
What data is collected: company identity, user accounts, uploaded security POLICIES, derived control status, estimated SPRS scores, and questionnaire answers. By explicit design it does NOT hold the customer's CUI or operational telemetry (configs, logs, scans). This is the decisive scoping choice and it should be treated as a hard product invariant, not a guideline — because the moment real CUI enters, the regime jumps from "ordinary SaaS handling sensitive business data" to "system processing CUI," which pulls in NIST 800-171 / FedRAMP-Moderate-equivalent expectations and likely AWS GovCloud, and that is a different (much heavier, much costlier) business.

Threat model and sensitivity: the data is sensitive-but-not-classified. The realistic threats are (a) cross-tenant data leakage — one subcontractor seeing another's gaps/policies (the worst-case here is competitive/embarrassing and erodes the whole trust premise), (b) account takeover, (c) prompt-injection via uploaded documents causing the LLM to leak another tenant's context or emit bad guidance, and (d) the LLM vendor's data-handling/training-use of customer content. Note the delicious irony and the bar this sets: a product that grades others on NIST 800-171 must itself credibly meet a meaningful subset of those same controls, or it has no right to exist. The founders' SOC 2 Type II / HIPAA / GDPR operating background is a genuine asset here and de-risks this dimension.

Controls needed at launch: TLS 1.2+ in transit; encryption at rest (managed DB + S3 default KMS encryption); secrets in a managed secret store (not env files in the repo); MFA available and encouraged; strict row-level multi-tenant isolation with automated tests that assert no cross-tenant read; least-privilege access for the two founders with audit logging of admin/data access; a signed data-processing posture with the LLM vendor that contractually excludes customer data from model training (Anthropic/OpenAI enterprise/API terms support this — verify and pin it); upload guardrails (file-type limits, size limits, and ideally a "no-CUI" acknowledgment + lightweight content checks); and prompt-injection mitigations (treat uploaded document text as untrusted, isolate per-tenant retrieval, never let one tenant's content reach another's prompt). Incident readiness: a written IR runbook, backups with tested restore, and breach-notification language in the contract. SOC 2 implications: this product is SOC 2 Type II-attainable on this stack, and given the customer base's compliance-mindedness, the founders should expect to need a SOC 2 (or at least a credible security whitepaper + the controls above) earlier than a typical consumer SaaS — fitting, since they've run SOC 2 programs before. None of this requires GovCloud or FedRAMP at launch precisely because CUI is out of scope.

TOP 3 TECHNICAL STRENGTHS
- Deliberately scoping CUI and operational telemetry OUT of the system, holding only compliance documentation — this single decision keeps the product on ordinary commercial SaaS hosting instead of GovCloud/FedRAMP, and is the difference between a bootstrappable build and a regulated-infra slog.
- A fixed, knowable problem domain (110 NIST 800-171A control objectives + a published SPRS scoring formula) that the founders can curate as version-controlled content, with the LLM doing extraction/matching against it rather than inventing the standard — this is what makes the scores defensible and the build tractable.
- An almost entirely off-the-shelf stack with a thin custom wedge (per-tenant RAG + curated rubric), a ~7–10 dev-week path to revenue, and founders who have actually run SOC 2 Type II / HIPAA / GDPR programs — rare alignment of build-ability, security literacy, and bootstrap timeline.

TOP 3 TECHNICAL RISKS
- LLM grading/scoring accuracy: a confidently wrong free SPRS estimate or a flawed generated policy in a forced-consequence domain damages trust and invites liability; the whole funnel rests on this being demonstrably "close enough."
- Scope-creep into CUI: customer and prime pressure to upload real evidence (configs, scans, screenshots) could drag the platform into holding controlled data, which would detonate the lean hosting model and pull in ITAR/FedRAMP-equivalent obligations.
- Content-currency maintenance: the standard and SPRS methodology are moving (CMMC phase-in Nov 10 2026; 800-171 r2→r3), so stale grading logic can silently give advice that costs a customer a purchase order — a permanent operations burden distinct from app maintenance.

BIGGEST SINGLE RISK
The single most likely thing to break the business is the accuracy-and-liability problem at the LLM grading layer. The product's entire acquisition engine is a free, self-serve, plain-English SPRS score, and its core paid value is AI-generated remediation guidance and policy text — all in a domain where the buyer faces a fast, concrete consequence (losing work with their prime) if the guidance is wrong. If the model's estimated score is materially off, or it generates a policy that reads correct but fails a real C3PAO assessment, the damage is two-fold: reputationally, a non-technical owner who relied on a "95" and then fails feels actively misled and tells the tight-knit DIB community; legally, "estimates/drafts/we're-not-an-accredited-assessor" disclaimers reduce but do not erase exposure when a customer's lost contract traces to the software's output. This is not a reason for no-go — it is precisely measurable and cheaply de-riskable before a line of production code (run real/realistic policy sets through the grading prompt and measure agreement against the DoD/DIB advisor's manual scoring; set a publish-quality accuracy bar; gate the free-score feature on clearing it; and keep a human-reviewable "show your work" trail tying every score and template to the specific control objective). But it must be confronted head-on as engineering-plus-content discipline, not waved away as "the AI handles it."

QUESTIONS THE FOUNDERS MUST ANSWER BEFORE I'M COMFORTABLE
- What measured accuracy bar must the LLM grading hit against expert-scored test cases before you publish a free SPRS number, and how will you build and maintain that evaluation harness as models and the standard change?
- How will you contractually and technically guarantee customer policy documents are excluded from LLM-vendor model training, and which named provider terms (Anthropic/OpenAI enterprise/API) actually deliver that today?
- Where exactly is the CUI line drawn in-product, and what enforced guardrails (upload policy, content checks, acknowledgments) stop owners or primes from pushing real controlled evidence into a platform you've designed NOT to hold it?
- What is the rev-control and update cadence for the 110-control grading logic, templates, and SPRS methodology as CMMC Level 2 phases in (Nov 10, 2026) and 800-171 moves r2→r3 — and who owns that content upkeep?
- How is strict multi-tenant isolation enforced and tested (so no subcontractor can ever read another's gaps/policies), and what prompt-injection defenses treat uploaded document text as untrusted?

RECOMMENDATION: GO
On purely technical and security grounds this is a clean, bootstrappable build: a proven off-the-shelf stack, a thin and well-chosen custom wedge, a ~7–10 dev-week path to revenue that respects the "no 12-month build" constraint, founders with real compliance-security operating experience, and — most importantly — an architecture that deliberately keeps CUI out of scope, which is the decision that makes the whole thing financeable without GovCloud/FedRAMP. The genuine risk is not feasibility but the accuracy and liability of LLM-generated scores/policy in a forced-consequence domain, plus the discipline to never let the product drift into holding CUI and to keep the compliance content current. Those are de-risking experiments, not blockers: run the grading-accuracy spike (15–25 expert-scored policy sets) and the counsel review of the attestation/disclaimer language before full build, hold the no-CUI invariant as a hard architectural rule, and budget ongoing content-maintenance hours. Do those, and the technology is a GO.
