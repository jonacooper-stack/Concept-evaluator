# COO REVIEW — Self-Serve CMMC / NIST 800-171 Compliance Software for Small Defense Subcontractors

ONE-PARAGRAPH OPERATIONS READ
Operationally this is close to an ideal two-founder shape: a self-serve, product-led SaaS where the "unit of work" is software running against a customer's documents, not a human delivering a service per account. The repeated work that scales with revenue — grading 110 control objectives, generating remediation guidance, auto-filling questionnaires, re-scoring — is done by code and an AI layer, not by the founders showing up. There is no truck, no install, no on-site visit, no per-customer human bottleneck at the core, which means two founders can in principle carry hundreds to low-thousands of paying accounts. The real operational risk is NOT capacity — it is that "self-serve" is a load-bearing assumption for a non-technical, cost-sensitive, first-time-CMMC owner who is exactly the customer most likely to get stuck and email for help, and the content/knowledge-base accuracy burden (110 controls, evolving rules) is a recurring operational cost the founders own personally because they hold no CMMC credential.

THE TUESDAY-IN-MARCH WALKTHROUGH
It is Tuesday, March 2027 (CMMC L2 has been phasing into contracts since November 2026, so the pain is present-tense). The business is ~14 months live. Say there are **420 paying subscribers** at a blended ~$375/mo (we'll test this number in the capacity math) plus a larger pool of free-assessment users who haven't converted. Here is the day:

- **Overnight / automated, no human:** ~30 free-assessment signups came in from SEO and an APEX Accelerator newsletter link. Each uploaded existing policies; the system graded them against the 110 NIST 800-171 objectives, returned an estimated SPRS score and a plain-English gap list. Zero founder time — this is the funnel running itself. Of those 30, maybe 3–6 will convert to paid over the following weeks.
- **Founder A (product/eng), morning:** Triage of the support inbox. At 420 accounts, realistically **15–30 tickets/day** arrive (we model this below). The pattern is predictable: "I adopted your access-control policy template but I don't understand what MFA my IT guy needs to turn on," "the questionnaire from Lockheed asks something your auto-fill didn't cover," "is my SPRS score of 68 good enough to keep my PO?" Founder A clears the technical/product ones and routes the genuinely compliance-judgment ones to a saved-reply library and the advisor-reviewed content. Spends ~2.5 hours here.
- **Founder A, midday:** Ships a content/template update. A prime (say a Tier-1 aerospace prime) changed the wording on its flow-down questionnaire; three customers flagged it. Founder A updates the questionnaire-mapping logic and the auto-fill knowledge base so all 420 accounts benefit. This is the recurring "content is the product" maintenance load — it never goes to zero because the rules and prime questionnaires drift.
- **Founder B (marketing/GTM), morning:** Publishes the week's SEO content ("What a stale SPRS score does to your eligibility for new POs"), reviews the free-assessment-to-paid conversion funnel, and emails two MEP (Manufacturing Extension Partnership) center contacts about a co-marketing webinar. No founder-led sales *close* — the published price and credit-card signup do the transacting — but Founder B is running the demand engine that fills the top of the funnel.
- **Founder B, afternoon:** Onboarding-success outreach to the ~5 accounts that signed up in the last week but haven't completed remediation steps (the activation/retention motion — the single biggest churn lever in self-serve compliance). A light-touch "you're 40% to a passing score, here's your next 3 steps" nudge. Semi-automated, founder-reviewed.
- **Where it breaks on this Tuesday:** A customer emails, alarmed, that their prime's third-party C3PAO assessor "rejected" something the software told them was fine. This is the recurring scary moment: the product issues *estimates and drafts*, the customer *self-attests*, and an accredited assessor may disagree. The founders must handle this calmly with crisp disclaimers, an accurate knowledge base, and a clear "we get you ready, the C3PAO certifies" boundary — because if this becomes a frequent pattern, it is both a churn event and a liability/trust event. No single Tuesday kills the business; a *month of Tuesdays* where the content is subtly wrong does.

Net: a normal Tuesday is two founders running a software product and a content/marketing engine — entirely inside their skill set, no field ops, no labor pool to recruit. The day is dominated by support triage + content upkeep + demand generation, all of which are tractable and largely leverage-able with tooling.

CAPACITY MATH (show the work)
The question that matters: at the income ladder, how many accounts, how many support events per week, and can two founders + at most a tiny team carry it?

**Step 1 — Accounts needed to hit the ladder.**
Blended ARPU [ASSUMED]: $375/mo = **$4,500/yr** (midpoint of the stated $200–$600/mo). Assume bootstrapped SaaS gross margin ~80% and modest opex; for founder *income* (not revenue), treat roughly 55–65% of revenue as distributable to two founders after infra, tooling, content/advisor cost, and marketing at steady state [ASSUMED — verify against actual COGS once infra cost per account is known].

- **~$300K/founder by mo 12 = ~$600K/yr to the two founders.** At ~60% pass-through that implies **~$1.0M ARR ≈ 222 paying accounts.**
- **~$500K/founder by mo 24 = ~$1.0M/yr to founders ≈ ~$1.67M ARR ≈ 370 paying accounts.**
- A healthier read uses the 420-account / $375 figure from the walkthrough = **~$1.9M ARR**, comfortably above the mo-24 rung.

So the *operational* target is on the order of **200–450 active subscriptions**, not thousands. That is the crux: this is a low-account-count, high-ARPU-per-SMB SaaS, which is exactly why two people can run it.

**Step 2 — Is the market deep enough to supply those accounts?** Reachable population [ASSUMED, verifiable]: the Defense Industrial Base has on the order of **~80,000+ companies expected to need CMMC**, with the small-sub segment (15–200 employees, handling CUI) numbering in the *tens of thousands*. Capturing **300–450 paying accounts is well under ~1%** of a credible serviceable base — operationally, supply of customers is not the constraint. (Verify segment size cheaply via SAM.gov registrant counts filtered by NAICS + size, and APEX/MEP client rosters.)

**Step 3 — Support load (the real throughput question).**
This customer is non-technical and doing CMMC for the first time, so ticket rate per account is the variable that decides staffing. Model three regimes per active paying account per month:

- Optimistic self-serve: **0.5 tickets/acct/mo**
- Base case: **1.5 tickets/acct/mo**
- Pessimistic ("they get stuck a lot"): **3.0 tickets/acct/mo**

At **420 accounts**:
- Optimistic: 210 tickets/mo ≈ **~10/day** (22 working days)
- Base: 630 tickets/mo ≈ **~29/day**
- Pessimistic: 1,260 tickets/mo ≈ **~57/day**

Time per ticket [ASSUMED]: ~12 min average for a maturing knowledge base / saved replies, blended across trivial and hard.
- Base case: 630 × 12 min = **126 hrs/mo ≈ ~5.7 hrs/day** of support → comfortably **one person part-to-full time**, or split across both founders with tooling.
- Pessimistic: 1,260 × 12 min = **252 hrs/mo ≈ ~11.5 hrs/day** → this is where you make the **first hire: one customer-success / compliance-support associate** (not a credentialed assessor — a sharp, trainable support rep with a compliance-curious mind), fully loaded **~$60–85K/yr**. One such hire absorbs the pessimistic regime entirely and is trivially affordable against ~$1.9M ARR.

**Step 4 — Content/knowledge-base upkeep (the other recurring labor line).**
This does not scale with account count — it scales with *rule and questionnaire drift*. Estimate **~6–12 hrs/week** of founder (or advisor-reviewed) content maintenance at steady state: updating the 110-control guidance, refreshing templates, adding prime-questionnaire mappings. This is a fixed, bounded load — one engineer-day to a day-and-a-half per week — and it is leverage-positive: one update improves all accounts. The DoD/DIB advisor de-risks accuracy; budget the advisor at a retainer (a few thousand $/mo) as a real, modeled cost.

**Step 5 — Staffing conclusion.**
- **Mo 12 (~220 accts):** Two founders, **zero employees**. Support is ~3–5 tickets/day base case; content upkeep is a few hours/week. Entirely founder-carryable.
- **Mo 24 (~370–450 accts):** Two founders **+ optionally one support/CS hire** if ticket rate runs hot. Bottleneck is support volume, not delivery — and it is solved with **one** affordable, non-credentialed, hireable-anywhere (remote) person.
- **Bottleneck location:** First constraint to bind is **support/activation labor**, not compute, not the founders' compliance judgment at the per-ticket level, and emphatically not field capacity. That is the best possible place for a two-founder SaaS bottleneck to land, because it is linear, cheap, remote, and hireable.

The math says two founders can carry this to the mo-24 rung, with at most one inexpensive support hire as insurance against a hot ticket regime. There is no version of this where you need a team of 50, trucks, or specialized labor in tight markets.

TOP 3 OPERATIONAL STRENGTHS
- **The unit of work is code, not a human visit.** Grading 110 fixed controls, generating templated remediation, and auto-filling questionnaires are software operations that scale with compute, not headcount — so 200–450 paying accounts (the whole income ladder) are carryable by two founders with at most one cheap support hire. This is the structural opposite of a field-ops model and the single best thing about the concept operationally.
- **Pristine logistics and supply chain.** Zero physical fulfillment, fully remote, national self-serve, no routing/shipping/installs; the only meaningful dependency (an LLM provider) is model-swappable and the knowledge base is the founders' own owned asset. Nothing to single-source, no MOQs, no deposits, no weather.
- **Bottleneck lands in the cheapest, most hireable place.** The first constraint to bind is linear support/activation labor, solvable with one ~$60–85K remote, non-credentialed associate — not a scarce assessor, not capital, not engineering scale. A two-founder team can see, model, and afford this bottleneck well before it bites.

TOP 3 OPERATIONAL RISKS
- **"Self-serve" is load-bearing for the *least* self-serve customer.** A non-technical, cost-sensitive, first-time owner is the profile most likely to get stuck and convert "guidance" into a support ticket. If the real ticket rate runs at the pessimistic ~3/acct/mo, support becomes ~11.5 hrs/day at 420 accounts — still solvable with a hire, but it erodes the lean-team thesis and can stall activation (and thus retention).
- **Content/knowledge-base accuracy is a permanent, founder-owned QC liability.** The product issues estimates/drafts against a 110-control standard with real consequences (a failed C3PAO assessment loses a customer their PO). The founders hold no CMMC credential; correctness rests on them + one advisor and must be maintained as rules and prime questionnaires drift indefinitely. A subtly-wrong knowledge base is a slow, reputational, churn-and-liability failure mode — the most dangerous kind because it isn't visible on any single Tuesday.
- **Activation/retention friction in self-serve compliance.** Getting a non-technical owner all the way "to done" (a defensible posture, a completed questionnaire) is the hard part; if they sign up, stall at 40% complete, and churn before value, the recurring-revenue spine weakens. This is an operational/customer-success challenge, not a delivery-capacity one — but it is the one most likely to quietly cap the business.

BIGGEST SINGLE RISK
The operational issue most likely to hurt this is not capacity — two founders can clearly run the software at the target account count — it is the **collision between a high-consequence compliance output and a self-serve, no-credential, do-it-yourself delivery model aimed at the least technical buyer in the market.** The product tells a non-technical owner what their SPRS score is and how to remediate, the owner self-attests, and at some point an accredited C3PAO assessor (or an angry prime) tests that attestation for real. If the knowledge base is even subtly wrong, or if owners systematically misapply correct guidance because no human checked their work, the failure surfaces as a lost purchase order for the customer and a trust/liability event for the company — and it surfaces *in clusters around assessment events*, not gradually, so the founders could be flying blind on quality right up until a wave of "your software was wrong" complaints hits. This is operationally dangerous precisely because it is invisible day-to-day: the support inbox can look healthy while the *correctness* of attestations the product enabled is quietly deteriorating. It is manageable — rigorous disclaimers, a tight "we ready you, the C3PAO certifies" scope boundary, a well-paid DoD/DIB advisor on a real retainer, sampling/QA of generated outputs against actual assessment results, and a fast content-update loop — but it requires the founders to treat knowledge-base accuracy and an outcomes-feedback loop as a core operational discipline from day one, not a content task they bolt on. If they do, this is an 8+ operation; if they treat content as set-and-forget, this is the thing that erodes the brand.

QUESTIONS THE FOUNDERS MUST ANSWER BEFORE I'M COMFORTABLE
- **What is the real support-ticket rate per active account per month**, and how much of it can a maturing knowledge base + saved replies + in-product guidance deflect? (Cheap test: run 25–50 free-assessment users through a manual-concierge version of the remediation flow and *count* the questions per user per week. This single number decides whether you need zero hires or one at the mo-24 rung.)
- **How do you assure and maintain knowledge-base accuracy across 110 controls and drifting prime questionnaires without a CMMC credential in-house** — what is the advisor's actual scope, retainer, and review cadence, and what is your QA loop that compares the product's outputs to real C3PAO assessment outcomes? (Without an outcomes feedback loop you cannot detect the silent failure mode.)
- **What is your activation definition and your plan to get a non-technical owner all the way to a defensible posture** — what percentage of paid signups reach "done," over what time, and what is the churn curve for those who stall at, say, 40% complete? (This is the retention/recurring-revenue question dressed as an ops question.)
- **At what account count and ticket regime does the first support/CS hire trigger, and have you written the runbook** so a $60–85K remote associate can absorb base-case support without founder escalation? (Define the trigger now so the bottleneck never surprises you.)
- **What is the per-account infrastructure + LLM cost**, and does it hold the ~80% gross margin the income-ladder math assumes as accounts grow and re-assessment/auto-fill usage rises? (Verify by metering token + storage cost on the first 50 accounts.)

RECOMMENDATION: GO
Operationally this is a strong, lean, remote-first, software-leveraged business that two founders can genuinely run to the income ladder — the work that scales with revenue is code, the logistics and supply chain are pristine, the only bottleneck is cheap and hireable support labor, and the model is the structural opposite of the field-ops core the founders rule out. It earns a high operational score on its merits, not on optimism: the capacity math closes with two founders and at most one inexpensive hire at the mo-24 rung. It is a GO rather than a REFINE because none of the risks are *operational-capacity* risks — they are *quality-assurance and customer-success* risks, which are addressable with disciplined process. If I were tightening it, I would name three operational disciplines as launch requirements, not afterthoughts: (1) a measured ticket-rate experiment before scaling marketing spend, (2) a formal knowledge-base accuracy/QA loop tied to a properly-retained DoD/DIB advisor and to real assessment outcomes, and (3) an explicit activation-to-"done" success motion with churn instrumentation. Get those three right and the lean-team thesis holds all the way up the ladder.
