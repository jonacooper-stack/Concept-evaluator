# LEGAL / REGULATORY REVIEW — Self-Serve CMMC / NIST 800-171 Compliance Software for Small Defense Subcontractors

*Standard disclaimer, stated once: I am not a licensed attorney and this is not formal legal advice. I am an experienced operator with strong regulatory pattern-recognition flagging the issues the founders must brief to real counsel before launch.*

ONE-PARAGRAPH RISK READ
This is a navigable, well-understood regulatory neighborhood that the founders are unusually well-suited to operate in — they have run SOC 2, HIPAA/HITECH, and GDPR/CCPA programs, which is exactly the compliance-operator muscle this product needs, and the concept is deliberately architected to dodge the two scariest landmines: it explicitly does NOT touch the customer's CUI or operational security data, and it explicitly does NOT certify, accredit, or assess (the customer self-attests; all outputs are labeled estimates/drafts). The real risk is not licensing and not "is CMMC regulated" — regulation here is the demand engine, not the threat. The real risk is the LIABILITY TAIL of being a "consultant-replacement" that a non-technical owner relies on to produce an SPRS score and an SSP/POA&M that ultimately feed a signed DFARS 7012 attestation and (post-Nov 2026) a real C3PAO assessment — and what happens to the platform's reputation and contracts when a customer who paid for guidance fails an assessment or, worse, a False-Claims-Act allegation lands on a sub that over-represented its score. That tail is real but it is contractual, disclaimable, and insurable, not catastrophic or uninsurable, and there is a clean precedent (Vanta, Drata, FutureFeed, Totem all sell readiness software without being accredited assessors). This earns a SERIOUS-BUT-MANAGEABLE rating that does NOT gate the tier — it is a due-diligence checklist, not a penalty.

RISK-GATE RATING: SERIOUS BUT MANAGEABLE
There is no FATAL element here: no unbridgeable license blocks a software product (you do NOT need to be an RP, RPO, or C3PAO to sell readiness software, and the concept correctly does not claim to certify), the liability tail is insurable with standard tech E&O / professional-liability coverage plus a properly drafted disclaimer-and-limitation-of-liability stack, and the data handled is deliberately scoped to compliance documentation rather than the customer's actual CUI. It rates SERIOUS-BUT-MANAGEABLE rather than MINOR for one reason only: the product positions itself as the thing a non-technical owner relies on to produce score representations and documents that feed a legally-binding government attestation (DFARS 7012) and, soon, an accredited third-party assessment — so the gap between "labeled estimate/draft" and "customer's actual signed obligation" is the one place a real lawyer must build the firewall before launch. That firewall is standard and buildable; nothing here approaches a tier-gating FATAL.

REGULATORY MAP
- **DFARS 252.204-7012 / 7019 / 7020 (DoD / DFARS clauses)** — These govern the customer, not the platform. 7012 requires safeguarding of Covered Defense Information / CUI and rapid incident reporting; 7019 requires a current NIST 800-171 self-assessment posted to SPRS; 7020 gives DoD assessment access and flows the requirement down to subs. The platform is a tool the customer uses to MEET these — it carries no direct DFARS obligation of its own UNLESS it begins to store the customer's CUI (which by design it does not). Burden on the platform: low, indirect — but it must be precise in marketing that the customer remains the responsible party and the attester. This is the demand engine, credited as forcing-function elsewhere; here it is simply the backdrop.
- **32 CFR Part 170 — the CMMC Program rule (final rule effective Dec 16, 2024) and 48 CFR / DFARS 252.204-7021 (the contractual CMMC clause, phasing into new CUI contracts beginning ~Nov 10, 2026)** — Level 2 requires assessment against the 110 NIST SP 800-171 (Rev. 2) controls, conducted for most CUI work by an accredited C3PAO. **This is the most important line on the map for the platform's positioning:** the program rule defines who may *assess and certify* (C3PAOs, accredited by the Cyber AB / under DoD's CMMC Accreditation Body), and it is unlawful for an unaccredited party to hold out as a certifying assessor. The product explicitly does NOT certify and issues no certification — correct and necessary. The platform is "readiness/remediation software," a category that lawfully exists alongside the accreditation regime. Burden: must NEVER blur into "we certify you" or "we are an assessor"; clear self-attestation framing keeps it clean.
- **CMMC ecosystem credentialing (Cyber AB / CMMC-AB roles: RP, RPO, CCP, CCA, C3PAO)** — These are credentials for *people and organizations who advise on or perform assessments*. They are NOT legally required to publish compliance software. The founders lacking RP/CCP is therefore NOT a licensing barrier to the product as scoped. It DOES have a marketing-claims consequence: the product cannot describe its guidance as delivered by "Registered Practitioners" or imply credentialed status it does not have. Burden: low, but a real line for advertising review (see FTC below).
- **FTC Act §5 / advertising-substantiation rules (FTC)** — The pitch ("become CMMC compliant yourself," "an estimated SPRS score," "replaces the consultant") makes performance and outcome claims to businesses. The FTC polices deceptive/unsubstantiated claims. "Estimated" and "self-attest, not a certification" labeling is the right instinct; the standing risk is an over-strong implied guarantee ("you WILL pass," "guaranteed compliant"). Burden: moderate, fully controllable with disciplined claim language.
- **FTC ROSCA / state automatic-renewal laws (CA ARL, the 2024 FTC "Click-to-Cancel"-style rules to the extent in force, NY, and others)** — This is a published, credit-card, recurring B2B subscription. Auto-renewal disclosure + easy-cancel + renewal-reminder obligations apply, and they vary by state. Burden: low IF built in from day one (clear pre-checkout disclosure, simple self-serve cancel, renewal emails). Standard SaaS hygiene; the founders have run subscription compliance before.
- **State data-privacy / data-security laws (CCPA/CPRA in CA; comparable laws in ~19 states) and contractual security flow-downs** — The platform stores business compliance documents and limited business-contact PII (owner name, email). It deliberately excludes CUI and operational security data, which is the single best risk decision in the design. Burden: low-to-moderate — it is a B2B tool holding business documents, not consumer or special-category data. The founders' GDPR/CCPA background covers this directly. NOTE: customers will still send DPA/security-questionnaire flow-downs of their own; the platform should expect to answer security questionnaires about *itself* and maintain its own SOC 2 — which the founders know how to do.
- **False Claims Act (31 U.S.C. §§3729–3733) — indirect but the most consequential backdrop** — DoD's cyber-DFARS regime is an active FCA enforcement theater (DOJ's Civil Cyber-Fraud Initiative; the Aerojet Rocketdyne and Penn State matters are the named precedents where contractors faced FCA exposure for cybersecurity misrepresentations). The platform never signs anything and is not the attester, so it is not a primary FCA defendant — but if its scoring or document generation contributes to a customer materially over-stating an SPRS score that a relator later challenges, the platform can be dragged in as a third party and will at minimum face reputational and contractual blowback. Burden: managed by disclaimers, "estimate/draft/you-attest" framing, and not auto-publishing scores to SPRS on the customer's behalf without the customer's explicit, informed action.
- **State unauthorized-practice-of-law (UPL) — minor, monitor** — Generating policy templates and "what this control means" guidance is squarely allowed self-help/legal-information territory (LegalZoom-style precedent), not legal advice. Keep it informational and template-based; do not give bespoke legal opinions. Burden: minimal with standard "not legal advice" framing.

LICENSING SUMMARY
For the product AS SCOPED, **no special government license is required to operate, and no CMMC credential is legally required to publish the software.** This is the confident headline. The founders need only the ordinary stack: a properly formed entity (LLC or C-corp) for liability shielding, a standard business license in their home jurisdiction, and clean clickwrap terms. They do NOT need to be a Registered Provider Organization (RPO), do NOT need Registered Practitioners (RP/RPI) on staff, and do NOT need C3PAO accreditation — those are required only to *advise as credentialed practitioners* or to *perform and issue the actual CMMC assessment/certification*, neither of which this product does. The named competitors prove the path: Vanta, Drata, FutureFeed, and Totem all sell CMMC/800-171 readiness and documentation software without being accredited assessors. The two real "licensing-adjacent" constraints are non-blocking: (1) the product must not market itself as a certifying/accredited assessor or imply credentialed staff it lacks; and (2) if the founders later add the optional "higher-touch help," that human-advisory layer is where RP/RPO/CCP credentialing (and its modest exam/registration cost, low four figures and a learnable exam — squarely bridgeable for these founders or a contracted credentialed advisor) becomes a "before-scale," not "before-launch," item. The senior DoD/DIB advisor already informing the methodology is the right scaffold for content credibility in the interim.

LIABILITY POSTURE
The entity's exposure is concentrated in one place: **professional-reliance / errors-and-omissions risk** — a customer pays for guidance, relies on the estimated SPRS score and the generated SSP/POA&M, then either fails a real C3PAO assessment, loses a purchase order, or (worst case) is accused of a false attestation, and points back at the software. This is a real, foreseeable tail, but it is the *ordinary* tail of every compliance-SaaS and readiness vendor, and it is handled by a three-layer stack the founders know how to build: (1) **contractual** — clickwrap terms with an explicit disclaimer that all scores/documents are estimates and drafts, that the customer alone is responsible for the accuracy of its SPRS posting and its DFARS attestation, that the product is not an accredited assessor and provides no certification or guarantee of passing, plus a limitation-of-liability cap (typically fees paid) and an as-is/no-warranty clause; (2) **insurance** — technology E&O / professional liability plus cyber liability, both readily available to B2B SaaS at modest premiums (low-to-mid four figures annually at this revenue scale), with the no-CUI design materially lowering the cyber premium because there is no high-value government data to breach; (3) **product design** — labeling outputs as estimates/drafts (already in the spec), and critically NOT auto-submitting an SPRS score on the customer's behalf without explicit informed action, which keeps the customer as the affirmative attester and the platform as a tool. Personal founder exposure is the standard pre-formation gap: until the entity is formed and respected (no commingling, proper capitalization, signed contracts in the entity's name), the founders carry personal liability — closed cheaply by forming the entity before the first paying customer. The leftover residual after all three layers is reputational (a customer who fails an assessment will be loud) and the small but nonzero chance a plaintiff or relator names the platform alongside a customer in an FCA matter — neither is uninsurable or catastrophic, and the no-CUI, no-certification, estimate-only architecture is precisely the posture that keeps it manageable.

TOP 3 LEGAL/REGULATORY STRENGTHS
- **The architecture pre-empts the two worst risks by design.** Explicitly excluding the customer's CUI/operational security data from the platform (it holds only compliance documentation) defuses the catastrophic data-breach/government-data tail, and explicitly NOT certifying/assessing (customer self-attests; outputs labeled estimates/drafts) keeps it on the lawful "readiness software" side of 32 CFR Part 170. These are the right calls and they are baked into the spec.
- **No license or credential gates the product, and there is a named, proven path.** You do not need to be an RP/RPO/C3PAO to publish readiness software, and Vanta, Drata, FutureFeed, and Totem demonstrate the category operates lawfully without accreditation — so the founders' lack of a CMMC credential is not a launch blocker.
- **Founder-regime fit is unusually high.** SOC 2 Type II, HIPAA/HITECH, and GDPR/CCPA operator experience maps almost one-to-one onto the compliance, privacy, auto-renewal, and security-questionnaire obligations this business actually faces — the regime is bridgeable because these specific founders have already bridged near-identical ones.

TOP 3 LEGAL/REGULATORY RISKS
- **Reliance/E&O tail with FCA adjacency.** A non-technical owner relies on the estimated score and generated SSP/POA&M, those feed a binding DFARS 7012 attestation and a future C3PAO assessment, and if the customer over-states posture or fails, the platform faces E&O claims and reputational/contractual blowback — with a small chance of being named alongside a customer in a DOJ Civil-Cyber-Fraud / False Claims Act matter.
- **Marketing-claims overreach.** The aggressive, outcome-oriented pitch ("become CMMC compliant yourself," "replaces the consultant," "estimated SPRS score") risks crossing into an implied guarantee of passing or an implied accredited/credentialed status the company does not hold, which is FTC §5 deceptive-claims and CMMC-program-rule territory.
- **Estimate-to-attestation gap mechanics.** If the product makes it too frictionless to push a generated score into SPRS or to treat a draft SSP as final, it blurs the line that keeps the customer (not the platform) as the responsible attester — the single mechanic most likely to convert a manageable tail into a real one.

BIGGEST SINGLE RISK
The biggest single risk is the **professional-reliance / errors-and-omissions liability tail created by positioning the software as the thing that "replaces the consultant" for a non-technical owner who then relies on its estimated SPRS score and generated SSP/POA&M to fulfill a legally-binding government attestation under DFARS 252.204-7012 and, from November 2026, to pass an accredited C3PAO assessment.** When a customer fails an assessment, loses a purchase order, or is accused of a false attestation, the foreseeable human reaction is to point at the tool they paid to "do the consultant's job" — and because DoD cybersecurity compliance is now an active False Claims Act enforcement theater (the DOJ Civil Cyber-Fraud Initiative, with named precedents like Aerojet Rocketdyne and Penn State), the downside is not merely a refund dispute but potential entanglement in an FCA matter and meaningful reputational damage in a tight-knit DIB community. This is what moves the rating from MINOR to SERIOUS-BUT-MANAGEABLE. Crucially, it is NOT fatal and NOT uninsurable: it is the ordinary tail of every compliance-readiness vendor, and it is contained by the three-layer stack the founders already know how to build — disclaimers and a fees-paid liability cap in clickwrap terms, technology-E&O plus cyber-liability insurance at modest SaaS premiums (kept low by the deliberate no-CUI design), and product mechanics that keep the customer as the affirmative attester (estimate/draft labeling, and never auto-submitting an SPRS score on the customer's behalf without explicit informed action). The mandate for counsel before launch is to harden exactly this firewall.

QUESTIONS FOR REAL COUNSEL BEFORE LAUNCH
1. **Liability stack:** Draft clickwrap terms with (a) an explicit "estimates and drafts only / customer is solely responsible for SPRS accuracy and its DFARS attestation," (b) a no-certification / not-an-accredited-assessor disclaimer, (c) a limitation-of-liability cap (fees paid) and an as-is/no-warranty clause, and (d) a no-guarantee-of-passing clause — and confirm enforceability of these against B2B customers in the states where customers will sit.
2. **Marketing-claims review:** Have counsel (or a regulatory-marketing specialist) review the full claim set — "become CMMC compliant yourself," "replaces the consultant," "estimated SPRS score" — against FTC §5 substantiation standards and the CMMC program rule's restrictions on holding out as a certifying/accredited assessor, and set a green/yellow/red claim list for the marketing team.
3. **CMMC program-rule line:** Confirm precisely what a software product may and may not say and do under 32 CFR Part 170 without RPO/C3PAO accreditation, and whether any feature (e.g., auto-filling a prime's questionnaire, or pushing a score toward SPRS) edges into regulated "consulting" or "assessment" activity.
4. **FCA exposure mapping:** Assess the platform's third-party exposure under the False Claims Act / DOJ Civil Cyber-Fraud Initiative if a customer materially over-represents its score using platform outputs, and design the SPRS-submission UX so the customer — never the platform — performs and owns the affirmative attestation act.
5. **Insurance scoping:** Confirm technology-E&O and cyber-liability coverage limits/exclusions are appropriate for a compliance-guidance product in the defense supply chain, and verify the no-CUI design is reflected (and credited) in the underwriting.
6. **Auto-renewal / privacy compliance:** Confirm the subscription checkout, cancellation flow, and renewal notices satisfy FTC ROSCA and the relevant state auto-renewal laws (CA ARL and peers), and confirm the privacy posture under CCPA/CPRA given the business-contact PII and stored documents — plus the platform's own security posture (likely SOC 2) for answering customers' inbound flow-down questionnaires.
7. **AI guidance layer:** Review the model/API provider's terms for any restriction on regulated-advice use cases and confirm output-IP ownership and the right to commercialize generated templates/answers, and confirm no competitor templates or copyrighted content are being ingested or reproduced.
