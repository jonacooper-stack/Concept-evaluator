CTO REVIEW — CMMC Level 2 Certification Lifecycle Platform ("get certified, stay certified")

ONE-PARAGRAPH TECHNICAL READ

This is a fundamentally buildable concept for two software-capable founders: at its core it is a structured-content + workflow + document-generation SaaS — a guided questionnaire engine over a fixed, public, knowable rubric (NIST SP 800-171 Rev. 2's 110 controls, the basis of CMMC Level 2), with templated document assembly (SSP, POA&M), an evidence-vault, and a partner-matching CRM bolted on. None of the hard parts are research problems; the genuine engineering risk is low and the stack is almost entirely off-the-shelf (Next.js + Postgres + Stripe + an object store + a managed auth provider). The real difficulty is NOT technical, it is content-and-correctness: the moat and the danger both live in the accuracy of the 110-control-to-evidence mapping and the trustworthiness of any AI-generated guidance/documents — get that wrong and you ship a liability, not a product. The two consciously good architectural decisions here (do NOT store CUI; stay on the prep side of the C3PAO independence line) materially shrink the security blast radius and make this a SOC 2-attainable build rather than a FedRAMP/CMMC-on-yourself nightmare — though "we don't store CUI" must be enforced by design and policy, not just asserted in a one-pager.

ARCHITECTURE SKETCH

- Frontend: A single React/Next.js web app (hosted on Vercel or AWS Amplify) — a guided wizard UI walking a non-technical owner through the 110 controls one assessment-objective at a time, plus dashboards (readiness %, gap list, evidence-collection status, affirmation calendar). No mobile app needed at launch. This is the bulk of the UX work and is standard CRUD-with-state.
- Backend: A conventional API server (Node/TypeScript or Python/Django — founders' choice) on managed compute (AWS ECS/Fargate, Render, or Fly.io). Business logic is a deterministic rules/scoring engine over a structured "control model": each of the 110 controls (really ~320 assessment objectives in NIST 800-171A) is a data record with required-evidence definitions, question prompts, status, and links to artifacts. The single most important engineering artifact is this content model — it is data + config, not exotic code.
- Data store: Postgres (managed — RDS/Supabase/Neon) for all structured state: orgs, users, control status, answers, POA&M items, affirmation dates, assessor records, referral pipeline. An object store (S3 with SSE-KMS) for uploaded evidence artifacts (policies, screenshots, config exports) and generated PDFs/Word docs.
- Document generation: Templated SSP/POA&M assembly. Off-the-shelf: a templating engine (e.g., docxtemplater for Word, or HTML→PDF via a service like DocRaptor/Puppeteer). This is deterministic merge-field work, NOT generative AI — and it should stay that way for the legal artifacts of record.
- AI/LLM layer (bounded, optional): An LLM (OpenAI/Anthropic API) used ONLY for plain-English explanation, draft policy prose, and "translate this control into a question an owner understands" — never as the authority on pass/fail and never as the unattended author of the legal record. Retrieval-augmented over the firm's own curated, advisor-reviewed control library so outputs are grounded, not free-associated. This is the highest-judgment, highest-liability component and must be sandboxed behind human-reviewed templates.
- Integrations: Stripe (billing); a managed auth provider (Auth0/Clerk/AWS Cognito) for SSO/MFA; transactional email (Postmark/SES); optionally a help-desk and a lightweight CRM (or just Postgres) for the assessor-partner pipeline. The flow-down questionnaire auto-answer feature (v2) is internal logic over stored evidence, not a third-party dependency. No integration sits on the critical path of "can the customer use the product today."
- Async/background: Minimal — scheduled jobs (a cron/worker like BullMQ, Celery, or a managed scheduler) for affirmation reminders, "drift" nudges, and renewal-prep triggers. This is a calendar-and-notifications engine, the easiest async pattern there is.
- Auth/hosting/security: Managed auth with mandatory MFA, role-based access (owner / IT-provider / read-only), TLS everywhere, KMS-managed encryption at rest, audit logging from day one. US-region hosting only. The platform's customers are defense contractors, so even though no CUI is stored, buyers will expect a credible security story (see INFOSEC POSTURE).

Where the real complexity hides: NOT in the code. It hides in (1) the correctness and assessor-defensibility of the control-to-evidence content model — this is a knowledge-engineering effort, not a coding effort; and (2) the legal framing and reliability of AI-generated guidance and documents. The software is a 6–10 week build; the content is the multi-month, advisor-dependent work, and is the actual product.

BUILD PLAN TO REVENUE-EARNING MVP

Estimate: ~10–14 dev-weeks of one developer's time to a revenue-earning MVP, with the binding constraint being content authoring (the 110-control model with assessor-expected evidence), which runs in parallel and is gated on the DoD/DIB advisor's time, not the founders' coding speed. [ASSUMED] range 10–16 weeks for software; verify by timeboxing the content model for 10 controls first (below).

In order:
1. The control content model + readiness/gap engine (core wedge, ~3–4 weeks software, plus heavy parallel content work). Encode the controls/objectives as structured records with question prompts, scoring logic, and "evidence-expected" definitions. Ship a working gap analysis and prioritized remediation list. This is the irreducible core — without a defensible control model there is no product.
2. Guided remediation + evidence vault (~2–3 weeks). Per-control walkthrough UI, file upload to S3, evidence-status tracking, control-by-control evidence package assembly. This is the "core" stage the one-pager names and the thing that differentiates from "documentation tools that stop at the score."
3. SSP + POA&M document generation (~1.5–2.5 weeks). Deterministic templated assembly into Word/PDF, clearly watermarked "draft/estimate, not an accredited assessment." High customer-perceived value, modest engineering.
4. Auth, billing, accounts, security baseline (~2–3 weeks). Managed auth + MFA, Stripe subscription tiers, org/user model, encryption + audit logging wired in from the start (not retrofitted).
5. Assessor matching/handoff + referral tracking (~1–2 weeks; partially punt to v2). MVP can be a simple curated assessor directory + a "request a referral" handoff that emails the organized evidence package. The two-sided network and revenue-share accounting are a v2 concern.

Punt to v2 without blocking revenue: the flow-down-questionnaire auto-answer engine; "drift" monitoring automation; the full two-sided assessor marketplace with revenue-share reconciliation; any deep AI policy-drafting beyond bounded templates. The recurring "stay-certified" engine is the long-term value but the FIRST dollar comes from readiness + evidence + documents, which a contractor facing a Nov-2026-phased deadline will pay for now. Critically: the MVP earns revenue the moment stages 1–4 are live and one control library is trustworthy enough to sell; you do not need the full lifecycle to transact.

CRITICAL ASSUMPTIONS

1. The 110 controls (and their ~320 assessment objectives) can be mapped to assessor-expected evidence reliably enough as software that a contractor following the tool "walks in genuinely ready to pass." This is the load-bearing assumption of the entire business. [ASSUMED true and largely tractable] — the source material is public and stable (NIST SP 800-171 Rev. 2, the NIST 800-171A assessment guide, and CMMC scoping/assessment guidance), which is exactly what makes it encodable; but assessor judgment varies, and "evidence sufficiency" is partly subjective. Cheap verification: with the DoD/DIB advisor, fully author 8–10 representative controls end-to-end (question → required evidence → sample artifact → how an assessor scores it), then put that mini-package in front of 2–3 independent C3PAO assessors and ask "would this pass your review?" Cost: advisor hours + a few assessor conversations. This single experiment de-risks the whole thesis.

2. AI-generated guidance and documents can be made reliable AND legally safe. If the product lets an LLM author the SSP/POA&M or adjudicate pass/fail unattended, hallucination becomes a direct liability (a firm fails an assessment, loses a contract, blames the tool). [ASSUMED manageable IF AI is bounded] — the mitigation is architectural: deterministic templates for documents of record, LLM only for explanation/first-draft prose with mandatory human acceptance, retrieval grounded in the advisor-curated library, and clear "estimate/draft, not an accredited assessment" labeling throughout. Cheap verification: have the advisor red-team 25–50 AI-explained controls for accuracy and "would this mislead an owner?"; measure error rate before exposing it to customers.

3. "We store evidence-of-control, not CUI" holds in practice. The risk is that customers upload artifacts that incidentally contain CUI (a screenshot, a config export, a policy referencing a controlled program). If that happens, the platform silently becomes a CUI store and the whole security posture (and the deliberate avoidance of FedRAMP-equivalent obligations) is invalidated. [ASSUMED enforceable with controls] — needs upload guidance, scanning/DLP heuristics, contractual prohibition, and possibly a redaction workflow. Cheap verification: interview 3–5 target contractors / a friendly C3PAO on what evidence artifacts actually look like in a real assessment and how often they contain CUI; confirm the "no-CUI" line is operationally real, not aspirational.

4. The assessor-partner network is achievable and assessors will accept/pay for pre-qualified, evidence-organized referrals. The second revenue stream and a core part of the wedge depend on ~scarce accredited assessors agreeing to a referral/handoff relationship. [ASSUMED plausible given assessor scarcity favors a demand-pipeline partner] — but it is a business-development assumption, not a technical one, and the platform shouldn't be architected to depend on it for the primary subscription revenue. Cheap verification: get 2–3 signed (even informal) partner LOIs from C3PAOs before building the marketplace mechanics.

5. The regulatory content stays stable enough that maintenance is bounded. CMMC rulemaking and 800-171 revisions (e.g., movement toward Rev. 3) mean the control set can shift. [ASSUMED bounded, ~quarterly content review] — the architecture must treat the control model as versioned data so a rule change is a content update, not a code rewrite. Cheap verification: confirm with the advisor the realistic cadence and magnitude of expected changes through 2027.

INFORMATION SECURITY POSTURE

What data is collected: organizational compliance metadata (control status, answers, scores), uploaded evidence artifacts (policies, procedures, screenshots, config exports, training records), generated SSP/POA&M documents, assessor/referral records, and standard account PII (names, emails, company info, billing via Stripe). Explicitly NOT collected by design: the customers' actual CUI / controlled technical data. This single design choice is the most important security decision in the concept and it is correct — it keeps the platform out of the FedRAMP-Moderate-equivalent / DFARS 252.204-7012 cloud-storage-of-CUI obligation that would otherwise be crippling for a two-founder bootstrap.

Where it lives: US-region cloud only (data residency matters to this buyer). Postgres (managed, encrypted at rest with KMS), S3 (SSE-KMS) for artifacts and documents, Stripe for card data (never touch raw PAN).

Threat model: (a) the obvious one — a breach of stored "evidence of control" is embarrassing and a reputational killer for a vendor selling security readiness to defense firms, even though the data isn't classified; (b) the sneaky one — inadvertent CUI ingestion turning the platform into a CUI store it isn't authorized to be; (c) account takeover of an owner account exposing a firm's complete security-gap inventory — effectively a roadmap of that contractor's weaknesses, which is sensitive even if not CUI; (d) supply-chain/dependency risk in the document-gen and LLM path.

Controls needed at launch (non-negotiable, because the buyer is a security-conscious contractor): mandatory MFA, managed auth with strong session handling, RBAC (owner / IT-provider / read-only), TLS 1.2+ everywhere, encryption at rest via KMS with documented key management, comprehensive audit logging (who saw/changed what control and artifact), least-privilege IAM, automated backups with tested restore, dependency scanning, and a written incident-response plan. US-region pinning and a clear data-handling/no-CUI policy surfaced in the upload UI. A vulnerability-disclosure path.

Controls at scale: formal SOC 2 Type II (the founders have run SOC 2 Type II before — this is a genuine, evidenced strength and makes the security story credible to buyers and a real differentiator versus sleepy incumbents); DLP/scanning to detect inadvertent CUI uploads; SIEM/log aggregation; pen testing; vendor risk management for the LLM and doc-gen providers; possibly customer-managed-key options for larger clients.

PII and incident readiness: PII is low-volume and ordinary (contact + billing); Stripe offloads card data. The reportable-incident posture is meaningfully eased BY the no-CUI design — but the founders must verify they don't trip CUI-handling obligations the moment a customer uploads the wrong file, and they should have breach-notification language and an IR runbook from day one given the customer base. SOC 2 attainability: high and credibly so, given founder experience — this is one of the strongest parts of the concept.

7. Data architecture quality: 8 — Simple, owned, portable relational + object model; the deliberate no-CUI decision keeps the data scope tight and the liability surface small, and the stored compliance content is itself a switching-cost moat (the evidence library that auto-answers future questionnaires lives in the platform). 8. Information security posture: 8 — Security is designed in from day one with named controls (MFA, KMS-at-rest, RBAC, audit logging, US-region), the no-CUI design slashes the regulatory blast radius, and the founders have run SOC 2 Type II before — a concrete, evidenced capability that makes the audit story credible to this exact buyer. Held below 9 by the genuine inadvertent-CUI risk and the fact that the founders are explicitly not deep security engineers.

9. Scaling headroom: 9 — Load is trivial: a few thousand contractors, modest per-org data, document generation and notifications that parallelize easily on managed infra; nothing here strains Postgres/S3 within 100x of the realistic customer base, and there is no rewrite on the horizon.

Maintenance burden: 7 — The software largely runs itself, but the product is a living regulatory artifact: the control content must be kept current with CMMC/800-171 rulemaking (a recurring, advisor-dependent content task), and any AI-guidance surface needs ongoing accuracy monitoring. Good, not great, because correctness maintenance never fully ends.

AVERAGE SCORE: 8.0 / 10

TOP 3 TECHNICAL STRENGTHS
- The wedge is a thin custom layer over a finite, public, stable spec on an entirely off-the-shelf stack — low engineering risk, fast to a revenue-earning MVP (~10–14 dev-weeks), and squarely inside two software-capable founders' reach.
- The two deliberate design choices — store evidence-of-control NOT CUI, and stay on the prep side of the C3PAO independence line — are exactly right and collapse what could have been a FedRAMP/CMMC-on-yourself security burden into a tractable, SOC 2-attainable posture.
- Founder fit on security/compliance is real and evidenced (hands-on SOC 2 Type II, HIPAA/HITECH, GDPR/CCPA program experience), so the security story and the control-content quality are bridgeable rather than aspirational, and credible to the exact buyer.

TOP 3 TECHNICAL RISKS
- Content correctness and assessor-defensibility: the value is only as good as the 110-control-to-evidence mapping; if a tool-prepared firm fails its assessment, the product's core promise breaks and so does trust.
- AI-generated guidance/document liability: an LLM that hallucinates pass/fail or authors a flawed SSP/POA&M unattended converts a feature into a direct liability against high-stakes contract revenue.
- Inadvertent CUI ingestion: customers uploading the wrong artifact can silently turn a "no-CUI" platform into a CUI store, invalidating the security posture and triggering obligations the architecture was specifically designed to avoid.

BIGGEST SINGLE RISK

The single most dangerous issue is the correctness-and-liability coupling between the control-to-evidence content model and any AI-generated guidance or documents. Technically this product is easy to build; that is precisely the trap, because the easy-to-build part is not where the business lives. The business lives in a promise — "follow our tool and you will walk into a real, accredited assessment genuinely ready to pass" — made to a customer for whom failure is not a bad UX moment but the loss of live, revenue-bearing DoD contracts. If the encoded evidence expectations are subtly wrong for even a handful of the 110 controls, or if an LLM-generated explanation quietly misleads a non-technical owner, or if a templated SSP/POA&M is treated by the customer as authoritative when it is in fact a draft estimate, then the product's first high-profile assessment failure becomes both a reputational kill-shot and a plausible liability claim ("your software told me I was ready"). This is manageable — bound the AI to explanation and human-accepted drafts, keep documents of record deterministic and watermarked as estimates, ground everything in an advisor-curated library, and validate the mapping against real C3PAO assessors before selling — but it must be designed and contractually framed correctly from day one. It is the rare case where the security/correctness posture, not the architecture, is the thing most likely to break the business after launch.

QUESTIONS THE FOUNDERS MUST ANSWER BEFORE I'M COMFORTABLE
- For the AI/document layer: exactly which outputs will be deterministic templates versus LLM-generated, and what is the human-in-the-loop and disclaimer/liability framing for anything the customer might treat as authoritative? (I want a hard line that no legal document of record and no pass/fail call is authored unattended by an LLM.)
- On content correctness: who authors and signs off on the 110-control-to-evidence mapping, how is the advisor's CMMC/800-171 currency kept fresh, and have you validated even a 10-control slice with 2–3 independent C3PAO assessors who would say "yes, this passes"?
- On the no-CUI boundary: how will you technically and contractually prevent or detect inadvertent CUI in uploaded evidence (DLP/scanning, upload guidance, redaction), and at what point would a customer's behavior force you into CUI-handling obligations you've designed to avoid?
- (Bonus) On content versioning: is the control model built as versioned data so a CMMC/800-171 rule change (e.g., Rev. 3 movement) is a content update rather than a code rewrite — and what cadence of regulatory change are you budgeting for?

RECOMMENDATION: GO

From a pure technical-feasibility, build-effort, and security standpoint this is a green light. The stack is boring and proven, the wedge is a thin custom layer over a public and finite spec, the time-to-revenue is short for two software-capable founders (~10–14 dev-weeks of software with content authoring running in parallel on advisor time), and the two key architectural decisions (no CUI; prep-side of the independence line) are exactly the choices that keep the security and regulatory burden tractable for a bootstrap. The founders' hands-on SOC 2 Type II background makes the security and content story credible rather than hand-wavy. The one place I require discipline before declaring victory is the correctness-and-liability axis: bound the AI to explanation and human-accepted drafts (deterministic, watermarked documents of record), validate the control-to-evidence mapping against real C3PAO assessors on a small slice before scaling content, and enforce the no-CUI boundary by design (upload guidance + scanning + contract), not by assertion. Do those de-risking spikes — they are cheap, mostly advisor-hours and a few assessor conversations — and the technical risk profile of this concept is genuinely strong.
