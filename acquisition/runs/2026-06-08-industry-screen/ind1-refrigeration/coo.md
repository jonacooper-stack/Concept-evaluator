COO REVIEW — Commercial Refrigeration Service

ONE-PARAGRAPH OPERATIONS READ
The operating model here is inherently systematizable: dispatch field service built on a backbone of recurring, scheduled preventive-maintenance (PM) routes plus urgent break-fix, which is exactly the work-shape that field-service software, scheduling/dispatch, and route density were designed to optimize. The founders' ops-modernization edge applies broadly because the industry is structurally manual (paper, whiteboards, no FSM), and the unit of work is a defined job at a known asset, not a bespoke craft project. The one genuine operating drag — and it is a TYPE-level trait, not a one-company issue — is that the binding constraint industry-wide is the supply and retention of EPA-608-certified refrigeration technicians, a skilled-trade labor pool that is tighter and ages faster than general HVAC or unskilled field labor.

THE TYPICAL OPERATING MODEL
A representative shop runs $1.5M–$4M revenue with a fleet of 3–10 service trucks, each manned by one EPA-608-certified refrigeration technician (sometimes a tech plus a helper/apprentice). The unit of work is a dispatched service call to a fixed asset — a walk-in cooler/freezer, a reach-in display case, a compressor rack, or an ice machine — at a grocery, c-store, restaurant, food processor, or cold-storage site. Revenue has two shapes: (1) contracted PM visits, scheduled and routed in advance, where a tech works a planned list of stops per day; and (2) emergency break-fix, which is unscheduled, urgent (spoiling inventory), and priced at high T&M/after-hours rates. A dispatcher/office staffer books calls, assigns techs, and handles invoicing; the owner typically runs sales, customer relationships, and scheduling personally. The day-to-day bottleneck is billable tech-hours in the field: trucks are the asset, certified techs are the scarce input, and dispatch efficiency (drive time vs. wrench time) is the lever that determines how many calls a given headcount can clear. Diagnostic skill and parts-on-truck completeness drive first-visit-fix rate, which in turn drives both customer satisfaction and tech productivity.

CAPACITY MATH (typical)
Take a representative tech. A working day is ~8 paid hours; in a manual shop, drive time, parts runs, paperwork, and dispatch slack commonly consume a large share, leaving perhaps ~4–5 billable wrench-hours/day [ASSUMED — verify with a target's job-time data]. At ~4.5 billable hours/day × 5 days × ~48 working weeks ≈ ~1,080 billable hours/tech/year. A 6-truck shop ≈ ~6,480 billable tech-hours/year. The binding bottleneck is certified-tech billable hours, not demand (emergency demand is non-discretionary) and not trucks (a truck is cheap relative to a tech). Modern dispatch/FSM that lifts billable utilization from ~4.5 to ~5.5 hours/day is a ~22% throughput gain on the SAME headcount — roughly +1,440 billable hours across that 6-truck shop with zero added techs. That is the core operating thesis: in a tech-constrained model, software that converts windshield time into wrench time drops almost entirely to capacity and margin. Growth beyond that requires adding certified techs (or growing apprentices into them) — which is the labor constraint, below.

AVERAGE SCORE: 6.9 / 10

TOP 3 OPERATIONAL STRENGTHS (of the type)
1. Steady, non-seasonal, non-discretionary demand — cold assets run 365 days and failures can't wait, so the work is recurring and urgency-priced rather than weather-bound or deferrable.
2. Clean modernization fit — a defined-job, route-plus-break-fix model maps directly onto off-the-shelf FSM (ServiceTitan/BuildOps) and IoT monitoring, so the founders' ops edge converts windshield time to wrench time and adds a recurring SKU.
3. Manageable as GMs with a real roll-up path — certification sits with employees (transferable), and the fragmented, consolidator-light sub-$5M field supports tuck-in growth on a shared dispatch backbone.

TOP 3 OPERATIONAL RISKS (of the type)
1. Scarce, aging EPA-608 certified-technician labor pool — the structural, industry-wide binding constraint on both delivery and growth.
2. Capacity is gated by certified-tech supply — software lifts utilization once, but scaling beyond that requires hiring/growing techs in a tight market.
3. Refrigerant-regulation transitions (e.g., HFC phase-downs / lower-GWP changeovers) add input-cost volatility and require ongoing tech retraining.

BIGGEST SINGLE RISK
The defining operating risk of this TYPE is the structural scarcity of EPA-608-certified refrigeration technicians. Unlike a route-density or unskilled-labor model where you can throw bodies at growth, this model's entire throughput is gated by a credentialed, skilled, and visibly aging trade pool, and the one-pager itself names technician recruiting and retention as the central industry-wide constraint. Modernization can wring a one-time ~20% throughput gain out of existing heads by converting drive time to wrench time, but every dollar of growth past that point depends on hiring or apprenticing more certified techs in a market where supply is tight and wages are rising. That makes the labor pool — not demand, not capital, not customers — the operating ceiling on this industry, and it is a TYPE-level trait that travels with every company in the space, which is why it caps the overall operating score below the strong band even though most other dimensions are good.

QUESTIONS TO ANSWER WHILE SOURCING IN THIS INDUSTRY
1. In the target metro, what is the realistic supply of EPA-608-certified refrigeration techs, the prevailing wage trend, and the typical time-to-hire — and does the target have an apprentice pipeline to grow its own?
2. What share of revenue is contracted PM (routed, predictable) vs. emergency break-fix (urgent, high-margin), and how dense/clustered is the account map for route optimization?
3. What is the current billable-hour utilization, first-visit-fix rate, and days-sales-outstanding baseline — i.e., how much real headroom does FSM/dispatch modernization actually unlock here?

RECOMMENDATION: MAYBE
The operating model is genuinely attractive — repeatable defined-job dispatch, steady non-seasonal essential demand, a clean off-the-shelf modernization path, GM-manageable with employee-held certifications, and a real tuck-in roll-up lane in a consolidator-light field. The single thing holding it out of an unqualified PURSUE on operating grounds is the structural scarcity of EPA-608 certified technicians, which is both the delivery bottleneck and the growth ceiling for the whole TYPE. The sub-segment that works best is denser metro/regional operators with a contracted-PM-heavy book and an existing apprentice/helper pipeline — i.e., shops where route clustering and an in-house tech-development engine blunt the labor constraint — rather than thin rural single-truck operations wholly dependent on a tiny local certified-tech pool.
