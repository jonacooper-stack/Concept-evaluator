# LEGAL / REGULATORY REVIEW — Commercial Refrigeration Service

(Not formal legal advice — these are issues to take to M&A counsel, a CPA, and an SBA lender.)

## ONE-PARAGRAPH RISK READ
Commercial refrigeration service is a clean, SBA-friendly trade-services type whose central
regulatory hook — EPA Section 608 refrigerant-handling certification under the Clean Air Act
— sits with individual technicians, not the owner, so the licensing structure is transferable
or hireable rather than a personal-credential trap for a non-tradesperson buyer. The dominant
legal/regulatory risks are not deal-killers at the industry level: a real but generally
manageable environmental exposure tied to refrigerant handling/venting (CAA §608 enforcement
and the AIM Act HFC phasedown), the ordinary trade-services liability and workers'-comp/wage-
hour profile of a W-2 field crew, and contractor-licensing rules that vary by state but
typically allow a qualifying employee. The biggest watch item is regulatory trajectory — the
AIM Act and EPA's Technology Transitions and refrigerant-management rules are actively
tightening — but that tightening forces recurring retrofit/service demand more than it
threatens the model. Net: a low-to-moderate-risk industry to hunt in.

## REGULATORY & SBA-ELIGIBILITY MAP (industry-level)
This is a for-profit, US-based, operating service business (NAICS 238220 mechanical
contractors / 811310 commercial equipment repair). It is squarely SBA 7(a)-eligible under SBA
SOP 50 10: it is not passive, not real-estate-holding, not lending/investment, not
speculative, not an ineligible type (no cannabis/gambling/MLM). Operating change-of-control
on an SBA acquisition is routine for this trade. The specific regimes that apply to the TYPE:

- **EPA Clean Air Act §608 (40 CFR Part 82, Subpart F)** — federal refrigerant-handling
  certification for technicians (Types I–IV/Universal), refrigerant recovery/recycling
  standards, a prohibition on knowing venting, recordkeeping, and leak-repair thresholds on
  larger appliances. Regulator: US EPA (with state delegation). Burden: moderate, routine, and
  borne at the technician/company level — it is the operating license of the trade.
- **AIM Act of 2020 (American Innovation and Manufacturing Act)** — EPA's HFC phasedown,
  allowance allocation, the Technology Transitions Rule (GWP limits driving the move to A2L
  refrigerants like R-454B/R-32), and the Refrigerant Management Rule (Subpart B) extending
  leak-repair/recovery requirements to HFCs. Regulator: US EPA. This is the live trajectory
  item — tightening, but demand-forcing.
- **State/local contractor & mechanical licensing** — most states require a refrigeration/
  mechanical or HVACR contractor license (e.g., Florida CILB Class A/B; Texas TDLR ACR;
  California CSLB C-20) typically satisfied via a qualifying individual/RMO/RME on staff, plus
  local trade permits and inspections.
- **OSHA** — general industry + refrigerant safety (PSM under 29 CFR 1910.119 applies only to
  large anhydrous-ammonia/industrial-refrigeration thresholds — relevant to ammonia cold-
  storage work, not typical fluorocarbon grocery/restaurant service).
- **DOT** — service trucks generally under CDL/40-hour thresholds; refrigerant cylinders are
  hazmat with materiality-of-quantity exceptions; modest burden.

## LICENSE & DEAL-TRANSFER NORMS
The trade's core federal credential (EPA §608) is **held by individual technicians and travels
with them** — it is not an owner credential and not a transfer obstacle; a buyer simply
retains/hires §608-certified techs. State contractor/mechanical licenses are typically
satisfied by a **qualifying employee / responsible managing officer or employee (RMO/RME)**,
so a non-tradesperson owner-operator can hold the company license through a qualified staffer
— the founder profile (manage the crew, don't perform the trade) fits cleanly. These trade
businesses **typically trade as asset deals**, which limits successor liability: the buyer
generally takes the assets and assumed contracts and leaves most pre-closing liabilities with
the seller's entity, subject to the usual exceptions (de facto merger, mere continuation,
fraudulent-transfer, and product-line/environmental successor theories) and to bulk-sales/tax-
clearance steps in some states. Customer relationships are largely **PM/service agreements and
repeat T&M**, which are usually assignable or short-term and re-signable; change-of-control
clauses exist on some national/chain accounts but are not the industry norm for the SMB book.
The main contract to scrutinize on any deal is the **commercial real-estate lease** (assignment
consent / change-of-control) for the shop/yard.

## WHAT I AM DEFERRING TO DUE DILIGENCE
- This particular seller's litigation history, tax liens, EPA/OSHA citation record, and any
  undisclosed environmental events (e.g., a documented venting violation or a contaminated yard
  → Phase I).
- This particular company's actual contracts: assignability and change-of-control terms on its
  largest PM/national accounts, and its shop/yard lease assignment terms.
- This particular company's labor file: W-2 vs 1099 classification of techs, I-9s, overtime/
  prevailing-wage exposure, and any union/CBA.

## TOP 3 LEGAL/REGULATORY STRENGTHS (of the type)
1. Clean SBA 7(a) eligibility — an operating service trade with no ineligible-category problem.
2. The binding credential (EPA CAA §608) lives with technicians and state licenses ride on a
   qualifying employee, so a non-tradesperson buyer faces no personal-credential trap.
3. Asset-deal norm plus simple B2B posture (no consumer PII/auto-renewal regime) keeps deal
   structure clean and the data/consumer-law surface near zero.

## TOP 3 LEGAL/REGULATORY RISKS (of the type)
1. Environmental exposure inherent to refrigerant handling — CAA §608 venting/leak-repair and
   HFC recovery, plus potential yard/site contamination.
2. Tightening regulatory trajectory under the AIM Act and EPA Technology Transitions Rule
   (A2L transition, GWP limits) raising compliance and retrofit complexity.
3. Wage-hour / classification exposure on a skilled, overtime-heavy W-2 field crew.

## BIGGEST SINGLE RISK
The single issue most worth watching is the **environmental-plus-trajectory bundle** around
refrigerants: the industry's entire reason to exist is handling regulated substances, and the
governing regimes are simultaneously the operating license (EPA CAA §608, 40 CFR Part 82) and a
moving target (the AIM Act HFC phasedown, EPA's Technology Transitions GWP limits forcing A2L
refrigerants such as R-454B, and the Refrigerant Management Rule extending leak/recovery duties
to HFCs). At the industry level this is genuinely double-edged and net-tolerable: a knowing-
venting violation or improper recovery carries CAA penalties and the tightening rules raise
compliance load, but the same mandates **force recurring demand** for compliant service,
recovery, retrofits, and A2L-ready equipment work — making the regulatory pressure a tailwind to
revenue durability rather than a threat to the model. It does not approach a deal-killing or
uninsurable tail for the TYPE; it simply means counsel and an environmental check matter on any
specific yard/site, and that the buyer must keep the crew current on A2L/§608 requirements.

## QUESTIONS FOR COUNSEL / DILIGENCE WHEN SOURCING IN THIS INDUSTRY
1. In each target state, exactly how is the mechanical/refrigeration contractor license held and
   transferred (qualifying-employee/RMO-RME rules, residency, experience, and the timeline to
   re-qualify if the licensed staffer departs post-close)?
2. What is the EPA CAA §608 and refrigerant-recordkeeping/leak-tracking compliance posture, and
   how exposed is a typical operator to AIM Act / Technology Transitions A2L-transition costs and
   to any historic venting/recovery violations?
3. Do the largest PM/service agreements and the shop/yard lease contain assignment or change-of-
   control restrictions, and is the deal cleanly structured as an asset purchase with the standard
   successor-liability carve-outs (and any state bulk-sales/tax-clearance step)?

## RECOMMENDATION: MAYBE
At the industry level this is a low-to-moderate legal/regulatory risk type — cleanly SBA-eligible,
no personal-credential trap, asset-deal norm, near-zero consumer/data surface — and on those axes
it is a comfortable place to hunt. It lands at MAYBE rather than a clear PURSUE because two real,
type-inherent drags hold the average down: the refrigerant environmental profile (CAA §608 +
possible yard contamination) and a genuinely tightening trajectory (AIM Act / EPA Technology
Transitions). The risk-reducing sub-segment to favor is **HFC/fluorocarbon grocery-and-restaurant
service that stays below OSHA PSM ammonia thresholds**, with a documented §608 compliance program,
a stable qualifying-employee license arrangement, and predominantly W-2 crews — which keeps the
environmental tail modest, avoids the heaviest industrial-refrigeration regime, and turns the
tightening rules into a demand tailwind instead of a liability.
