# INDUSTRY MEMO — Niche Industrial Distribution (Fasteners / Industrial Supply with VMI)

Date: 2026-06-08
Council reviewed: CFO, CMO, COO, Legal/Regulatory, CTO

## ONE-PARAGRAPH READ
This is a genuinely attractive TYPE on four of the five lenses: the customer shape is among
the stickiest in B2B (VMI bins physically embedded on the customer's production floor, the
model Fastenal built a multi-thousand-branch business on), the modernization gap is the
purest in the entire candidate set (phone/fax + paper catalogs + legacy/no ERP across nearly
the whole sub-$5M field), the operating model is cleanly systematizable with a named roll-up
path (Fastenal, Bunzl, DXP), and the legal profile is clean (no trade license, clearly
SBA-eligible, light regulatory burden). But the CFO finding is load-bearing and structural,
not company-specific: distribution traps cash in inventory (~$630K–$1.05M on the floor) and
AR, SBA deals frequently fund inventory-at-cost on top of the goodwill price, and the
representative-midpoint deal lands at ~1.21x base DSCR — below the 1.25x lender floor, ~0.97x
stressed — with year-1 owner earnings (~$205K) under the ladder. That working-capital drag is
true of the kind of business, so it holds the BROAD industry to MAYBE even though demand,
stickiness, and modernization are strong. The industry becomes a PURSUE only in a narrower
sub-segment (below).

## WHERE THE EXPERTS AGREE
- The modernization gap is the broadest and purest in the candidate set — fax/clipboard/paper
  default with a deep, named, off-the-shelf stack to close it (CMO, COO, CTO all independently
  rank it 8–9).
- Revenue is genuinely durable and embedded — VMI bin replenishment is recurring consumable
  reorder revenue with real switching costs; a missing fastener stops a customer's line (CFO
  8, CMO 9, COO).
- The operator fit is clean — no trade license, field work is warehouse/driver/VMI labor a GM
  can manage, founders' edge (purchasing/pricing/software) maps onto the owner's real job
  (COO 8, Legal 9).
- Working capital tied in inventory is a structural feature, not a company quirk — CFO, COO,
  and even the one-pager all name it explicitly. The disagreement is only about how
  load-bearing it is for the SCORE.

## WHERE THE EXPERTS DISAGREE
**The key disagreement — CFO (MAYBE, 6.0) vs. the other four (PURSUE, 7.3–7.7).** The
underlying assumption split: the four PURSUE reviewers score the demand/stickiness/
modernization attractiveness of the TYPE; the CFO scores the cash that attractiveness actually
converts to after the model's structural balance-sheet drag. Both are looking at the same
facts; they weight different ones.

**My load-bearing call on the CFO working-capital point: I weight the CFO heavier, and it
holds the broad industry to MAYBE.** Here is the reasoning, kept strictly industry-level:

- The drag is structural, not company-specific. The CFO's math rests on representative,
  clearly-`[ASSUMED]`-and-ranged industry benchmarks (NAICS 423840; ~3–5x turns; net-30/45
  B2B terms; SBA funding inventory-at-cost on top of goodwill). Every distributor of this kind
  ties up cash in inventory + AR, and the cash absorption gets WORSE with growth. That is the
  physics of distribution, exactly the kind of thing Stage 1 is supposed to score.
- It bites the two dimensions doc-01 weights most for financeability: Deal economics (15) and
  it caps Income-ladder headroom (10). ~1.21x base DSCR is below the 1.25x lender floor named
  in doc-01 itself; ~0.97x stressed means the type does not absorb a downturn at the midpoint.
- It is NOT a Stage-2 tell. The CFO correctly deferred the company-specific items
  (quality-of-earnings, dead-stock health, real turns by SKU, customer concentration) to the
  due-diligence box. What he scored is the TYPE's cash-conversion profile, which is fair game.
- The counter-argument (COO/CTO: modernization lifts turns and recovers margin, widening DSCR)
  is real but is upside the buyer must EARN post-close, not a property the industry hands you
  at the representative midpoint. We do not score the bar toward the optimistic end.

Net: demand and modernization are strong enough to keep this off PASS, but the structural
DSCR/working-capital drag is decisive enough to deny PURSUE for the broad industry. MAYBE.

Secondary disagreements (lower stakes): CMO rates competitive structure 7 (consolidator
tuck-in creep) while COO rates roll-up operability 8 (clean tuck-in path) — same consolidator
fact, opposite sign; I treat it as a wash, net neutral. CTO's modernization 9 vs.
implementation-effort 5 is an internal tension resolved in the red-team above.

## TOP 5 STRENGTHS OF THIS INDUSTRY (RANKED)
1. The purest, broadest pure-software modernization gap in the candidate set — phone/fax +
   paper + legacy/no ERP across nearly the whole sub-$5M field, with a deep named stack
   (NetSuite, Epicor P21, BigCommerce B2B, Zilliant) ready to close it.
2. Exceptionally sticky embedded revenue where VMI is deployed — bins on the customer floor,
   integrated par levels, reorder history; among the highest natural switching costs in B2B
   (Fastenal's proven model).
3. Clean operator fit and legal profile — no trade license, SBA-eligible operating wholesaler
   (NAICS 423840), light regulatory burden; the founders' purchasing/pricing/software edge IS
   the owner's job.
4. Low-cost, high-yield pricing lever — sleepy operators rarely re-price; a cost-plus matrix +
   annual increases recaptures margin on analyst time over existing ERP data (~$5K–$20K).
5. Named roll-up / tuck-in path — regional distributors consolidate cleanly onto one ERP and
   warehouse network (Fastenal, Bunzl, DXP grew this way), supporting a bolt-on growth and
   step-up-buyer story.

## TOP 5 RISKS OF THIS INDUSTRY (RANKED)
1. Structural working-capital absorption — inventory (~$630K–$1.05M) + AR trap cash that
   competes directly with SBA debt service, pulling the representative-midpoint deal to ~1.21x
   base / ~0.97x stressed DSCR (below floor). The defining financial weakness of the type.
2. Income-ladder shortfall at the midpoint — ~$205K/founder year 1, below the ~$300K rung;
   reaches the ladder only at the higher-SDE end or after modernization lifts turns/margin.
3. Consolidator/PE creep down-market — Fastenal/Grainger/Bunzl and PE-backed roll-ups push
   VMI/e-commerce expectations and tuck-in acquisitions toward smaller accounts over time.
4. The full tech upside is back-loaded — the highest-value unlock is a 6–12 month ERP
   migration with data-cleansing and pricing-tier-mapping risk, not a weekend SaaS swap.
5. Pass-through pricing and demand cyclicality — distributors are largely price-takers on
   commodity steel/fastener inputs (tariff-exposed), and demand softens with manufacturing
   capex in a downturn.

## THE BIGGEST OPEN QUESTION
The single industry-level unknown that most affects the PURSUE call is: **at what point in the
size/specialty band does the cash-conversion cycle stop dragging DSCR below the lender floor —
i.e., does a definable sub-segment (higher SDE, higher turns, specialty/spec'd SKUs with
pricing power) reliably clear ~1.25x+ base DSCR at sane multiples, or is the working-capital
drag pervasive enough that even the favorable end needs a large standby seller note to
finance?** This is answerable cheaply pre-deal: pull 5 closed NAICS 423840 comps and confirm
whether inventory is in the price or charged separately at cost, and pull RMA/IBISWorld turns
and gross-margin benchmarks for the $3M–$6M tier. The answer determines whether the MAYBE has
a real PURSUE sub-segment inside it or is a soft PASS.

## RECOMMENDATION: MAYBE
The broad industry does not clear the bar on honest, post-red-team scores: mean 7.2 (< 8.0),
lowest sub-score 3 (< 7), objectives 68.5 (< 80). The structural working-capital/DSCR drag —
an industry-level, not company-specific, finding — is load-bearing enough to deny PURSUE even
though demand, stickiness, and the modernization gap are genuinely strong. It is firmly off
PASS because four lenses are 7.3–7.7 with named evidence and the modernization upside is the
purest in the set.

### THE NARROWER SUB-SEGMENT THAT WOULD MAKE IT A PURSUE
**SUB-SEGMENT TO TARGET — Specialty / spec'd-SKU industrial distribution, high-SDE end,
VMI-embedded.**
- **The narrower definition:** distributors of **engineered / specified / non-commodity
  parts** (e.g., aerospace- or medical-grade fasteners, specialty fittings, critical low-cost
  SKUs where price is tiny vs. stop-the-line cost), at the **higher-SDE end (~$800K–$900K
  SDE)**, with a **genuinely VMI-embedded book** (bins on customer floors, not transactional
  will-call), **higher inventory turns**, and **demonstrable pricing power** — structured with
  a **substantial standby seller note** to thin serviced debt, and underwriting modernization
  (e-commerce reorder + dynamic pricing + turn analytics) as the explicit DSCR-widening lever.
- **Why it works where the broad industry doesn't:** specialty/spec'd SKUs carry real pricing
  power (not commodity pass-through), the national platforms compete least there, switching
  costs are highest, and the higher-SDE/higher-turn profile + standby seller note is what
  pushes the representative deal above the 1.25x DSCR floor and the year-1 income ladder — the
  exact two thresholds the broad midpoint misses.
- **What to screen out:** commodity-steel/generic-fastener "jobbers"; low-turn, low-SDE
  operators; transactional will-call books with little real VMI embedding; territories where a
  Fastenal/Grainger branch or PE roll-up has already planted a flag.

### THE #1 STAGE-2 DILIGENCE PRIORITY (hand-off)
**The specific company's true working-capital gap and inventory health** — actual inventory
turns by SKU, dead/obsolete/slow-moving stock, how much "inventory at cost" is genuinely
salable, the real cash-conversion cycle, and AR aging. This is the single biggest cash item,
it is exactly what the Stage-1 DSCR finding hinges on at the company level, and it cannot be
judged from outside. (See 10-due-diligence.md.)
