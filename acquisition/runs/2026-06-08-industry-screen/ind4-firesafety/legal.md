# LEGAL / REGULATORY REVIEW — Commercial Fire & Life-Safety Inspection / Testing (ITM)
(Not formal legal advice — issues to take to M&A counsel, a CPA, and an SBA lender.)

## ONE-PARAGRAPH RISK READ
This is a clearly SBA-eligible, for-profit operating service industry with the single best
regulatory trait an acquirer can ask for: demand is *legally mandated and re-occurs on a
fixed deadline* under NFPA codes enforced by local fire marshals, so the regulatory regime
is the revenue rather than a tax on it. The dominant legal risk is not eligibility or
transfer — both are routine — it is the **life-safety liability tail**: a missed deficiency
on a sprinkler, alarm, or hood-suppression system can be causally tied to deaths or a total
loss, and inspection reports are durable, dated, signed documents that become Exhibit A in
litigation. Licensing is bridgeable (company-held state fire license plus a NICET-certified
qualifying employee), and deals trade as clean asset purchases, but the catastrophic-tail
profile and the genuinely heavy multi-jurisdiction compliance burden are real, structural
costs of being in this trade and must be priced and insured, not wished away.

## REGULATORY & SBA-ELIGIBILITY MAP (industry-level)
**SBA eligibility:** Clearly eligible. A commercial fire/life-safety ITM company is a
for-profit, US-based, *operating* small business (NAICS 561621, Security Systems Services,
or 238990) — not passive, not real-estate-holding, not on SBA SOP 50 10 ineligible types
(no lending, gambling, speculation, or federal illegality). Standard 7(a) acquisition
candidate.

**Regimes that typically apply:**
- **NFPA codes** — the technical spine. NFPA 25 (water-based/sprinkler & standpipe ITM),
  NFPA 72 (fire alarm & signaling ITM), NFPA 10 (portable extinguishers), NFPA 96
  (kitchen-hood/commercial cooking suppression), NFPA 101 (Life Safety Code, exit/emergency
  lighting). These set the inspection *frequencies* (monthly/quarterly/annual) that create
  the recurring book.
- **Local fire marshal / AHJ (Authority Having Jurisdiction)** — adopts and enforces the
  codes, receives filed inspection documentation, and can red-tag a building. Enforcement is
  hyper-local, which both forces demand and fragments the market.
- **State fire marshal / licensing boards** — most states license fire-protection
  contractors and/or specific disciplines (e.g., Florida DFS fire equipment dealer/permit
  licenses; Texas SFMO Fire Alarm/Fire Sprinkler/Fire Extinguisher licenses; California
  State Fire Marshal C-16 fire-protection contractor licensing alongside CSLB).
- **NICET** (National Institute for Certification in Engineering Technologies) — the
  individual technician certification (e.g., NICET Level II/III in water-based systems or
  fire alarm) that states and AHJs routinely require the qualifying individual to hold.
- **Backflow** — often a separate state/water-authority certification where standpipe/
  backflow testing is offered.

**Rough burden:** Heavy but *stable and well-trodden*. Multi-jurisdiction (state license +
local AHJ filings + per-discipline certs), but the rules are codified, predictable, and the
same compliance machinery the founders would inherit.

## LICENSE & DEAL-TRANSFER NORMS
**Licensing transfer:** This is the crux and it is favorable. The operating license is
typically held at the **company level with a designated qualifying individual (QI) /
qualifying employee** who carries the NICET certification and/or named state license. The
founders do **not** need to personally hold a fire credential — they inherit or hire the QI.
On a sale, the practical paths are: (a) the seller's licensed technician/QI stays on (often
required during transition anyway), (b) a NICET-certified employee already on staff is named
QI, or (c) the buyer recruits a QI. Several state licenses are entity-specific and must be
**re-applied for / reissued to the new owning entity** rather than literally "transferred,"
so timing the license reissuance to the closing is a standard, solvable deal-mechanics item
— not a structural blocker. This satisfies the objectives' bridgeable-credential test.

**Contract / change-of-control:** Inspection-service agreements are usually short, renewable,
and assignable; the durable demand driver is the *code mandate on the building*, not a
locked contract, so revenue is sticky to the building and the local relationship rather than
hostage to change-of-control clauses. Some larger property-management or national-account
contracts carry assignment/consent clauses — a diligence checklist item, not an industry-wide
threat.

**Deal structure / successor liability:** Trades as a **clean asset deal** is the norm —
trucks, test equipment, customer book/goodwill, assumed key employees. Asset structure lets
the buyer largely avoid the seller's historic corporate liabilities. BUT the life-safety
exception matters: tort plaintiffs in a fire/injury case will name everyone in the chain,
and successor liability for *product/service-defect* claims can be argued under the
"continuity of enterprise" / "mere continuation" doctrines in some states even in an asset
deal — so tail-insurance (extended reporting period on the E&O/professional and general
liability), strong indemnities, and an escrow/holdback are standard protective structures
here.

## WHAT I AM DEFERRING TO DUE DILIGENCE
- **This seller's litigation/claims tail** — any past or pending fire-loss, injury, or E&O
  claims tied to inspections this company performed or signed off; the company's actual
  claims history and loss runs.
- **This company's actual license/QI status** — whether the named qualifying individual is
  an owner who will leave, the exact state license reissuance path, and lapses/violations on
  file with the state fire marshal or AHJ.
- **This company's actual contracts, I-9s, and labor classification** — assignment/consent
  clauses in its larger accounts, technician W-2/overtime compliance, any environmental
  handling (e.g., halon/clean-agent or extinguisher recharge chemicals) at its specific site.

## AVERAGE SCORE: 7.1 / 10

## TOP 3 LEGAL/REGULATORY STRENGTHS (of the type)
1. **The regulation IS the revenue** — NFPA-mandated, AHJ-enforced inspection frequencies
   force recurring demand on a deadline; regulatory trajectory only tightens (score 9).
2. **Bridgeable licensing** — company-held license + NICET qualifying employee means the
   founders never need a personal fire credential; clean fit to the operator-fit constraint.
3. **Clean, eligible, B2B asset deal** — squarely SBA-eligible (NAICS 561621), minimal PII,
   low environmental load, and trades as an asset purchase.

## TOP 3 LEGAL/REGULATORY RISKS (of the type)
1. **Catastrophic life-safety liability tail** — a missed deficiency can be causally linked
   to deaths/total loss; signed, dated inspection reports are durable litigation exhibits.
2. **Successor-liability creep** — even in an asset deal, "mere continuation"/continuity-of-
   enterprise doctrines plus tort joinder can pull the buyer into pre-close inspection claims.
3. **Heavy multi-jurisdiction compliance burden** — state fire license reissuance + per-
   discipline NICET certs + local AHJ filings raise the cost and complexity of operating.

## BIGGEST SINGLE RISK
The defining legal risk of this *type* of business is the **life-safety liability tail**:
the firm's core deliverable is a signed, dated certification that a fire-protection system
will work, and if it does not — and a fire causes injury, death, or a major loss — the
inspection company is a primary target, with its own dated reports used as evidence against
it. Unlike most service trades, the failure mode here is not a refund or a re-do; it is
wrongful-death/major-property litigation, often years after the inspection (long-tail), with
severity that can exceed policy limits. This is structural to the industry, not to any one
company. It is insurable (professional/E&O plus general liability, with extended-reporting/
tail coverage on a sale), and disciplined operators manage it with rigorous documentation
and deficiency follow-up — but it is a permanent cost of being in this business, it caps the
liability sub-score, and it is the reason asset-deal protections (tail insurance, indemnities,
escrow) are non-negotiable here rather than optional.

## QUESTIONS FOR COUNSEL / DILIGENCE WHEN SOURCING IN THIS INDUSTRY
1. In each target state, is the operating license held at the entity level with a named
   qualifying individual, and what is the exact path/timeline to reissue it to the buyer's
   entity at closing (e.g., TX SFMO, FL DFS, CA SFM/CSLB)?
2. What E&O/professional and general-liability coverage (limits, exclusions, claims-made vs.
   occurrence) is standard in this trade, and what does tail/extended-reporting coverage
   cost to neutralize the long-tail life-safety exposure on an asset deal?
3. In the target states, how aggressively do courts apply "mere continuation"/continuity-of-
   enterprise successor liability to asset deals in service trades, and what indemnity/
   escrow/holdback structure best insulates the buyer from pre-close inspection claims?

## RECOMMENDATION: PURSUE
This is a legally attractive *type* to hunt in: clearly SBA-eligible, bridgeable licensing,
clean assignable B2B contracts, light environmental and data load, and — rarely — a stable,
demand-forcing regulatory mandate that manufactures the recurring revenue. The average is
held down (and honestly so) by the genuine catastrophic life-safety liability tail and the
heavy multi-jurisdiction compliance burden, which are structural traits, not deal-breakers.
To de-risk, favor the **smaller/mid SBA-tier independent** (below the national consolidators'
buy-box, as the one-pager notes), structure every deal as an **asset purchase with tail E&O
insurance, strong indemnities, and an escrow/holdback**, and confirm a NICET-certified
qualifying employee can be retained or hired so license reissuance times to closing.
