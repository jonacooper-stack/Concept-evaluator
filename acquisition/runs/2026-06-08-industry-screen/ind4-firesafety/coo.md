# COO REVIEW — Commercial Fire & Life-Safety Inspection / Testing (ITM)

## ONE-PARAGRAPH OPERATIONS READ
This is one of the cleanest operating models on the field for a systems-minded GM: the unit of work is a code-mandated, recurring inspection on a fixed building, performed on a published schedule, documented to a standard form, and filed with a fire marshal — which is to say it is inherently a route/scheduled-job business, not bespoke craft work. The founders' modernization edge applies broadly and directly because the typical operator runs paper tickets, manual renewal tracking, and no field-service or inspection-reporting software, while best-in-class tools (Inspect Point, BuildOps, ServiceTrade) already exist off the shelf and are designed for exactly this work. The model scales by adding inspectors/trucks and densifying a building book, and it rolls up cleanly. The one genuine operating constraint that is structural (not company-specific) is the supply of licensed/NICET-certified inspectors — a real labor scarcity that caps how fast you can grow crews and pressures wages.

## THE TYPICAL OPERATING MODEL
A representative $1M–$3M operator holds a book of a few hundred to ~1,000+ commercial buildings under recurring ITM obligations (extinguishers, alarms, sprinkler/standpipe, backflow, exit/emergency lighting, kitchen-hood suppression). The unit of work is one inspection visit producing a pass/fail report and a deficiency list. Work is calendar-driven: NFPA codes set monthly/quarterly/semiannual/annual cadences per system, so a building generates predictable, repeating visits whose due-dates are knowable years out. A licensed/NICET-certified inspector (or a small crew) runs a daily route of stops in a service truck; a scheduler/dispatcher and an office person handle calendars, customer calls, report filing, and invoicing; the owner runs sales, the inspection calendar, and the "inspect → find deficiency → quote → fix" conversion that produces the higher-margin repair revenue. The binding bottleneck is qualified inspector-hours: a building can't be inspected by an unlicensed body, so throughput is gated by how many certified inspectors are on the truck and how tight the route is. Secondary bottlenecks are scheduling friction (no-access, reschedules) and the office's ability to turn completed inspections into filed reports and converted repair quotes.

The wins here are unusually concrete because the gap between the paper-ticket norm and available software is wide:
- **Inspection-reporting / asset software (Inspect Point, BuildOps, ServiceTrade class):** replaces paper tickets with mobile inspection forms, an asset register per building, and auto-generated code-compliant reports. ## CAPACITY MATH (typical)
Take one certified inspector. Inspections range widely by system, but a representative mix runs ~3–6 buildings/day; call it **4 inspections/day × 5 days = 20/week**, ~**1,000 inspections/inspector/year** at ~48 working weeks. A shop with **4 inspectors** does ~**4,000 inspections/year**. The binding bottleneck is certified inspector-hours and route density, not demand (demand is mandated). Where a modern operator unlocks headroom: (a) software recovers ~25% of time lost to paper/admin — roughly the equivalent of **+1 inspector of capacity per 4 without hiring** [ASSUMED — verify via time study]; (b) route densification within a metro turns ~4 stops/day toward ~5–6 by cutting drive time, another 20–30% throughput; (c) the renewal engine refills the book faster than it churns. Net: a paper 4-inspector shop doing ~4,000 inspections/year can plausibly reach ~5,000–6,000 with the SAME licensed headcount through software + routing, then grows further only by hiring/certifying more inspectors. That last clause is the wall — capacity past the software gain requires scarce licensed labor.

## AVERAGE SCORE: 7.7 / 10

## TOP 3 OPERATIONAL STRENGTHS (of the type)
1. Inherently repeatable, calendar-driven route work with due-dates knowable years in advance — the easiest kind of operation to systematize and forecast.
2. Wide, concrete modernization gap (paper → off-the-shelf inspection software + automated renewals + deficiency conversion) that applies across almost every sleepy operator.
3. Clean roll-up/route-density economics behind a licensing moat that keeps the SBA tier un-bid while proving the consolidation thesis at the large tier.

## TOP 3 OPERATIONAL RISKS (of the type)
1. Scarce licensed/NICET-certified inspector labor caps crew growth and pressures wages — the structural ceiling on scaling.
2. Professional-liability/documentation exposure: a missed or sloppily-documented inspection ahead of an incident is a serious operating and reputational risk.
3. Capacity past the software-efficiency gain still requires hiring and certifying more inspectors — growth beyond modernization is gated by that pipeline.

## BIGGEST SINGLE RISK
The structural risk that makes this a harder TYPE than it first appears is the **licensed-inspector labor constraint.** Every inspection legally requires a person holding the right state fire license and/or NICET certification; you cannot grow throughput by hiring cheaper general labor, and the certified pool is thin and aging in many metros. That means the route-density and software gains are real but bounded — they let you do roughly 25% more with the heads you have, after which every additional unit of growth depends on recruiting, certifying, and retaining scarce inspectors in a wage-pressured market. For a roll-up thesis this is the gating variable: tuck-ins are attractive partly because they come WITH certified inspectors attached, but the same scarcity that protects the moat also slows organic expansion and raises your single largest variable cost. It is a manageable, well-understood constraint — not a disqualifier — but it is the thing a buyer must plan the growth model around, not wish away.

## QUESTIONS TO ANSWER WHILE SOURCING IN THIS INDUSTRY
1. In the target metro, how deep is the certified-inspector labor pool — what are prevailing wages, turnover, and how long does it take to certify a promising hire to inspector level?
2. What share of a typical book's recurring revenue silently lapses each year (the renewal-engine opportunity), and what is the typical deficiency-to-repair conversion rate that modern workflow could lift?
3. How portable is the company's qualifying license at change of control in the target state — can a qualifying employee carry it, and what is the realistic bridge time and risk?
4. What is the typical revenue split between recurring inspection fees and lumpier repair/install work, and how does that mix affect route stability and crew utilization?

## RECOMMENDATION: PURSUE
This is a genuinely strong operating TYPE: the work is inherently repeatable and calendar-mandated, the modernization gap is wide and addressable with proven off-the-shelf tools that fit the founders' compliance edge, density and roll-up economics are real, and the founders can run it as GMs because the credential is bridgeable. The one structural drag — scarce licensed-inspector labor — is real and pulls the labor score to mediocre, but it is a planning constraint, not a knockout, and it doubles as part of the moat. From an operations lens, hunt here, targeting the SBA-tier independent (~$1M–$3M revenue) below the national consolidators' buy-box, with the growth plan built explicitly around an inspector-recruiting/certification pipeline.
