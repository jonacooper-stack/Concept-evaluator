# COO REVIEW — Industrial Water-Treatment Service (boiler / cooling-tower / closed-loop chemical treatment & testing)

## ONE-PARAGRAPH OPERATIONS READ
This is one of the cleanest systematizable operating models in the field-service universe: route-based recurring service visits, a defined unit of work (test the water, dose/deliver chemicals, file a report), and a contracted calendar that the operator controls rather than waits on. The model is inherently repeatable and the modernization edge — test-reporting dashboards, route/dispatch software, IoT controllers, a compliance portal, contract-renewal automation — applies broadly across the industry because the typical operator still runs on paper logs and tribal scheduling. The one real industry-level operating constraint is technical labor: the people doing the visits need genuine water-chemistry competence and that talent pool is thin, ages out, and takes months to ramp, which caps how fast you can add crews even when demand exists.

## THE TYPICAL OPERATING MODEL
A representative firm ($1.5M–$3.5M revenue [ASSUMED — verify with NAICS 562910/541380 comps]) runs a small fleet of service vehicles, each driven by a water-treatment service technician who carries test kits, sensors, and drummed/totes of treatment chemical. The unit of work is a **scheduled site visit**: the tech pulls water samples from boilers, cooling towers, and closed loops; runs titrations/electronic tests for conductivity, pH, hardness, inhibitor residual, microbiological (Legionella-relevant) counts; adjusts chemical feed; tops off chemical inventory; and files a service report that doubles as the customer's compliance record. Contracts are monthly or quarterly recurring, multi-year, and very sticky because switching risks corrosion, scale, Legionella exposure, and equipment downtime. A tech typically runs **a route of scheduled accounts per day**, with visit length driven by site size (a 20-minute closed-loop check vs. a multi-hour multi-tower industrial plant). Behind the field crew sits a lab/reporting function and an owner who personally holds the top technical accounts, prices the contracts, and does the selling. The **binding bottleneck is qualified technician-hours**, not vehicles, chemicals, or demand: every new account needs a competent person to service it, and competent water-treatment techs are the scarce input.

## STREAMLINE & MODERNIZE PLAN (typical)
The industry is broadly un-systemized, so the same playbook lifts most operators:
- **Field-service / route software** (ServiceTitan-class, or vertical tools like a Jobber/FieldRoutes/BuildOps setup) for scheduling, dispatch, and digital service reports — replaces paper service tickets and whiteboard scheduling. Expected gain: **1–2 extra stops/tech/day from tighter routing and eliminated drive-back/paperwork time** [ASSUMED — verify by ride-along time study on a real target's routes].
- **Digital test-reporting dashboards + customer compliance portal** — auto-generates the compliance-grade report the customer needs for ASHRAE 188 / Legionella documentation, which is today retyped by hand. Cuts post-visit admin **~30–60 min/tech/day** [ASSUMED — verify against current report turnaround] and becomes a retention/upsell hook.
- **IoT / automated water monitoring** (remote conductivity/level/feed controllers) — lets one tech monitor more sites between physical visits, converting some weekly visits to bi-weekly without losing control; this is the single biggest capacity unlock and also the clearest premium-pricing story.
- **Contract-renewal + price-escalation automation** — the typical operator "rarely raises prices"; a CRM with renewal alerts and built-in CPI/annual escalators recovers margin across the whole book. Expected gain: **3–7% annual price realization** that historically leaked [ASSUMED — verify by inspecting a target's renewal/pricing history].
- **B2B digital marketing + CRM pipeline** — most of the field has no website lead engine; facility-manager-targeted outreach feeds organic growth.
Net: a modern operator can plausibly add **15–25% effective tech capacity** from routing + reporting automation before adding headcount, plus recover margin on pricing.

## CAPACITY MATH (typical)
Take a representative single-region operator with **6 service techs**.
- Assume each tech works **~5 days/wk** and today completes **~6 scheduled stops/day** (mix of short closed-loop checks and longer tower/boiler sites) → **30 stops/tech/wk → ~180 stops/wk → ~9,000 visits/yr** [ASSUMED — verify by counting a real route board].
- The binding constraint is qualified technician-hours: a stop consumes drive time + on-site test/dose + report-writing.
- Modernization attacks the non-billable slice. If routing software removes ~1 redundant trip/day and digital reporting removes ~45 min/day of retyping, that frees roughly **1–1.5 hours/tech/day**, i.e. **~1 extra stop/tech/day → ~7 stops/day → 35/wk → ~10,500 visits/yr.** That is **~15% more throughput on the same 6 techs and the same fleet** — pure margin, no new payroll.
- IoT remote monitoring can stretch further by converting a subset of weekly physical visits to remote-plus-periodic, but that is a multi-year capex/retrofit play, so I treat the routing+reporting **~15%** as the near-term, defensible number and the IoT lift as upside.
- **To grow beyond ~+15% you must add techs**, and that runs straight into the labor constraint below — which is why scaling this type is paced by hiring/training competent water-chemistry techs, not by trucks or chemicals.

## TOP 3 OPERATIONAL STRENGTHS (of the type)
1. Contracted, route-based recurring service with a clean, defined unit of work — among the most systematizable field-service models there is.
2. Broad, real modernization headroom (paper-to-software, IoT monitoring, automated compliance reporting, renewal/price automation) that lifts capacity and margin across the typical operator.
3. Year-round, essential, recession-resilient demand with sticky multi-year contracts; the technical moat sits with bridgeable employee skill, not a non-transferable owner license — strong operator fit.

## TOP 3 OPERATIONAL RISKS (of the type)
1. Scarce technical labor: competent water-treatment techs are the binding bottleneck and are thin and aging industry-wide, capping growth speed.
2. Compliance/safety stakes: Legionella and corrosion/scale risk mean a service lapse or bad program is a high-consequence event, raising the bar on quality systems and insurance.
3. Chemical handling/logistics exposure: DOT-classed product on vehicles and on-site dosing carry safety and regulatory handling burden beyond a typical light-service route.

## BIGGEST SINGLE RISK
The structural operating risk of this TYPE is **technical-labor scarcity**, and it is an industry trait, not a one-company problem. The service cannot be performed by a generic field tech — the person at the site must genuinely understand water chemistry, dosing, and what a failing reading means, because a mistake can mean corroded equipment, a Legionella exposure event, or a blown compliance record. That competent pool is small, aging toward retirement, and takes months of supervised ramp to build, so an operator can have demand, vehicles, and chemicals ready and still be unable to add a route because there is no qualified person to drive it. This caps the speed of organic and roll-up growth, makes wage pressure a live margin threat, and means the modernization edge (which can squeeze ~15% more out of existing techs) is partly a hedge against a constraint you cannot simply hire your way around quickly. Any hunt in this industry must treat technician recruiting/training as the core operating program, not an afterthought.

## QUESTIONS TO ANSWER WHILE SOURCING IN THIS INDUSTRY
1. In the target's region, what is the realistic supply, wage, and ramp time for competent water-treatment technicians, and how many are within reach to fund a route-add growth plan?
2. What share of the typical book is genuinely recurring multi-year contract revenue vs. one-off remediation/equipment work, and how are renewals and price escalators actually structured?
3. How much physical-visit frequency can credibly be converted to IoT/remote monitoring without raising compliance risk, and what is the retrofit cost per site to unlock that capacity lift?
4. What is the industry's insurance/EMR and DOT-handling burden for chemical transport and on-site dosing, and how does that shape per-tech overhead at scale?

## RECOMMENDATION: PURSUE
The operating model is structurally excellent for a buy-and-modernize play: defined recurring unit of work, year-round essential demand, sticky contracts, broad un-systemized modernization headroom, a clean roll-up path below the chemical majors, and strong owner-operator fit because the credential sits with employees. The one genuine industry-level drag is technical-labor scarcity, which paces growth and pulls capacity, scalability, and quality/safety scores into the 5–6 band. That is a constraint to plan around, not a disqualifier — it argues for hunting operators in **dense metro/industrial corridors with an existing trained crew and a renewable contract book**, where the modernization edge and route density can compound while the founders build a technician pipeline as a core program.
