# LEGAL / REGULATORY REVIEW — Industrial Water-Treatment Service

(Not formal legal advice — these are issues to take to M&A counsel, a CPA, and an SBA lender before any deal.)

## ONE-PARAGRAPH RISK READ
Industrial water-treatment service is a clearly SBA-eligible, B2B route-service business whose legal profile is dominated by one real exposure — chemical handling and discharge — bolted onto an otherwise clean, assignable, lightly-licensed service model. The trade itself rarely requires a personal occupational license to operate (unlike electrical or plumbing), so the founders' lack of a trade credential is not a structural blocker; the technical "license" is functionally the trained chemist/technician, who can be retained as an employee. The two things that make this a heavier-than-average industry are (1) DOT/EPA/OSHA chemical regimes (hazmat transport, RCRA waste, SDS/HazCom, discharge permitting) that travel with the work, and (2) a rising but DEMAND-FORCING Legionella regime (ASHRAE 188 / CMS / state codes) that is a regulatory tailwind for revenue even as it raises the liability ceiling. Net: a good-but-not-pristine legal posture, with the environmental and product-liability tail as the dimensions to underwrite hard.

## REGULATORY & SBA-ELIGIBILITY MAP (industry-level)
**SBA eligibility:** Clearly eligible. This is a for-profit, US-based, active **operating** service company (NAICS ~562910 environmental/remediation services, 541380 testing labs, 325998 chemical prep) — not passive, not real-estate-holding, not a lender/investor, not on SBA SOP 50 10 ineligible list (no gambling, cannabis, speculation, lending, pyramid sales). A 7(a) acquisition loan is squarely available; the business model (recurring service + product resale) is exactly what 7(a) is built to finance.

**Specific regimes that typically apply:**
- **EPA / state environmental — discharge & waste.** Cooling-tower blowdown and boiler discharge to sewer or surface water implicate the **Clean Water Act NPDES** program and local **POTW pretreatment** permits; chemical waste implicates **RCRA** (hazardous-waste generator status, manifests). Burden: moderate, mostly handled by the customer facility but the treater can carry generator/transporter obligations.
- **DOT / PHMSA — hazardous-materials transport (49 CFR).** Technicians transporting treatment chemicals (acids, biocides, oxidizers) in service vehicles trigger **hazmat shipping papers, placarding, packaging, and HazMat employee training**. Real, recurring, but routine in the trade.
- **OSHA — HazCom (29 CFR 1910.1200), PPE, HAZWOPER where applicable.** Safety Data Sheets, labeling, exposure control. Standard industrial-service burden.
- **EPA FIFRA — pesticide/biocide registration & applicator rules.** Many treatment biocides are FIFRA-registered antimicrobials; selling/applying them can require **state pesticide-applicator licensing** for technicians in some states. This is a state-by-state qualifying-employee item, not an owner-personal blocker.
- **Legionella / public-health regime (demand-forcing).** **ASHRAE Standard 188**, **CMS** requirements for healthcare facilities' water-management plans, and state codes (e.g., **New York 10 NYCRR Part 4** for cooling towers) increasingly MANDATE testing, treatment, and documentation — a structural revenue tailwind that also raises liability stakes.

## LICENSE & DEAL-TRANSFER NORMS
**Trade licensing:** There is generally **no single owner-held occupational license** required to own/operate a water-treatment service firm — this is the key favorable fact versus a licensed trade. Where credentials exist (state **pesticide/antimicrobial applicator certifications** under FIFRA; sometimes a **wastewater/backflow** certification), they are held by **technicians as a qualifying-employee model** and are retainable through the transition. The founders need not personally hold a credential; they manage the crew that does. This satisfies hard-constraint #4.

**Contract / change-of-control norms:** Recurring service contracts in this B2B trade are typically **assignable** in an asset purchase, though institutional customers (hospitals, universities, government facilities) often carry **assignment-consent / change-of-control clauses** and may require re-bid or re-qualification on a sale. Master service agreements with large facilities and any government/municipal contracts are the ones that can require consent — a diligence item per listing, not a structural industry defect.

**Deal-structure & successor liability:** These businesses **typically trade as asset deals**, the clean and lender-preferred structure that leaves most pre-closing liabilities with the seller. The asset-deal norm is undercut in this industry by **environmental successor liability**: CERCLA and many state environmental statutes can reach a successor for contamination regardless of deal form, and product/exposure (e.g., chemical injury, Legionella outbreak) claims can have a long tail. So while the structure is clean, the environmental/product carve-out is the real negotiation — escrows, reps & warranties, and tail insurance.

## WHAT I AM DEFERRING TO DUE DILIGENCE
- **The specific seller's environmental history** — past spills, RCRA violations, NPDES exceedances, open EPA/state consent orders, and any contamination at owned/leased sites (Phase I, possibly Phase II).
- **The specific company's contracts** — which accounts carry change-of-control/assignment-consent clauses, customer concentration, and whether key technician certifications transfer with retained staff.
- **The specific company's labor file** — I-9s, technician classification, applicator-license currency, OSHA/DOT training records, and any prior injury/wage-hour claims.

## TOP 3 LEGAL/REGULATORY STRENGTHS (of the type)
1. No owner-personal occupational license is required; the technical credential sits with retainable employees — clears the non-bridgeable-credential constraint cleanly.
2. The Legionella/ASHRAE 188 / CMS regulatory trajectory is a stable, demand-FORCING mandate that legally underwrites recurring revenue.
3. Clean SBA eligibility and a normal assignable B2B service-contract / asset-deal structure.

## TOP 3 LEGAL/REGULATORY RISKS (of the type)
1. Inherent environmental exposure — chemical handling, DOT hazmat transport, RCRA waste, and NPDES discharge — that no operator escapes.
2. Successor environmental liability (CERCLA/state) that can pierce the otherwise-clean asset-deal structure.
3. A high-severity liability tail (chemical injury, Legionella outbreak) that makes pollution/environmental and product insurance a cost-and-exclusion negotiation rather than a checkbox.

## BIGGEST SINGLE RISK
The defining legal risk of this TYPE is the **environmental-and-exposure tail that survives the deal structure**. Even though these businesses trade as asset deals — normally the clean way to leave a seller's sins behind — **CERCLA and parallel state statutes impose successor and even arranger/transporter liability for contamination regardless of how the deal is papered**, and a single Legionella outbreak or chemical-exposure incident traced to a treated system can generate catastrophic, long-tailed bodily-injury claims. The same regulatory tailwind that forces recurring demand (ASHRAE 188, CMS, NY Part 4) simultaneously raises the documentary and liability stakes: failure to maintain a defensible water-management program becomes evidence in a claim. This is fully manageable — it is underwritten with environmental/pollution-legal-liability and product-liability coverage, tail/run-off policies, robust reps & warranties, and an environmental escrow — but it means the buyer must price insurance and a Phase I (and possibly Phase II) into every deal and cannot treat this as a "clean" service business the way a janitorial or HVAC-maintenance route would be.

## QUESTIONS FOR COUNSEL / DILIGENCE WHEN SOURCING IN THIS INDUSTRY
1. In our target states, do treatment biocides require **FIFRA state pesticide-applicator licensing**, are those held by retainable employees, and do any operating permits (NPDES contributor, generator IDs) transfer or require re-issuance on a change of control?
2. What **pollution/environmental-impairment-liability** and **product-liability** coverage and limits are standard in this trade, what are the typical exclusions (Legionella? gradual pollution?), and is **tail/run-off** coverage available for pre-closing acts?
3. Which customer segments (hospitals, universities, government/municipal) routinely carry **assignment-consent or re-bid-on-sale clauses**, and how much typical revenue sits behind change-of-control gates?
4. What is the realistic **CERCLA/state successor-liability** exposure for a buyer of an asset deal here, and what escrow / R&W-insurance structure neutralizes it?

## RECOMMENDATION: PURSUE
Hunt here, with eyes open. The industry clears every hard constraint — clearly SBA-eligible, no owner-personal license barrier, assignable recurring B2B contracts, normal asset-deal structure — and enjoys a genuine, stable, demand-forcing Legionella/ASHRAE 188 regulatory tailwind. From a pure legal-risk standpoint the average is dragged into the mid-6s by the inherent environmental profile, multi-agency operating burden, and a high-severity liability tail, none of which are deal-killers but all of which must be priced. If the buyer wants to compress the risk, the cleaner sub-segment is **closed-loop / HVAC and cooling-tower treatment for commercial buildings and institutions** (lighter discharge and waste exposure than heavy-industrial/boiler-and-process work at manufacturing or food/bev plants), paired with disciplined environmental insurance, a Phase I on any owned site, and an environmental escrow on every transaction.
