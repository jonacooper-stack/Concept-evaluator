# LEGAL / REGULATORY REVIEW — Calibration & Test-Equipment Service Labs (ISO/IEC 17025-accredited metrology labs)

**(Not formal legal advice — these are issues to take to M&A counsel, a CPA, and an SBA lender.)**

## ONE-PARAGRAPH RISK READ

This is one of the cleaner legal/regulatory profiles among "sleepy" acquirable trades. There is **no government operating license** to run a calibration lab — the gating credential is a **private, voluntary accreditation (ISO/IEC 17025)** granted by a signatory of the ILAC Mutual Recognition Arrangement (in the US, principally **A2LA, ANAB, NVLAP, or Perry Johnson Laboratory Accreditation**), and it attaches to the **legal entity, its documented quality-management system, its scope of accreditation, and its qualified technical personnel** — not to the owner personally. That single fact drives both the upside (a real barrier to entry) and the central deal risk (accreditation is **entity-and-quality-system-bound, not freely portable on an asset sale, and does not automatically survive a change of ownership** — it must be confirmed/transferred and is contingent on continuity of the QMS and key staff). The trade is otherwise lightly regulated, low environmental burden, B2B with minimal consumer/PII exposure, and the liability tail (a bad calibration cascading into a customer recall) is real but standard, contractually limitable, and insurable. Net: low regulatory burden, modest insurable tail, but a **deal-structure question (accreditation transfer/re-grant) that must be solved at signing.**

## REGULATORY & SBA-ELIGIBILITY MAP (industry-level)

**SBA eligibility:** Clearly eligible. A for-profit, US-based, operating B2B service business (NAICS **541380 — Testing Laboratories**) is squarely within SBA 7(a) eligibility. It is not passive, not a real-estate holder, not lending/investment, not speculative, not an SBA-ineligible category (no cannabis, gambling, MLM, adult, etc.). Standard 7(a) acquisition financing applies, subject to SBA SOP 50 10 affiliation/eligibility checks done at the company level.

**Applicable regimes (industry-typical):**
- **ISO/IEC 17025:2017** — the governing competence standard for testing/calibration labs. Not a law; a **voluntary accreditation** assessed by an accreditation body. Burden: an annual/biennial surveillance assessment plus a full reassessment cycle (typically every ~2 years; ILAC-MRA bodies operate on a four-year accreditation cycle with interim surveillance). Maintaining the documented QMS, measurement-traceability chain to **NIST** (national metrology institute), proficiency testing, and measurement-uncertainty budgets is the ongoing day-to-day compliance.
- **NIST traceability** — calibrations must be traceable to SI units via NIST or another NMI; this is a documentation/standards-maintenance burden, not a licensing one.
- **Sector overlays (customer-driven, not on the lab directly in most cases):** **AS9100 / Nadcap** for aerospace/defense supply chains, **FDA 21 CFR 820 / ISO 13485** for medical-device customers, **ITAR/EAR** export-control exposure *only* where the lab handles controlled defense articles/technical data (a real but segment-specific item — defense/aerospace calibration work can pull ITAR registration obligations). These largely flow from *who the customers are*, and matter most in the aerospace/defense and medical sub-segments.
- **OSHA** general-industry workplace safety; routine for a light-industrial bench environment.

Overall ongoing regulatory burden to *operate* is **light-to-moderate**: the heavy lifting is maintaining accreditation, not satisfying a government agency.

## LICENSE & DEAL-TRANSFER NORMS

**This is the crux, so treat it carefully.** There is **no state operating license** for the trade itself, which removes the classic "owner must personally hold a non-bridgeable credential" knockout — the founders need not be metrologists; the technical competence lives in the accreditation scope and qualified staff. So on the *licensing* axis the industry scores well.

**But ISO/IEC 17025 accreditation does NOT transfer like a license, and this is an industry-level (not company-specific) norm:**
- Accreditation is granted to a **defined legal entity at defined location(s)** for a defined **scope** (the specific measurement parameters/ranges/uncertainties), and it is **contingent on the continuity of the documented QMS and the technical/authorizing personnel.**
- On a **stock/membership-interest purchase** (the buyer takes the same legal entity), the accreditation generally **continues**, but the accreditation body (A2LA/ANAB/NVLAP) must be **notified of the change in ownership/management**, and bodies reserve the right to conduct an assessment to confirm the QMS and competence survived the transition; a wholesale loss of the quality manager / signatory metrologists can put the scope at risk.
- On an **asset purchase into a new entity** (common SBA structure for liability reasons), accreditation **does not automatically follow the assets** — the new entity typically must apply to **transfer/extend** the scope or pursue re-accreditation, with the accreditation body verifying the same site, equipment, traceability, and qualified personnel carried over. This can be expedited where continuity is genuine, but it is **not guaranteed and not instantaneous.**

This creates a structural tension with the SBA buyer's instinct to do a clean asset deal: the cleanest liability structure (asset deal, new NewCo) is the one that most jeopardizes the very accreditation that is the moat. Standard mitigations are well-trodden: (a) structure to **preserve the accredited entity** or pre-clear the transfer with the accreditation body before close; (b) **retention/non-compete and continuity agreements** for the quality manager and signatory technicians; (c) a **closing condition / seller note holdback** tied to accreditation surviving the transition; (d) early engagement with A2LA/ANAB on a transition plan.

**Contract / change-of-control norms:** Customer relationships are typically **recurring purchase-order / recall-cycle** based rather than long-term master contracts, so change-of-control clauses are *less* of a revenue threat than in contract-heavy industries — most of the recall book is sticky habit + audit pressure, not assignable-contract-dependent. Where larger aerospace/defense/medical customers do have master supply agreements, those frequently *do* contain change-of-control / re-qualification / approved-supplier provisions, and supplier requalification (and continued accreditation) is a condition of staying on the AVL. Leases for the lab space are ordinary commercial and assignable with landlord consent.

**Deal-structure & successor-liability profile:** The trade **typically trades as an asset deal**, the SBA norm, which limits assumed liabilities. The main successor-liability vectors are ordinary (employment/WARN if mass-staffed, sales/use tax, environmental at site) plus the **professional-liability tail** described below; none is industry-aberrant. Successor liability here is **lower than in environmentally heavy trades** — the differentiator is the accreditation-transfer mechanics, not assumed liabilities.

## WHAT I AM DEFERRING TO DUE DILIGENCE

- **This particular lab's accreditation status, scope, last assessment findings, open nonconformances, and whether THIS quality manager/signatories will stay** — the single most important Stage-2 item; confirm with the accreditation body directly and condition close on it.
- **This seller's specific liability tail:** any pending/threatened claims tied to a bad calibration (and whether a customer recall was ever traced to the lab), current E&O/professional-liability and general-liability policy limits and claims history.
- **This company's specific customer contracts** (any master supply agreements with change-of-control/requalification clauses), any **ITAR/EAR-controlled work** actually performed, its lease, its I-9/worker-classification status, and any environmental history at its specific site.

## TOP 3 LEGAL/REGULATORY STRENGTHS (of the type)

1. **No personal/government operating license** — the gating credential (ISO/IEC 17025) is institutional and bridgeable via qualified staff, so the founders are not blocked by a non-transferable personal credential.
2. **Mandate-driven, stable demand with a real accreditation moat** — customer quality systems (ISO 9001/AS9100/FDA) force recurring recalibration, and A2LA/ANAB accreditation keeps casual entrants out; regulation works *for* the buyer.
3. **Clean profile across environmental, labor, and data axes** — light-industrial, W-2 non-union, simple B2B with no consumer-PII/auto-renewal tail and no CERCLA exposure.

## TOP 3 LEGAL/REGULATORY RISKS (of the type)

1. **Accreditation does not freely transfer on an asset sale** and is contingent on QMS + key-staff continuity — the moat and the deal risk are the same asset.
2. **Professional-liability tail**: a bad calibration can cascade into a customer recall/scrap claim; insurable and limitable, but real, and larger in aerospace/medical work.
3. **Segment-specific overlays**: aerospace/defense work can carry **ITAR/EAR** obligations and master-agreement requalification clauses that add compliance and change-of-control friction.

## BIGGEST SINGLE RISK

The defining legal/deal risk of this industry is that **the ISO/IEC 17025 accreditation — which is the entire moat — is bound to the legal entity, its quality-management system, and its qualified personnel, and does not automatically survive the change of ownership or the asset-into-NewCo structure that SBA buyers prefer.** A buyer who closes a clean asset deal without pre-clearing the accreditation transfer with the accreditation body (A2LA/ANAB/NVLAP/PJLA), or who loses the quality manager and signatory metrologists in the transition, can find that the new entity must re-apply for or rebuild its scope — temporarily losing the right to issue accredited certificates and, with it, the very customers (and recall book) that justified the price. This is solvable with standard mechanics (preserve the accredited entity or pre-clear the transfer, lock in staff with retention/continuity agreements, and make accreditation survival a condition of close with a seller-note holdback), but it must be engineered *before* signing, not discovered after.

## QUESTIONS FOR COUNSEL / DILIGENCE WHEN SOURCING IN THIS INDUSTRY

1. For our preferred SBA structure (asset purchase into NewCo vs. entity purchase), **how do A2LA / ANAB / NVLAP / PJLA actually treat a change of ownership** — does accreditation continue with notification, require a transfer assessment, or require re-application — and what is the realistic timeline and cost to keep the scope live through close?
2. What **retention, non-compete, and continuity terms** for the quality manager and accredited signatories are necessary to preserve the scope, and can we make **continued accreditation a closing condition** backed by a seller-note holdback or escrow?
3. Does the target perform any **ITAR/EAR-controlled (defense/aerospace) calibration work**, and what registration/export-control compliance and supplier-requalification (AS9100/Nadcap, FDA approved-supplier) obligations attach — including change-of-control/requalification clauses in any master supply agreements?
4. What **E&O/professional-liability and general-liability** coverage and limitation-of-liability terms are standard for this trade, and what does the historical claims experience for "bad calibration → customer recall" look like?

## RECOMMENDATION: PURSUE

This is a legally attractive TYPE to hunt in: clearly SBA-eligible (NAICS 541380), no non-transferable personal license, mandate-driven recurring demand, and a clean environmental/labor/data profile. The one genuine, industry-level legal risk — that ISO/IEC 17025 accreditation is entity-and-staff-bound and does not freely follow an asset sale — is real but **structurable**, not a knockout: it is solved by pre-clearing the transfer with the accreditation body, locking in the quality manager and signatories, and conditioning close on accreditation survival. If a narrower posture is wanted, **favor general-industry/industrial-customer labs over deep aerospace/defense-only labs**, which carry the heaviest ITAR/EAR and master-agreement requalification overhead. From the legal/regulatory seat, this industry is worth sourcing real deals in.
