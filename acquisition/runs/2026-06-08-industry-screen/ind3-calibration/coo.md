COO REVIEW — Calibration & Test-Equipment Service Labs (ISO/IEC 17025-accredited)

ONE-PARAGRAPH OPERATIONS READ
The operating model is genuinely systematizable and inherently repeatable: the unit of work is a discrete instrument calibration against a documented procedure, driven by a fixed recall cycle that schedules itself. That is a near-ideal shape for a modern operator — defined jobs, a recurring book, and a quality system that already demands process discipline. The founders' ops-modernization edge applies broadly because the typical lab runs on spreadsheets and paper certificates with no recall automation. The one real operating tension at the industry level is the labor pool: accredited metrology technicians are specialized and thin, which caps how fast you can convert recall demand into throughput.

THE TYPICAL OPERATING MODEL
A representative lab is a single facility (often with a small mobile/on-site arm) employing a handful of accredited metrology technicians, a quality manager who owns the ISO/IEC 17025 scope, plus an owner and an admin/customer-service person. The unit of work is one instrument: it arrives (shipped in, dropped off, or picked up on-site), is logged against the customer's asset, calibrated against reference standards per a documented procedure, a certificate of calibration is issued, and the instrument is returned. Work batches by discipline — dimensional, electrical, temperature, pressure, torque/force, mass — and each discipline needs the right reference standards and a tech qualified for that scope. The binding bottleneck is qualified bench-hours within a given discipline: you can only calibrate what your standards and your scoped technicians cover. Demand is self-generating because every calibrated instrument carries a due date and the customer's own audit (ISO 9001, AS9100, FDA) forces re-calibration on schedule. The day-to-day failure mode is not finding work; it is scheduling, turnaround time, and not letting recall-due instruments slip through tracked-by-hand spreadsheets.

STREAMLINE & MODERNIZE PLAN (typical)
The industry-wide upside is operational, not just marketing. (1) Calibration-management software (named off-the-shelf options exist: Indysoft, MET/CAL plus a management layer, ProCalV5, IndySoft GAGEtrak, Beamex CMX/LOGiCAL) to replace asset/recall spreadsheets — this alone converts a manual recall list into automated due-date reminders and a closed-loop tickler that recaptures slipped renewals. Expected gain: recapturing even [ASSUMED] 5–10% of lapsed/late recalls is pure incremental revenue at high margin; verify by asking a target for its recall-list size vs. instruments actually returned on time. (2) A customer asset/certificate portal so customers self-serve certs and see upcoming due dates — cuts admin handling and stickiness; reduces the certificate-retrieval phone/email load [ASSUMED 20–40% of admin time]. (3) Scheduling/dispatch for the on-site arm to cut windshield time and batch nearby customers. (4) Standardized turnaround-time and bench-utilization KPIs (instruments/tech/day, % on-time, % expedited) — most labs track none of these. (5) Procedure/SOP templating leveraging the founders' compliance background to keep the 17025 scope clean during growth. Realistic blended outcome: meaningfully higher technician throughput and recall capture without adding standards. Cheap verification: ask three target labs whether they have automated recall reminders and a customer portal — the answer is almost always no.

CAPACITY MATH (typical)
Take a lab with 5 accredited techs. Assume [ASSUMED] each productive tech completes ~8–14 calibrations per day depending on discipline (simple gauges/scales are fast; complex multi-point electrical/dimensional are slow). Midpoint ~11/day × 5 techs × ~230 productive days ≈ 12,650 calibrations/year. At a blended [ASSUMED] $90–140 fee, that's roughly $1.1M–$1.8M of calibration revenue, consistent with the one-pager's $1.5M–$3M band once repair, accreditation work, and expedite premiums are added. The binding bottleneck is qualified bench-hours per discipline — not building space and not demand. Headroom a modern operator can unlock without hiring: (a) recall-capture automation pulls slipped renewals back into the book; (b) better scheduling/batching lifts on-site stops per day; (c) shifting simple, high-volume calibrations to lower-cost qualified techs frees senior techs for complex (higher-fee) work. To grow beyond ~15–20% on a fixed bench you must add scoped technicians or add disciplines/standards — i.e., capital + hiring, which is the real wall. Show-work caveat: every throughput number above is [ASSUMED] and should be checked against a target's actual instruments-per-tech-per-day and revenue-per-tech.

WHAT I AM DEFERRING TO DUE DILIGENCE
- ISO/IEC 17025 accreditation continuity through the sale — whether THIS lab's scope, documented quality system, and named qualified signatories survive an ownership change and the accreditation body's review (explicitly a Stage-2 item).
- THIS owner's personal day-to-day role: whether the retiring metrologist is also a key technical signatory or the de facto quality manager, and how bridgeable that is.
- THIS company's process maturity, SOP documentation quality, and recall-book hygiene; and key-staff/technician retention (especially the quality manager and any sole-qualified-for-a-discipline tech).

AVERAGE SCORE: 7.0 / 10

TOP 3 OPERATIONAL STRENGTHS (of the type)
1. Inherently repeatable, self-scheduling work — a discrete instrument calibrated to a documented procedure on a mandated recall cycle.
2. Broad, concrete modernization-ops upside (recall automation + customer portal) against an industry on spreadsheets and paper.
3. Steady, year-round, geographically flexible demand with a clear consolidator-proven roll-up path.

TOP 3 OPERATIONAL RISKS (of the type)
1. Thin pool of accredited metrology technicians — the structural labor constraint on the whole type.
2. Throughput capped by qualified bench-hours per discipline; growth beyond ~15–20% needs hired/scoped techs and added standards (capital).
3. Accreditation/scope risk and calibration-error liability sit at the center of the operating model.

BIGGEST SINGLE RISK
The structural operating risk for this TYPE is labor: accredited metrology technicians are a specialized, slow-to-train pool, and the work cannot be done by general labor because it is the technical scope that underpins the lab's value. Unlike a route-based field business where you can hire and train crews quickly, here throughput is gated by how many technicians are qualified-and-scoped for each discipline, and adding capacity means either poaching scarce talent or running a long internal training pipeline against the quality system. This means the industry's beautiful recurring demand can outrun the operator's ability to serve it, and it makes technician retention (a Stage-2 item for a specific lab) an industry-wide vulnerability: lose a discipline's sole qualified tech and you can lose that part of your scope. The recurring book is the strength; the bench is the ceiling.

QUESTIONS TO ANSWER WHILE SOURCING IN THIS INDUSTRY
1. What is the local/regional supply of accredited metrology technicians, and what does it cost to add or train one to a given discipline scope?
2. For a target's recall book, how many instruments are tracked vs. actually returned on time — i.e., how much slipped-recall revenue can automation recapture?
3. What is instruments-per-tech-per-day and revenue-per-tech by discipline, so the bench bottleneck and real headroom can be modeled?
4. Which disciplines depend on a single qualified technician, and how concentrated is the scope on one or two people?

RECOMMENDATION: PURSUE
This is a strong TYPE to hunt in operationally: the work is repeatable and self-scheduling, the modernization-ops edge is broad and concrete, demand is steady and year-round, and a consolidator-proven roll-up path exists. The honest drag is labor — accredited techs are scarce and bench-hours cap throughput — which is why the average lands at good-not-rare rather than higher. Hunt it, but during sourcing weight the technician supply and discipline-concentration questions heavily; a lab whose recall demand is throttled by a thin or single-point-of-failure bench is the operating trap to avoid.
