# LEGAL / REGULATORY REVIEW — Regional Secure Document-Destruction / Shredding Company

(Not formal legal advice — I am an experienced deal operator with regulatory and transaction pattern-recognition, not a licensed attorney. Everything below is a set of issues to take to a real M&A attorney, a CPA, and an SBA lender. Do not close on it as legal counsel.)

## ONE-PARAGRAPH RISK READ
This is, on its face, one of the cleaner regulatory profiles a search-fund buyer can find: a for-profit US operating service business, plainly SBA-eligible, with no occupational/professional license that an owner must personally hold, hundreds of small B2B accounts (no concentration), and trucks/bins as hard collateral. The principal legal exposure is not "can we operate" — it is **what we inherit and what leaks on change of control**: (a) a data-security/chain-of-custody liability tail (this company's entire product is the legally-reliable destruction of others' regulated records — a botched job is a HIPAA/FACTA/state-breach event with contractual indemnity teeth), (b) auto-renewing customer service agreements whose **assignment/change-of-control terms determine how much recurring revenue survives the sale**, and (c) inherited employment exposure on a route-driver/operator crew (wage-hour, driver qualification, possibly DOT). All three are knowable in diligence and manageable with the right deal structure (asset purchase, escrow/holdback, reps on compliance history, NAID/insurance verification). None is presumptively fatal. The deal-killers here would be discovery of a live data-breach claim or a master customer contract that voids on sale — both of which diligence must surface before closing.

## REGULATORY & SBA-ELIGIBILITY MAP

**SBA 7(a) eligibility — clearly eligible.** This is a for-profit, US-based, active operating company (not a passive/real-estate-holding, lending, investment, or speculative entity), well under SBA size standards (~$2.2M revenue against a NAICS 561990 / 562111-area standard in the tens of millions), and not on the SBA ineligible-business list (13 CFR 120.110). Document destruction is a permissible service business. A full change of ownership (100% of the equity/assets bought) satisfies SBA's "complete change of ownership" rule under SOP 50 10; a seller note, if used toward the equity injection, must be on **full standby** for the loan term to count. Personal guarantee from any 20%+ owner is required. No citizenship/ownership red flags are presented (founders assumed US persons — confirm). I see no eligibility blocker.

**Industry regimes that apply day-to-day:**
- **HIPAA (45 CFR Parts 160/164) — central.** Medical/dental customers are HIPAA covered entities; the shredder is almost certainly a **Business Associate** handling Protected Health Information. That means a **Business Associate Agreement (BAA)** with each healthcare customer, breach-notification obligations under the HITECH Breach Notification Rule, and direct OCR (HHS Office for Civil Rights) enforcement exposure for a destruction failure.
- **FACTA Disposal Rule (16 CFR Part 682, FTC).** Federal rule requiring "reasonable measures" to dispose of consumer report information — the literal regulatory basis for paid shredding of financial/insurance records. The company's Certificate of Destruction is the customers' compliance artifact.
- **GLBA Safeguards Rule** for the financial/accounting/insurance customer segment (records containing nonpublic personal information).
- **State data-disposal & breach-notification statutes** in every state of operation (e.g., California, plus all 50 states' breach-notice laws; some states have specific records-destruction statutes). Multi-state if routes cross state lines.
- **DOT / FMCSA** — likely **only if** the company runs trucks over 10,001 lbs GVWR and/or crosses state lines: USDOT number, driver qualification files, hours-of-service, drug-and-alcohol testing program. Mobile shred trucks are often heavy; intrastate light-duty box trucks may fall below thresholds. Must be confirmed against the actual fleet.
- **OSHA** — general-industry standards for plant/baler operators (machine guarding, lockout-tagout on the shredder/baler) and route drivers; recordkeeping.
- **State/local business licensing, environmental permitting** for any plant site (stormwater, baling, possible solid-waste handling registration in some jurisdictions).
- **NAID AAA certification (i-SIGMA)** — *not* a government license; a private industry standard. Frequently a contractual prerequisite for healthcare/enterprise customers. If held, it conveys with the operating entity but requires re-audit; if not held, some customers' BAAs may assume it.

Regulatory trajectory: data-privacy regulation is **tightening, not loosening** (more state privacy laws, active OCR/FTC enforcement) — which is a demand tailwind for paid destruction but also raises the stakes of an inherited compliance failure.

## LICENSE & CONTRACT-TRANSFER SUMMARY

**Operating licenses — favorable.** There is **no personal professional/occupational credential** an owner must hold to run this business; the founder profile (operate, don't hold the wrench/credential) fits cleanly. The relevant "licensing" is (i) standard local business licenses (transfer or re-issue routinely in an asset deal), (ii) **DOT operating authority/USDOT number** if applicable — in an asset purchase the buyer typically obtains its **own** USDOT number and driver-qualification program rather than "transferring" the seller's, so this is a setup task, not a transfer risk, and (iii) **NAID AAA**, which is certification of the operating entity and must be re-verified/re-audited, not personally qualified. No state contractor/professional board gates operation. This is a strong point.

**Contracts & change-of-control — the real diligence battleground.** The value is recurring auto-renewing service agreements with hundreds of B2B accounts. The questions that determine how much revenue survives:
- Do the **customer service agreements contain anti-assignment or change-of-control clauses** requiring customer consent on a sale? In an **asset deal**, contracts must generally be **assigned**, and any anti-assignment clause can require hundreds of individual consents — a practical retention risk even where customers are happy. (A **stock/equity deal** sidesteps assignment because the contracting entity is unchanged — a key structuring tension here.)
- **BAAs (HIPAA)** typically need to name the correct entity; a change in the contracting party may require re-papering BAAs with healthcare customers.
- The **real-estate lease** for any plant/yard: assignment/change-of-control clause, landlord consent, remaining term, rent reset. A plant-based operation lives or dies on its lease being assignable on fair terms.
- **Recycler/paper-offtake agreements** (baled-paper resale) — usually short-term/spot and low-stakes, but check for any exclusive or below-market locked pricing.
- **Truck/equipment financing or leases** — change-of-control/acceleration clauses; whether the buyer assumes or pays them off at close.
- **Seller non-compete** — essential and enforceable here: the seller's 25-year relationships ARE the goodwill; a strong, geographically-scoped, multi-year non-compete/non-solicit against the seller is mandatory to protect the asset purchased.

## LIABILITY & DEAL-STRUCTURE POSTURE

**Recommend an ASSET purchase, not a stock purchase** — the SBA-search default — to leave behind the seller's unknown liability tail (old data-breach claims, tax, employment, contract disputes). The trade-off is the **assignment/consent burden** on customer contracts and BAAs noted above; if that consent burden proves severe (e.g., a master contract framework that doesn't survive assignment), a structured stock/equity purchase with a robust indemnity, escrow, and tax-clearance package becomes the fallback — at the cost of inheriting the entity's history. Resolve this with counsel once contract terms are in hand.

**Successor liability that can follow the buyer even in an asset deal:** (i) **environmental** (CERCLA) for any owned/operated plant site — low for a paper-shredding operation versus a solvent/fuel business, but baling equipment, hydraulic fluids, fuel for the fleet, and prior site uses warrant a Phase I; (ii) certain **tax and unpaid payroll/sales-tax** liabilities via state successor/bulk-sales rules — obtain **state tax clearance certificates** before close; (iii) **de facto merger** doctrine if the buyer continues the same business/management/name — mitigated by clean structuring; (iv) **the data-security tail** — a destruction failure that occurred pre-close but surfaces post-close can generate claims against the going concern and reputational/customer fallout regardless of structure.

**Seller liability tail containment:** reps & warranties on (a) no data breaches / no pending OCR/FTC/state inquiries / all Certificates of Destruction validly issued, (b) tax filings and no liens, (c) employment classification and wage-hour compliance, (d) all customer contracts in good standing and assignable; an **escrow/holdback (commonly ~10% for 12–18 months)** against breaches; tax-clearance certificates; and verification of current **professional liability / cyber / general liability insurance** with tail coverage for pre-close acts.

**Personal-guarantee exposure:** SBA 7(a) requires an **unlimited personal guarantee** from each 20%+ owner, typically a lien on available personal assets, a likely **spousal guarantee** (if a spouse owns 20%+ or for collateral consent), and a **life-insurance collateral assignment** on key principals. On ~$1.6M of debt (≈90% of a ~$1.8M price), the founders are personally on the hook if the recurring base erodes faster than debt amortizes. The mitigant is the business's structural strength — recurring contracted revenue, no customer concentration (>~5% none), hard-asset collateral (trucks/bins) — which supports the DSCR cushion that makes the PG survivable. The PG is real and unavoidable; it is not reckless on a recurring-revenue, collateralized deal at ~3.5× SDE if the recurring revenue and add-backs hold up in QoE.

## TOP 3 LEGAL/REGULATORY STRENGTHS
- **Clean SBA eligibility and no owner-held credential** — plainly financeable under 13 CFR 120.110 / SOP 50 10, and nothing requires a founder to personally hold a license, perfectly matching the owner-operator (not licensed-tradesperson) profile.
- **No customer concentration and hard-asset collateral** — hundreds of accounts with none over ~5% of revenue mean no single contract's change-of-control clause can sink the deal, and trucks/bins give the SBA loan real collateral supporting the PG.
- **Asset-deal path with strong containment tools** — the seller's tail is largely escapable via asset purchase + state tax-clearance certificates + a robust, enforceable seller non-compete protecting the 25-year goodwill being bought.

## TOP 3 LEGAL/REGULATORY RISKS
- **Inherited data-security / chain-of-custody liability tail** — an undiscovered pre-close HIPAA/FACTA/GLBA breach or a pattern of defective Certificates of Destruction surfaces as a claim against the going concern and damages customer trust regardless of deal structure.
- **Change-of-control / assignment leakage** — auto-renewing customer agreements and HIPAA BAAs may require consent to assign in an asset deal; hundreds of consents are a practical retention drag, and a master contract that voids on sale would re-trade the deal.
- **Inherited employment & DOT exposure** — route drivers and plant operators carry wage-hour, I-9, workers-comp/EMR, and (if heavy/interstate trucks) FMCSA driver-qualification and drug-testing obligations the buyer must stand up from day one.

## BIGGEST SINGLE RISK
The single issue most likely to kill or seriously re-trade this deal is the **inherited data-security and chain-of-custody liability tail**, because it is the one risk where the company's core product and its largest legal exposure are the same thing. Every shred job for a medical, dental, law, or financial customer is a regulated-records destruction event governed by HIPAA's Business-Associate rules, the FACTA Disposal Rule, GLBA, and state breach statutes; the Certificate of Destruction the seller has been issuing for 25 years is both the revenue justification and a written representation that destruction occurred properly. If diligence uncovers a live or threatened breach claim, an OCR/FTC inquiry, a history of lost loads or improperly retained material, lapsed cyber/professional-liability insurance, or BAAs that were never properly executed, the buyer could inherit indemnity obligations and reputational damage that no asset-deal structure fully escapes (claims attach to the operating goodwill and customer relationships even when the entity's old liabilities are left behind). This is contained but not eliminated by reps on no-breach/no-inquiry/valid-certificates, an escrow/holdback against pre-close acts, verification of in-force cyber and professional-liability insurance with tail coverage, and a NAID AAA / process audit — all of which must clear before close.

## QUESTIONS FOR REAL COUNSEL & DUE DILIGENCE
1. **Data-security history & insurance:** Have M&A counsel and a cyber/insurance advisor confirm whether there are any past, pending, or threatened data-breach claims, OCR/FTC/state inquiries, or lost-load incidents; whether properly executed **BAAs** exist with all healthcare customers; and whether current **cyber, professional-liability, and GL policies** are in force with tail coverage for pre-close acts.
2. **Contract assignability:** Have counsel pull and read the standard customer service agreement and the largest accounts' contracts for **anti-assignment / change-of-control / auto-renewal** terms, and the **plant/yard lease** for assignment and landlord-consent provisions — then decide **asset vs. stock structure** based on the consent burden.
3. **DOT/FMCSA applicability:** Confirm fleet GVWR and whether routes are intrastate or interstate to determine whether **USDOT registration, driver-qualification files, and a drug-and-alcohol testing program** are required, and verify the seller's current compliance (or lack of it) as an inherited fix.
4. **Employment compliance:** Have a CPA/employment counsel review **worker classification (W-2 vs 1099), wage-hour, I-9/work authorization for the crew, workers'-comp EMR, and any non-competes** on key drivers/dispatchers whose departure would hurt retention.
5. **Tax & successor liability:** Obtain **state tax-clearance certificates** (sales/use and payroll) and run a bulk-sales/successor-liability check in each state of operation; have the CPA confirm SDE add-backs and any tax liens in a QoE.
6. **NAID AAA & permits:** Verify whether **NAID AAA certification** is currently held and what re-audit the ownership change triggers, and confirm any plant-site environmental/solid-waste permits and a **Phase I** for any owned or operated facility.

## RECOMMENDATION: GO (asset structure, with conditions)
From a legal/regulatory standpoint this is a financeable, operable, structurally clean acquisition with no eligibility or licensing deal-killer and no owner-credential trap — a genuine GO, **conditioned on an asset-purchase structure** (falling back to a structured equity deal only if contract assignment proves prohibitive) plus the standard containment package: reps & warranties focused on data-security/compliance history and contract assignability, a ~10%/12–18-month escrow against the pre-close liability tail, **state tax-clearance certificates**, a Phase I if a plant site is owned/operated, verification of in-force cyber and professional-liability insurance with tail coverage, NAID AAA / process confirmation, and an enforceable seller non-compete/non-solicit to protect the goodwill. The data-security liability tail and the contract change-of-control terms are the two items that could flip this to RE-TRADE (price reduction, larger escrow, or specific indemnity) if diligence finds a live breach claim or a customer contract framework that fails to survive a sale — but on the represented facts, the legal posture supports proceeding.
