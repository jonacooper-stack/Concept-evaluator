COO REVIEW — Regional Secure Document-Destruction / Shredding Company (~$2.2M rev, ~$520K SDE)

ONE-PARAGRAPH OPERATIONS READ
This is a route-density service business, which is the COO's favorite shape: the work is repeatable, scheduled, and physically simple (place a locked console, pick it up on a cadence, shred, bale, recycle, issue a certificate), so the "does the work still get done Monday after closing?" answer is genuinely yes — drivers run fixed routes whether or not the owner is in the building. The real owner-dependency is concentrated in two places, sales/new-account origination and key-account relationships, not in the daily mechanics, which is the survivable kind of dependency. The operation is visibly sleepy and manual (paper certificates, paper invoicing, no portal, whiteboard/route-by-memory scheduling, pricing untouched for years), which is exactly where the founders' edge converts to margin and capacity. The two genuine operational landmines are driver retention through the change of control and the data-security/chain-of-custody liability that a single mishandled load can detonate.

THE TRANSITION WALKTHROUGH (first 90 days post-close)
Day one, the seller must be physically present and stays present on a contracted transition — routes run on their normal schedule because they're calendar-driven, not owner-driven; the drivers show up because the trucks, the bins, and the route sheets are where they always are. The single biggest day-one exposure is the drivers: in a 4–7 person shop [ASSUMED — verify headcount from payroll/org chart], losing even one route driver mid-transition strands a quarter of the routes, so stay agreements for the drivers and the dispatcher/office manager are non-negotiable and should be signed at close (typical 90-day to 12-month stay bonus, ~$5–15K/head [ASSUMED — verify against payroll; cheap to confirm]). Week one, the founders ride the routes literally in the passenger seat to learn the physical service, the bin locations, the gate codes, and which customers care about the certificate; simultaneously they inventory every recurring account, its price, its renewal date, and its service cadence (this likely lives on paper or in the owner's head, so reconstructing it IS the first project). Month one, the top-relationship accounts get a personal handover visit from seller + new owner together — concentration is low (no customer >~5%, hundreds of accounts), so no single defection is fatal, but the medical/legal accounts that value the compliance paper trail will test the new owner and need reassurance the certificate and chain-of-custody don't lapse. The seller-training/earnout period should run a minimum of 90 days hands-on plus a 6–12 month consulting tail for sales-relationship warm handoffs and any NAID AAA audit continuity. What breaks if mishandled: a driver quits (routes stranded — caught by the stay agreement), the recycler contract or a vendor re-prices on change of control (caught in diligence), or a load gets mis-handled and a certificate can't be produced (caught only by tight SOP — see biggest risk).

STREAMLINE & MODERNIZE PLAN
In priority order, with expected gains:
1. Route-optimization + field-service software (e.g., a routing/dispatch platform with mobile driver app and digital chain-of-custody scanning). Replace whiteboard/memory routing with optimized routes and barcode-scanned bin servicing. Expected gain: 10–20% reduction in drive-time/route hours, freeing one driver-day per week to absorb new accounts without adding a truck [ASSUMED 10–20% — verify by ride-along time study + comparing current miles/route to optimized; cheap, one week of GPS logging].
2. Digital Certificate of Destruction + chain-of-custody (scan-on-pickup, scan-on-destruction, auto-emailed cert + customer portal). This is both a modernization win AND a liability-reduction win: it converts the compliance paper trail from a manual single-point-of-failure into an auditable system. Expected gain: near-elimination of "we can't find the certificate" exposure, and a portal that materially raises retention/stickiness [ASSUMED].
3. Billing/payments automation: e-invoicing + auto-pay/ACH/card-on-file replacing paper invoices. Expected gain: DSO from ~45 days to ~15–20 [ASSUMED — verify from AR aging], plus ~0.5–1 office FTE of labor freed from manual invoicing.
4. Pricing reset: prices untouched "for years" against a recurring, contracted, low-churn base with a strong inelastic compliance rationale. A 5–10% catch-up increase on auto-renew accounts flows almost entirely to SDE. Expected gain: on ~$1.6M recurring revenue, a 7% lift ≈ +$110K to the top line, most of it margin [ASSUMED 5–10% — verify by benchmarking local/national per-console pricing; cheap competitor mystery-shop].
5. e-sign auto-renewing contracts replacing handshake/paper, hardening the recurring base legally and making it diligence-clean for a future buyer.
6. Modern marketing handoff to the CMO's domain, but operationally it means a defined intake-to-install SOP so new accounts onboard without the owner.

CAPACITY MATH
Show the work. Assume the recurring base ≈ $1.6M (≈73% of $2.2M) of scheduled service [ASSUMED]. At a representative ~$40–60 per console-service event [ASSUMED — verify from invoices], that's roughly $1.6M ÷ ~$50 avg ≈ ~32,000 service events/yr, ≈ ~615/week. A shred-truck/route driver realistically completes ~20–35 stops/day [ASSUMED — verify by ride-along], call it ~25/day × 5 days = ~125 stops/driver/week. ~615 ÷ 125 ≈ ~5 effective driver-days of routing/week — consistent with a small 2–4 truck fleet running partially loaded. The binding bottleneck is route hours / driver-days, NOT facility or shred capacity (a single mobile shred truck or a modest baler plant has far more throughput than the current account base uses). That is the headroom story: route optimization (item 1) reclaims ~10–20% of driver hours = roughly half-to-one full driver-day/week of latent capacity, enough to absorb 60–125 additional weekly stops — i.e., grow the account base ~10–20% with ZERO new trucks or drivers before the next CapEx step. Beyond that, growth is cheap and linear: each incremental truck (~$150–250K for a mobile shred unit [ASSUMED — verify dealer quote]) plus one driver adds another ~125 stops/week of capacity.

TOP 3 OPERATIONAL STRENGTHS
- Calendar-driven route density means the work survives the owner's exit on day one — drivers run scheduled stops independent of ownership.
- Genuinely sleepy, paper-based operation with concrete, sequenced modernization wins (route software, digital certs/portal, e-billing, pricing reset) that convert directly to margin and latent capacity.
- Low customer concentration (no account >~5%, hundreds of accounts) so no single transition defection is fatal; revenue is recurring and contractually sticky for compliance reasons.

TOP 3 OPERATIONAL RISKS
- Driver retention through change of control: in a tiny crew, one or two departures strand a large share of routes; mitigated only by signed stay agreements at close.
- Chain-of-custody / data-security liability: a single mishandled load or missing certificate on a HIPAA/FACTA-sensitive customer is a catastrophic, reputation-and-litigation event, and today the certificate trail is manual.
- Process maturity gap: delivery and pricing live on paper and in the owner's head, so reconstructing the account/price/cadence book and the SOPs is itself a first-90-days project, not a given.

BIGGEST SINGLE RISK
The operation will not die from "the owner is the business" — routes are calendar-driven and run themselves day to day — it will die from a chain-of-custody or data-security failure on a compliance-sensitive load combined with a manual, paper certificate system that can't prove what happened. This business sells trust and an audit trail to medical practices, law firms, and accountants whose entire reason for buying is HIPAA/FACTA defensibility; the product is not "shredded paper," it is "provable, certified destruction." If a console of patient records is lost, dumped, photographed, or shredded without a retrievable Certificate of Destruction during the founders' learning-curve transition — when they don't yet know the routes, the drivers, or the SOPs — the resulting breach notification, NAID/regulatory exposure, customer flight, and potential litigation could impair the recurring base faster than any modernization can rebuild it. The manual paper certificate system is the active wound: it is a single point of failure that depends on a driver and an office clerk doing the right thing every time with no system enforcing it. This is why digital chain-of-custody (modernization item 2) is not merely an efficiency play but a survival control, and why it must be installed early rather than queued behind the revenue-facing upgrades.
