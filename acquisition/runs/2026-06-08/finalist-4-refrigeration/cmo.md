# CMO REVIEW — Commercial Refrigeration Service Company (~$3.2M rev / ~$720K SDE)

## ONE-PARAGRAPH MARKET READ
This is a genuinely durable book: the customers are grocery, c-store, restaurant, food-processor, and cold-storage operators who cannot let a walk-in or compressor rack stay down without spoiling inventory — that makes the emergency revenue non-discretionary and the PM contracts genuinely sticky, not a nicety. The real question is how much of the goodwill lives in the company (contracts, anchor accounts, the certified crew, the dispatch number people call at 2am) versus the retiring owner's personal relationships and handshake renewals — the one-pager tells us customers come from "long-standing relationships and referrals," which is both the strength and the transition risk. The modernization upside is large and unusually clean because the seller runs the business on manual scheduling and invoicing, no CRM, no field-service software, no remote monitoring, and zero digital marketing — and critically, residential-HVAC PE roll-ups largely skip commercial refrigeration, so the founders aren't walking into a bidding war or a price war. The honest read: a strong, defensible niche customer base with real owner-relationship transition risk and a wide-open, founder-executable modernization runway.

## POSITIONING & MODERNIZATION DRAFT
Re-position from "the guy you call when the cooler dies" to **"the contracted cold-chain uptime partner that keeps your inventory from spoiling — proactive monitoring, guaranteed response, no surprises."** The headline GTM shift: stop selling break-fix labor by the hour and start selling **uptime-as-a-service** — tiered PM + remote-monitoring service plans with an SLA, a real renewal motion, and a B2B sales process that targets the multi-location grocery and cold-storage operators the owner never systematically pursued. One customer-facing line: *"We watch your refrigeration 24/7 so your product never warms up — and when something does fail, we're already on the way."* That moves the company up-market, raises the contracted share of revenue, and reframes the founders' modernization (IoT monitoring) as a customer benefit, not an internal IT project.

## COMPETITIVE PICTURE
The commercial-refrigeration service niche is fragmented and meaningfully insulated from the residential-HVAC roll-up frenzy — that's the key competitive fact in the founders' favor.

- **National/regional players to know:** **Source Refrigeration & HVAC** (now part of EMCOR) and **Hill Phoenix / Hillphoenix** (Dover) on the equipment-and-service side serve big national grocery chains — they are strong on enterprise accounts but generally won't fight over a regional shop's mid-market and independent grocery/restaurant book.
- **Hussmann (Panasonic)** and **Lennox/Heatcraft** are equipment OEMs with service arms — formidable on new-store builds and national supermarket contracts, less present in local emergency-service for independents.
- **HVAC consolidators to watch but NOT fear here:** the well-funded residential-HVAC/plumbing roll-ups — **Wrench Group, Apex Service Partners, Leap Partners, Redwood Services, the various PE-backed home-services platforms** — are concentrated in *residential* HVAC/plumbing/electrical and, per the one-pager and the general pattern, **largely skip commercial refrigeration** (different certs, different customers, different sales cycle). That is exactly the "fragmented sleepy field, no consolidator bidding war" the objectives want.
- **Where the air is:** mid-market grocery, c-store chains, restaurant groups, and cold-storage/food-processing facilities that the OEMs find too small and the residential roll-ups ignore — won on response time, monitoring, and a real contracted relationship. The local sleepy competitors are other boomer-owned shops with no web presence and no monitoring — the founders out-modernize them, not out-spend a PE platform.

## TOP 3 MARKET STRENGTHS
- Non-discretionary, spoilage-driven demand with recurring PM contracts — a durable, recession-resilient book, not project lumpiness.
- Genuinely sleepy go-to-market (no CRM, no FSM software, no website/reviews, no digital) = large, cheap, founder-executable modernization wedge.
- Fragmented niche that residential-HVAC PE consolidators skip and OEMs ignore at the mid-market — no bidding war, no margin-compressing roll-up.

## TOP 3 MARKET RISKS
- The owner personally owns sales, key accounts, scheduling, and the referral relationships — goodwill-walks-with-the-seller risk.
- Skilled EPA-608 tech scarcity caps capacity: marketing can generate demand the company can't staff to serve, throttling the growth runway.
- Unverified customer concentration — a couple of anchor grocery/cold-storage accounts could be a larger share of revenue than "moderate" implies.

## BIGGEST SINGLE RISK
The single most likely thing to kill the value of this acquisition is that **the customer goodwill is really the retiring owner's personal relationships, not the company's**. The one-pager is explicit that customers come from "long-standing relationships and referrals" and that the owner personally runs sales and key accounts — which means renewals on the contracted PM base, the loyalty of the anchor grocery/cold-storage accounts, and the inbound emergency calls may all be routed through trust in *one departing person*. If those relationships don't transfer, the founders bought a customer list that erodes the moment the seller leaves, and the whole modernization thesis (which assumes a stable base to grow from) loses its footing. This is bridgeable — a 6–12 month seller-intro/earnout, formal multi-year contract renewals signed before close, transferring the dispatch line and accounts to the company brand on day one, and retaining the key dispatcher/lead tech — but it must be structured into the deal, not assumed. The score on "brand vs owner dependence" (4) reflects exactly this.

## QUESTIONS THE FOUNDERS MUST ANSWER BEFORE I'M COMFORTABLE
- What % of revenue is on a written PM/service agreement vs at-will T&M, what are the contract terms and renewal mechanics, and are contracts assignable to a new owner without re-signing?
- What is the top-customer and top-5-customer concentration, and how many of the anchor grocery/cold-storage accounts are tied to the owner's personal relationship vs a facility-level contract that survives handover?
- What is the named transition plan — seller-intro length, earnout/standby-note structure, and which key staff (lead dispatcher, senior EPA-608 techs) are being retained — and is the owner contractually introducing the buyer to every anchor account before close?
- How does the company's current online presence (website, Google Business Profile, review count) compare to the strongest modern competitor in the metro, and what are incumbent PM contract prices vs what the market will bear for a monitored tier?

## RECOMMENDATION: GO
This is a strong buy-side candidate on the marketing/customer lens: durable non-discretionary demand, a recurring contracted base, a fragmented niche the PE roll-ups skip, and an unusually clean, founder-executable modernization wedge (FSM software, IoT monitoring service plans, B2B digital lead-gen) that plays directly to the founders' edge. The GO is conditioned on de-risking owner-relationship transfer: the deal must include a real seller-intro/earnout, pre-close renewal of anchor PM contracts to the company entity, transfer of the dispatch line/accounts to the company brand, and retention of key techs/dispatch — and diligence must verify contracted-revenue share and customer concentration. If contracted share turns out low and the anchors prove to be the owner's personal handshakes with no transferable contracts, this moves to RE-TRADE on price and structure (heavier earnout, lower multiple). On the marketing thesis alone, it's a clear GO.
