# LEGAL / REGULATORY REVIEW — Commercial Refrigeration Service Company
(Not formal legal advice — I am an experienced deal operator flagging landmines, not a licensed attorney. Every issue below must be taken to a real M&A attorney, a CPA, and an SBA lender before reliance.)

## ONE-PARAGRAPH RISK READ
This is, on the legal/regulatory axis, one of the cleaner buy-side profiles I see: a for-profit US operating field-service company in a lightly-regulated trade where the central credential (EPA Section 608 technician certification) is held by *employees*, not the owner, and is therefore hireable/retainable rather than a non-bridgeable founder credential. The deal's real legal risks are not eligibility or licensing — they are (a) revenue leakage through change-of-control consent on the recurring PM service agreements and the anchor grocery/cold-storage accounts, (b) an environmental/refrigerant-handling compliance tail under the Clean Air Act that, if the seller has been sloppy, can attach to the buyer, and (c) classic field-service employment exposure (overtime/on-call pay, workers' comp EMR, I-9 status of the crew). All three are knowable in diligence and containable with an asset-purchase structure, escrow/indemnity, and pre-close compliance audits; none reads as a deal-killer on the facts presented. Customer-concentration in the anchor accounts is the swing variable that decides whether this is a strong GO or a RE-TRADE.

## REGULATORY & SBA-ELIGIBILITY MAP
**SBA eligibility:** Clean. This is a for-profit, US-based, operating service business — not passive, not a real-estate holder, not on SBA's ineligible list. It sits comfortably under SBA size standards (NAICS 238220, plumbing/heating/AC contractors, or 811310, commercial machinery repair — both carry receipts-based size standards far above ~$3.2M revenue). A standard SBA 7(a) "complete change of ownership" structure (100% buy, full PG from 20%+ owners, seller note on standby permissible toward the equity injection) fits without contortion. No ownership/citizenship red flags are presented; confirm buyer citizenship/PR status as a checkbox item.

**The regulatory regimes that actually apply day-to-day:**
- **EPA Clean Air Act Section 608 (40 CFR Part 82, Subpart F)** — the central regime. Technicians who maintain/service/repair/dispose of appliances containing regulated refrigerants must hold EPA 608 certification (Type II/Universal for high-pressure commercial systems). The *company* must follow refrigerant-handling rules: recovery/recycling, leak-repair thresholds for appliances with a 50+ lb charge (large compressor racks and cold-storage systems routinely exceed this), recordkeeping of refrigerant added/recovered, and proper cylinder/refrigerant management. Recovery equipment must be certified. Violations carry civil penalties up to ~$50,000+ per day per violation [ASSUMED current CAA penalty figure — verify with counsel; EPA escalates these]. **The AIM Act (2020) and EPA's HFC phasedown** are tightening the regime: HFC production/consumption is being stepped down, and EPA's **technician-transition and refrigerant-management rules (40 CFR Part 84 + revised 608, "ERP" rule, 2024)** push toward leak inspection, automatic leak detection on large systems, and reclaimed-refrigerant requirements. This is a *tightening* trajectory — relevant to both compliance cost and to the modernization/IoT leak-monitoring upside.
- **State/local mechanical or HVAC-R contractor licensing** — many states (and some municipalities) require a contractor's license to perform commercial refrigeration/mechanical work, typically held by a "qualifying party"/master mechanic who can be an employee, not the owner. Burden: moderate, state-dependent.
- **DOT/FMCSA** — if any service vehicle exceeds 10,001 lbs GVWR or the company hauls refrigerant cylinders/compressed gas above threshold quantities, DOT registration, hazmat transport rules (49 CFR), and possibly driver/medical-card requirements apply. Most light service trucks fall under thresholds; verify the fleet.
- **OSHA** — field crews working on pressurized systems, refrigerants (asphyxiation/frostbite hazards), ladders, electrical, and lockout/tagout. Standard general-industry exposure; check the OSHA-300 logs and any citations.
- **DOL/state wage-hour** — overtime and on-call/after-hours pay for emergency techs (see Employment below).

## LICENSE & CONTRACT-TRANSFER SUMMARY
**Licenses/permits:** The defining favorable fact is that **EPA 608 certification is individual to each technician and is held by the existing crew** — it does not "transfer" with the business and it does *not* require the owner to qualify. The founders never need to hold it; they retain the certified crew and hire/certify replacements (608 is exam-based and bridgeable in weeks). Any state **contractor/mechanical license** is the watch item: in many jurisdictions it is held by a "qualifying individual" who must be a bona fide employee/officer — if the *retiring owner* is currently the qualifying party, his departure can leave the company unlicensed until a qualifying employee or new hire is substituted with the state board. This is **must-resolve-before-closing**, not fatal: identify the current qualifying party and confirm a crew member (likely a lead tech) can be named, or budget to recruit one.

**Contracts & change-of-control:** The recurring PM **service agreements** are the crown jewels and the leak point. In an **asset purchase**, contracts do not automatically assign — each PM agreement and each anchor account may require counterparty **consent to assignment** or contain a **change-of-control / termination-on-sale** clause, giving large grocery and cold-storage customers a window to re-bid, re-price, or walk. Vendor/parts-supplier and refrigerant-distributor accounts, the shop/yard **real-estate lease** (assignment clause + landlord consent), and any fleet leases or financing all need assignment review. **Must-resolve:** obtain assignments/consents on the anchor accounts (the ones that, per the one-pager, "may be meaningful") as closing conditions.

## LIABILITY & DEAL-STRUCTURE POSTURE
**Structure — asset purchase, strongly preferred.** An asset deal lets the founders cherry-pick assets (trucks, tools, parts inventory, contracts, goodwill, customer list) and **leave behind** the seller's corporate liability shell, historical tax exposure, and most pre-close claims. The trade-off is the contract-assignment friction above — acceptable and worth it.

**Successor liability that follows you anyway** (the part I never wave off): even in an asset deal, certain liabilities can attach to the buyer — **environmental/CERCLA** exposure if there's contamination at an owned/leased site, **de facto merger / mere continuation** doctrines if the business continues unchanged under the same name/crew (it will), certain **successor employment** liabilities, and in some states **bulk-sales / unpaid sales-and-use-tax** successor liability. The environmental angle matters here specifically because refrigeration shops handle refrigerants, oils, and solvents and often have a yard: **a Phase I ESA on any owned or leased site is mandatory**, with Phase II if it flags. Refrigerant mismanagement (improper venting/recovery) is a *regulatory* tail under CAA §608 that can convert into EPA enforcement — diligence the seller's refrigerant logs and any EPA correspondence.

**Seller liability tail to contain:** pending/threatened litigation (warranty claims on failed installs that spoiled a customer's inventory is the signature claim in this trade — spoiled-product/business-interruption exposure), unpaid payroll/sales tax, workers' comp claims, and undisclosed warranty obligations on prior PM work. **Containment:** reps & warranties on compliance/litigation/tax, an **escrow/holdback (10–15% for 12–18 months)** [ASSUMED market terms — verify with M&A counsel], **tax-clearance certificates** from the state, and a seller **non-compete + non-solicit** (enforceable against a retiring owner in most states; confirm state law and reasonable scope/duration).

**Personal-guarantee exposure:** The SBA 7(a) loan requires an **unlimited personal guarantee** from every 20%+ owner, very likely a lien on personal assets, possibly a **spousal guarantee** (where a spouse holds an interest or in community-property states), and a **collateral assignment of life insurance** on the key principals. On ~$2.5M debt this is real, full-recourse exposure. What mitigates it: a healthy DSCR cushion (the CFO's job, but the SDE/debt-service spread on this profile looks supportive), a seller note on standby absorbing part of the structure, and adequate transition/working-capital reserves so the deal doesn't stumble in year one. The PG is the founders' single largest personal-risk item and should be stress-tested against a churn-of-anchor-accounts downside.

## TOP 3 LEGAL/REGULATORY STRENGTHS
- **The critical credential is employee-held and bridgeable.** EPA Section 608 (40 CFR Part 82, Subpart F) certification sits with the technicians, not the owner — it does not require the founders to qualify and replacements can be certified in weeks, removing the single most common field-service deal-killer (a non-transferable owner credential).
- **Textbook SBA eligibility.** A for-profit US operating service company under NAICS 238220/811310 size standards, not passive or on the ineligible list — a clean 7(a) change-of-ownership with no structural gymnastics.
- **B2B with negligible privacy/consumer exposure.** No consumer PII/PCI/auto-renewal-law surface (CARD Act/state auto-renew statutes don't bite), so the modernization push into CRM/marketing carries only ordinary TCPA/CAN-SPAM hygiene, not a regulated-data minefield.

## TOP 3 LEGAL/REGULATORY RISKS
- **Change-of-control leakage on the recurring contracts and anchor accounts** — the very revenue that makes this attractive can be re-bid or walked at closing if PM agreements carry assignment/termination-on-sale clauses (asset deal).
- **Refrigerant/environmental compliance tail under the Clean Air Act §608 / AIM Act**, plus possible site contamination — an EPA enforcement or CERCLA exposure that successor-liability doctrines can attach to the buyer despite an asset structure.
- **Inherited field-labor exposure** — FLSA overtime/on-call-pay treatment for emergency techs, I-9/work-authorization status of the crew, and a workers'-comp EMR that prices forward insurance.

## BIGGEST SINGLE RISK
The deal's most likely re-trade trigger is **change-of-control / assignment risk on the recurring PM service agreements and the anchor grocery and cold-storage accounts.** The entire investment thesis rests on durable, recurring, contracted revenue; but in the asset purchase that the liability profile demands, those contracts do not transfer automatically. If the meaningful anchor accounts (the one-pager itself flags concentration as unverified — "a few anchor grocery/cold-storage accounts may be meaningful") hold consent-to-assignment or termination-on-change-of-control clauses, those customers get a clean window at closing to re-bid the work, demand price concessions from a new and unproven owner, or leave — and because the same window coincides with the retiring owner (who *is* the relationship) walking away, the timing is maximally adverse. A 15–25% post-close churn of contracted revenue would simultaneously hit the DSCR that secures the founders' full personal guarantee. This is not fatal — it is the textbook reason to (1) make written assignment/consent on the top accounts a *closing condition*, (2) tie a meaningful slice of price to a post-close revenue-retention holdback or earnout, and (3) keep the seller engaged through a transition/consulting agreement with a non-compete — but it is the single issue most likely to move price or terms.

## QUESTIONS FOR REAL COUNSEL & DUE DILIGENCE
1. **Contract assignment audit:** Pull every PM/service agreement and the top 5–10 accounts and have M&A counsel flag assignment, consent-to-assign, change-of-control, and termination-on-sale clauses. Which anchor accounts can walk or re-price at closing, and what % of revenue do they represent? Make consent on the material ones a closing condition.
2. **Contractor "qualifying party" confirmation:** Confirm with the relevant state mechanical/HVAC-R contractor board whether a license is required, who currently holds it as qualifying party (owner vs. an employee), and whether a retained lead technician can be substituted as qualifier before/at closing so the company is never unlicensed.
3. **CAA §608 / refrigerant-compliance and environmental review:** Have counsel/EHS diligence the seller's refrigerant recovery/leak-repair recordkeeping (40 CFR Part 82), any EPA notices or enforcement history, and AIM Act/HFC-phasedown readiness; commission a **Phase I ESA** on any owned or leased site (yard, shop) with Phase II if flagged, and scope CERCLA/contamination exposure.
4. **Employment diligence:** Review worker classification (W-2 vs 1099), FLSA overtime and on-call/after-hours pay practices for emergency techs, I-9/work-authorization files for the crew, the workers'-comp claims history and EMR, and any EEOC/wage-hour claims.
5. **PG / tax / structure:** Confirm with the SBA lender the exact personal-guarantee terms (spousal guarantee? life-insurance collateral assignment?), and with the CPA pull state sales/use-tax and payroll-tax standing plus tax-clearance certificates to size the escrow and indemnity.

## RECOMMENDATION: GO (conditional — structure-dependent)
On the legal/regulatory axis this clears cleanly: SBA-eligible, the gating credential is employee-held and bridgeable, B2B with negligible privacy exposure, and a workable asset-purchase path. Proceed — but as a **structured GO** contingent on diligence: do this as an **asset purchase**; make written **assignment/consent on the material PM and anchor accounts a closing condition** and tie part of the price to a **revenue-retention holdback or earnout**; require **R&W coverage, a 10–15% escrow (12–18 months), and state tax-clearance certificates** against the seller's tail; commission a **Phase I ESA** and a **CAA §608 refrigerant-compliance audit** before closing; substitute the **contractor qualifying party** to a retained employee; and lock a **seller transition/consulting agreement with a non-compete**. If the anchor-account consent or environmental diligence comes back ugly, this converts to a **RE-TRADE** on price/escrow rather than a kill.
