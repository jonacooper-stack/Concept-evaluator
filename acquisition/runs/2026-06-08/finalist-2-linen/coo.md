COO REVIEW — Independent Uniform & Linen Rental-Route Business (~$2.8M rev / ~$650K SDE)

ONE-PARAGRAPH OPERATIONS READ
This is a route-density business whose real asset is hundreds of small recurring B2B contracts serviced on a fixed weekly cadence — and the operation does survive the owner's exit IF the route sales reps (RSRs) stay, because the day-to-day work (pick up soiled, drop clean, retain the stop) lives in the trucks, not the owner's office. The genuine owner-dependency is concentrated and dangerous in two places: the owner "historically handles big accounts and pricing," which is exactly the salesperson + pricing-authority + key-relationship triad I distrust. But the modernization upside is large and concrete: paper route sheets, no routing software, no customer portal, no CRM, and pricing frozen for years in a business where a 3–5% annual price escalator is industry-standard and was simply never taken. The flip side is real operational weight — this is not an asset-light service; if there is an in-house laundry plant, the founders inherit industrial wash equipment, boilers, water/wastewater compliance, fleet, and a textile-inventory working-capital cycle, all of which are heavier to run than a dispatch-only trade.

THE TRANSITION WALKTHROUGH (first 90 days post-close)
Day one: the seller must still be present — a pure cliff-edge handover here is unacceptable because pricing logic and the "big account" relationships are undocumented and in his head. The route trucks roll Monday morning regardless of who owns the company; the RSRs know their stops, their items-in-service counts, and which customers complain — that is the part that does NOT break on close, and it is the load-bearing reason this is acquirable at all. Week one: the founders ride routes (literally sit in the passenger seat of each RSR's truck) to learn stop sequence, item counts, and which accounts are fragile. The first thing that breaks is at the plant or wash relationship — if laundering is outsourced, the change-of-control wash contract is a single point of failure that can re-price; if in-house, an equipment failure (boiler, tunnel washer, ironer) in month one with no documented maintenance vendor is a service-outage risk. Month one: a top "big three" account the owner personally held tests the new owner — calls about a billing question or a quality miss and listens for whether anyone competent answers. The fix is a 6–12 month seller transition with the seller making warm introductions to the top 15–20 accounts by revenue, plus stay agreements for every RSR (the RSRs ARE the customer relationships; an RSR who quits can take 10–30 stops to a competitor or the majors). The seller-training period must explicitly transfer the pricing book (per-item rates, escalator history, contract renewal dates) — without that, the founders cannot raise prices without flying blind. Month two to three: founders begin the modernization install in parallel, but do NOT touch pricing or routing until they have ridden every route and mapped every contract renewal date.

STREAMLINE & MODERNIZE PLAN
In order of cash-on-cash payback:
1. Take the price escalator. Pricing "hasn't been raised in years." Most linen/uniform service contracts permit an annual CPI or fixed 3–5% increase; the owner simply never invoked it. A one-time 5–8% catch-up across ~90% recurring revenue is the single highest-ROI move. [ASSUMED gain: +$110K–$200K to revenue, most of it flowing to SDE, at ~5–8% on ~$2.5M recurring] — verify by reading the actual contract escalator clauses and the rate card during diligence. Watch churn: stage increases by contract renewal date, not all at once.
2. Routing software (Roadnet/WorkWave/RouteManager). Replaces paper route sheets, optimizes stop sequence, captures proof-of-service digitally. [ASSUMED gain: 8–15% fewer route miles/hours, freeing ~0.5–1 driver-day/week per route for new-stop selling] — verify by GPS-logging current routes for 2 weeks vs. an optimized plan. Cost: ~$150–300/truck/month.
3. Field-service/billing system + customer portal + digital invoicing/ACH (e.g., ABS, SmartLinen, or a uniform-rental-specific ERP). Kills paper invoicing, speeds DSO, lets customers self-serve item adds/changes. [ASSUMED gain: DSO from ~45 to ~30 days, freeing working capital; 2–5% upsell from portal add-ons] — verify with current AR aging.
4. CRM + structured upsell (mats, restroom, FR garments, mat-exchange frequency). Turns referral/route-knock growth into a managed pipeline. [ASSUMED gain: 3–6% organic stop growth/yr] — verify by counting current penetration (items-per-stop) vs. industry benchmark.
5. Loss/replacement charge discipline. Sleepy operators under-bill for lost/abused garments. Tightening the count-and-charge process recovers margin. [ASSUMED gain: 1–3% of revenue] — verify by auditing replacement-charge rate vs. textile shrinkage.

AVERAGE SCORE: 5.5 / 10

TOP 3 OPERATIONAL STRENGTHS
- Recurring, weekly, contracted route revenue (~90%+) with deep stop density — the daily work survives the owner's exit because it lives in the RSRs and the route cadence, not the office.
- A rare, quantified pricing lever: years of un-taken escalators on ~$2.5M of recurring revenue, a +$110K–$200K SDE move that requires zero new customers and only contract-terms diligence to confirm.
- Latent capacity without CapEx: routing optimization frees ~half a truck-day/week per route and the plant is likely single-shift, so growth is sales-limited, not asset-limited.

TOP 3 OPERATIONAL RISKS
- RSR flight risk: the route reps hold the customer relationships; even one or two leaving on close can defect 10–30 stops each to UniFirst/Cintas/Aramark or a local rival — no stay agreements are in place.
- Owner-held pricing and big accounts: pricing logic and the largest relationships are undocumented and in the seller's head; lose them on a cliff handover and both revenue retention and the modernization pricing play are crippled.
- Plant/wash dependency: an in-house laundry means inherited industrial equipment, boilers, chemicals, and water/wastewater compliance; an outsourced wash means a change-of-control contract that can re-price the entire cost structure.

BIGGEST SINGLE RISK
The deal lives or dies on route-sales-rep retention through transition. In linen and uniform rental, the RSR is simultaneously the driver, the account manager, the upsell engine, and the human face the customer trusts — the customer relationship is functionally portable in the RSR's head, not locked to the company. If two or three RSRs read the ownership change as their moment to leave (or are recruited by the majors, who actively poach experienced route reps), they can each walk 10–30 stops out the door, and because the owner himself held the "big accounts," there is no GM or documented relationship layer to backstop the loss. A 15% stop defection on a $2.8M book is a ~$420K revenue hit that simultaneously vaporizes the DSCR cushion and the modernization thesis. This is why the deal must be structured with RSR stay-bonuses (named, dollar-quantified) and a long seller transition that converts the owner's personal big-account relationships into company relationships before he leaves.

QUESTIONS THE FOUNDERS MUST ANSWER BEFORE I'M COMFORTABLE
- Who are the RSRs, how long has each been on their route, what is their comp, and will each sign a stay agreement (with a named retention bonus) — and is there ANY #2/GM/dispatcher who holds the operation together, or is it owner + RSRs only?
- Is laundering in-house or outsourced? If in-house: age/condition of the tunnel washer, boilers, and ironers; deferred-maintenance and CapEx schedule; water/wastewater discharge permits and any environmental exposure. If outsourced: the wash contract's change-of-control and pricing terms and its remaining term.
- What do the contracts actually say about price escalation and renewal, and what is the documented rate card vs. current effective pricing — i.e., is the +$110K–$200K escalator lever contractually real, and what are the contract renewal dates so increases can be staged without triggering churn?

RECOMMENDATION: RE-TRADE
The operation is genuinely acquirable and the modernization upside (especially frozen pricing) is real and quantified — but the structure must protect the two fragile points before this is a GO. Conditions: (1) a 6–12 month paid seller transition with contracted warm introductions to the top ~20 accounts by revenue and full handover of the pricing book and contract calendar; (2) signed stay agreements with named retention bonuses for every RSR through at least the first renewal cycle; (3) full plant/wash diligence — equipment condition and environmental permits if in-house, or change-of-control terms if outsourced — with price/holdback adjusted for any deferred CapEx or re-pricing risk surfaced. With those terms, this becomes a GO; without RSR retention locked and the pricing book transferred, it is a NO-GO regardless of headline SDE.
