# LEGAL / REGULATORY REVIEW — Independent Uniform & Linen Rental-Route Business
(Not formal legal advice — these are deal landmines to brief an M&A attorney, a CPA, and an SBA lender on. I am an experienced deal operator flagging issues, not a licensed attorney resolving them.)

## ONE-PARAGRAPH RISK READ
This is a fundamentally **SBA-eligible, lightly-licensed operating business** with a clean financing path — no professional credential gates operation, and the founders managing an inherited route/plant crew is exactly the owner-operator model SBA 7(a) supports. The two real legal stories here are (1) **change-of-control risk in the multi-year auto-renewing customer contracts** (the whole revenue thesis is that they're sticky and assignable — if they contain anti-assignment or "consent on sale" clauses, revenue can leak or re-price at closing) and (2) the **environmental tail of an in-house laundry plant** (industrial wastewater discharge, solvent/soil residue from mechanic and shop-towel laundering, possible legacy contamination if real estate is owned or leased long-term). Both are knowable in diligence and structurable, but they are where this deal re-trades, not where it dies. Net posture: a clean, financeable asset-deal candidate with two specific diligence sweeps required before closing.

## REGULATORY & SBA-ELIGIBILITY MAP
**SBA eligibility: clearly eligible.** This is a for-profit, US-based, active operating business (textile rental + laundering services) — not a passive/real-estate-holding company, not on the SBA ineligible-business list (13 CFR 120.110), comfortably within SBA size standards for a ~$2.8M-revenue laundry/linen-supply firm (NAICS 812332, Industrial Launderers — size standard is in the tens of $M of receipts, so this is well under). A full change of ownership ("100% buy") satisfies SOP 50 10 change-of-ownership rules; a seller note can count toward the equity injection only if on **full standby for the loan term** (SBA SOP requirement) — flag, don't assume.

**Industry regimes that apply day-to-day:**
- **EPA / state environmental (the big one):** Clean Water Act / state POTW pretreatment permits for industrial laundry wastewater discharge; RCRA implications for solvents and hazardous residue laundered out of mechanic uniforms and shop towels (oil, grease, metals). Industrial launderers are a regulated wastewater discharger category.
- **DOT / FMCSA:** the delivery fleet — if any vehicle is >10,001 lbs GVWR, DOT number, driver qualification files, hours-of-service, and DOT physicals apply; most route box trucks fall in/around this line. State CDL rules for the larger trucks.
- **OSHA:** plant machinery (washers, extractors, dryers, ironers/flatwork), lockout-tagout, chemical handling (HazCom/SDS), and field/route safety.
- **DOL Wage & Hour (FLSA):** route sales representatives are a classic overtime-exemption gray zone (outside-sales vs. non-exempt drivers) and a frequent litigation target in route-based businesses.
- **State/local:** business licenses, plant operating permits, commercial vehicle registration, weights, and possibly air permits for boilers/dryers.

This is **moderately regulated** — heavier than a pure B2B service firm because of the plant and the fleet, lighter than healthcare/finance. Trajectory is stable, with environmental enforcement the only meaningfully tightening vector.

## LICENSE & CONTRACT-TRANSFER SUMMARY
**Licenses/permits — no owner-credential gate (key positive).** No professional or trade license requires an *owner* to personally qualify. The required authorizations are **business/facility-level**: the wastewater discharge/pretreatment permit, any air permit, the DOT operating authority, and standard local business licenses. These attach to the entity/facility and are **renewable/re-issuable to new ownership** rather than tied to an individual's credential — though several (especially environmental discharge permits and DOT authority) often require **re-application or formal transfer/notification on change of control**, not automatic carryover. A licensed *employee* model isn't even needed here, which removes the single most common acquisition kill-switch.

**Contracts — this is the diligence hotspot.** The entire value thesis rests on "multi-year auto-renewing rental-service contracts." Industry-standard linen/uniform rental agreements (Cintas/Aramark/UniFirst paper, which independents often mimic) typically *favor the supplier* and are *assignable by the supplier* — but you cannot assume the independent's homegrown contracts match. You must read the actual forms for: (a) **anti-assignment / change-of-control clauses** that give the customer a consent right or exit on sale; (b) **auto-renewal enforceability** under state auto-renewal statutes (e.g., California ARL, NY, Illinois) — B2B is often exempt, but contracts and notices must comply; (c) liquidated-damages/minimum-charge provisions (these are what make the contracts "sticky" and are sometimes challenged). The **real-estate lease** (if the plant is leased) almost certainly contains an assignment/consent clause — landlord consent and lease term/condition are gating items. Vendor wash arrangement (if outsourced laundering) and the fleet financing/leases also need assignment review.

## LIABILITY & DEAL-STRUCTURE POSTURE
**Recommend an asset purchase, not stock.** An asset deal lets the buyer take the textile inventory, fleet, plant equipment, customer contracts, and goodwill while leaving behind most of the seller's liability tail — but I am allergic to "asset deal so we inherit nothing": **successor liability still reaches the buyer** for (1) **environmental/CERCLA** liability on any owned or operated contaminated site (a Phase I, and likely Phase II given a laundry plant, is mandatory and the most important single diligence spend), (2) certain **employment and unpaid payroll/sales-tax** obligations, and (3) **de facto merger / bulk-sales** exposure where the buyer continues the same business with the same crew and customers — which is exactly the plan here. So the asset structure helps but does not fully wall off the plant's environmental and tax tail.

**Seller liability tail to contain:** pending FLSA/wage-hour or worker-classification claims on route reps; warranty/loss-charge billing disputes; unpaid sales/use tax on rental revenue (rental of tangible personal property is taxable in most states — a classic small-business sales-tax exposure if the seller under-collected); environmental notices of violation on plant discharge. Contain via **escrow/holdback (10–15% for 12–18 months), robust reps & warranties (especially environmental, tax, employment, and contract-assignability reps), tax-clearance certificates from the state, and a seller indemnity backed by the standby seller note** (which doubles as a clawback source).

**Personal-guarantee exposure:** SBA 7(a) requires an **unlimited personal guarantee from every 20%+ owner** (both founders), typically a **lien on personal assets, possible spousal guarantee, and a life-insurance collateral assignment**. On ~$2.3M of debt this is real but the collateral base is favorable — laundry equipment, trucks, and **rental textile inventory in service** are pledgeable, reducing reliance on personal collateral, and the [ASSUMED] ~$650K SDE against a 10-yr/~10.5% note should produce a comfortable DSCR cushion that lowers the practical PG risk. Verify the DSCR with real numbers before treating the PG as "manageable."

## TOP 3 LEGAL/REGULATORY STRENGTHS
- **No owner-personal credential gate** — operation depends on facility/entity-level permits (wastewater, DOT, local business license), not a transferable professional/trade license the founders would have to personally qualify for; removes the single most common acquisition kill-switch.
- **Clean SBA-eligibility profile** — for-profit US operating business, off the 13 CFR 120.110 ineligible list, well within the NAICS 812332 industrial-launderer size standard, with hard pledgeable collateral (laundry equipment, trucks, rental textile inventory) that strengthens the financing and softens the personal guarantee.
- **Asset-deal structure is available** and the customer base is fragmented/low-concentration (hundreds of small accounts), so there is no single-counterparty contract whose change-of-control veto can sink the whole deal.

## TOP 3 LEGAL/REGULATORY RISKS
- **Environmental tail of the laundry plant** — industrial wastewater discharge plus solvent/oil/metal residue from laundering mechanic uniforms and shop towels creates Clean Water Act/RCRA/CERCLA exposure that successor-liability and a Phase I/II must address.
- **Change-of-control / assignability in the customer contracts and plant lease** — the "sticky multi-year auto-renew" revenue is only as transferable as the actual contract language allows; consent-on-sale clauses would leak or re-price revenue at closing.
- **Inherited employment exposure** — route-sales-rep FLSA overtime classification, DOT driver-qualification compliance on the fleet, and I-9 status of plant/route labor are live wage-hour and immigration landmines in route-based businesses.

## BIGGEST SINGLE RISK
The **environmental tail of an in-house laundry plant** is the issue most likely to seriously re-trade or kill this deal. Industrial launderers that wash heavily-soiled mechanic uniforms and shop towels discharge oils, greases, solvents, and metals into wastewater and generate residue that triggers Clean Water Act POTW pretreatment permitting and potential RCRA hazardous-waste handling — and under **CERCLA, environmental liability follows the operator/owner regardless of an asset-purchase structure**, so a buyer cannot contract out of contamination that a Phase I/Phase II uncovers on owned or long-operated property. A legacy soil or groundwater problem at the plant site can run into six or seven figures of remediation and is not reliably covered by escrow or seller indemnity if the seller lacks the assets to stand behind it. This is precisely why the asset deal "I inherit nothing" instinct is dangerous here. The mitigants are knowable — a mandatory Phase I (and Phase II if any flag), an environmental rep & warranty backed by escrow and/or environmental insurance, and confirmation of current discharge-permit compliance — but until that sweep is done, the plant's environmental status is the deal's largest unpriced unknown. (If laundering is fully **outsourced** to a third-party wash, this risk drops materially — confirm which model this target runs.)

## QUESTIONS FOR REAL COUNSEL & DUE DILIGENCE
1. **Environmental:** Commission a Phase I ESA on the laundry plant/site (and Phase II if flagged); confirm current POTW industrial-wastewater pretreatment permit status, any notices of violation, RCRA generator status, and whether laundering is in-house or outsourced — and require an environmental rep/indemnity backed by escrow or environmental insurance.
2. **Contract assignability:** Have M&A counsel read the actual customer rental-service agreement forms for anti-assignment / change-of-control / consent-on-sale clauses, auto-renewal-statute compliance, and liquidated-damages enforceability; and review the plant lease for landlord consent, term, and condition. Quantify what share of revenue sits under contracts that survive assignment.
3. **Employment classification:** Have counsel/CPA assess FLSA overtime treatment of route sales reps (outside-sales exemption vs. non-exempt drivers), DOT driver-qualification and hours-of-service compliance on the fleet, I-9/work-authorization files for plant and route crew, and any open wage-hour or workers'-comp/EMR exposure — with reps and a holdback covering pre-closing liability.
4. **Tax & structure:** Confirm sales/use tax has been correctly collected and remitted on rental revenue (rental of tangible personal property is taxable in most states); obtain state tax-clearance certificates; confirm SBA standby treatment of any seller note; and confirm DSCR off audited/QoE SDE before relying on the personal-guarantee cushion.

## RECOMMENDATION: RE-TRADE
**RE-TRADE (conditional GO).** This is a clean, SBA-eligible, owner-operator-friendly business with no fatal license or eligibility defect — the kind of target this thesis is built for — but it carries two specific, structurable risks that should shape price and terms rather than stop the deal. Structure as an **asset purchase**, condition closing on a **Phase I (and Phase II if flagged) ESA with an environmental rep/indemnity backed by escrow or environmental insurance**, on **assignability confirmation across the customer-contract base and a landlord lease-consent**, and on **state tax-clearance certificates plus an employment-classification review**. Hold back **10–15% of price in escrow for 12–18 months** with environmental, tax, employment, and contract-assignability reps, and use the **standby seller note as an additional indemnity backstop**. If the Phase I comes back clean (or laundering is outsourced) and the contract forms prove assignable, this converts cleanly to GO; if material contamination or broad consent-on-sale clauses surface, re-price or walk.
