# LEGAL / REGULATORY REVIEW — Portable-Sanitation Rental Company

(Not formal legal advice — I am an experienced deal operator flagging landmines, not a licensed attorney. Every issue below must be taken to a real M&A attorney, a CPA, and an SBA lender before reliance.)

## ONE-PARAGRAPH RISK READ

This is a clean SBA-eligible operating business with no professional/trade-license credential gate — the founders do NOT need to personally hold a wrench-and-credential, which clears the doc-01 hard constraint cleanly. The legal risk here is NOT eligibility or licensing; it is (a) the environmental/wastewater tail inherent in vacuum-trucking, transporting, and disposing of human septage, (b) DOT/FMCSA fleet compliance and CDL-driver safety exposure inherited with the trucks, and (c) the seller's quiet liability tail — informal contractor accounts, possible 1099 misclassification of drivers, and unrecorded disposal/spill history. None of these are fatal; all are knowable through targeted diligence and containable through an asset deal with environmental and tax-clearance protections, but the septage-disposal and DOT exposures are real and recurring, not one-time. This is a RE-TRADE-able GO posture: structure it right and the risk is manageable.

## REGULATORY & SBA-ELIGIBILITY MAP

**SBA eligibility: ELIGIBLE.** A for-profit, US-based operating service-and-rental company is squarely within SBA 7(a) parameters. It is not on the SBA ineligible-business list (13 CFR 120.110) — it is not passive, not a real-estate holding company, not a lender, not speculative, not illegal under federal law. Portable-sanitation rental falls under NAICS 562991 (Septic Tank and Related Services) or 532490/562998 depending on classification; the SBA size standard for these (roughly $9M–$47.5M average annual receipts depending on exact NAICS) easily accommodates a ~$2.5M-revenue target. The hard-asset fleet (units + vacuum trucks) is genuine collateral that strengthens the loan. A full change-of-ownership (100% buyout of a retiring owner) with the standard ~10% injection (seller note can stand in for part, on full standby), 10-year goodwill term, and 20%+ owner personal guarantees satisfies SBA structure. **No eligibility landmine.**

**Industry regulatory regimes that apply day-to-day:**
- **EPA / state environmental + wastewater disposal (the central regime).** Vacuum trucks collect human waste ("septage") and must dispose of it at a permitted POTW (publicly owned treatment works) or licensed land-application site under the federal Clean Water Act and EPA 40 CFR Part 503 (biosolids/septage standards), as administered by state environmental agencies (e.g., state DEQ/DEP). Septage haulers typically need a **state septage-hauler / waste-transporter permit or registration** and must keep disposal manifests/records. This is the most consequential ongoing regime.
- **DOT / FMCSA (the fleet).** Vacuum service trucks and delivery trucks are commercial motor vehicles. The company needs a **USDOT number**, likely **CDL drivers** (depending on GVWR — many vacuum trucks exceed 26,001 lbs), DOT medical cards, hours-of-service and driver-qualification files, drug-and-alcohol testing program (49 CFR Part 382), vehicle inspection/maintenance records (49 CFR Part 396), and possibly **hazmat considerations** if waste classification triggers them (generally domestic septage is not hazmat, but disposal/manifest rules still bite). If any operation crosses state lines, full interstate FMCSA applies; intrastate-only still triggers state DOT equivalents.
- **OSHA (field + yard crews).** Confined-space, biological-hazard/bloodborne-pathogen exposure (29 CFR 1910.1030) from servicing units, chemical handling (deodorizers/biocides), lockout-tagout on trucks, and general industry standards. EMR and workers'-comp posture matter — sanitation field work carries injury exposure.
- **State/local business licensing + commercial vehicle registration/IRP/IFTA** for the fleet, plus possible local permits for the yard/disposal staging.

**Bottom line on the map:** lightly-to-moderately regulated. No regime requires the OWNER to personally hold a professional credential — this is an operations-and-compliance burden carried by the company and its drivers, not a personal-license gate. That is the key favorable fact.

## LICENSE & CONTRACT-TRANSFER SUMMARY

**Licenses/permits — transferability:**
- **Septage-hauler / waste-transporter permit (state):** These are typically issued to the BUSINESS ENTITY or facility, not to an individual owner. In a **stock purchase** they generally ride along automatically; in an **asset purchase** the buyer usually must **re-apply or transfer** the permit to the new entity — a routine but NOT instantaneous process. There can be a gap. CONFIRM the issuing state's transfer mechanism and timeline; a lapse would halt disposal operations. None of these require the founders to personally qualify as a credentialed tradesperson — they are corporate/operational permits, sometimes requiring a designated "responsible person" who can be a hired manager or driver. **Hireable/transferable — not a credential gate.**
- **USDOT number / operating authority:** A USDOT number is **not transferable** between entities (FMCSA policy); a new operating entity (typical in an asset deal) generally must obtain its OWN USDOT number. This is administrative, fast, and routine — but it must be planned so trucks aren't operating under a dead number on day one.
- **CDLs:** Held by the DRIVERS, not the company — they transfer with the inherited crew, which is exactly why retaining drivers through transition matters.
- **No state contractor/professional license** of the kind that gates HVAC/plumbing/electrical. **No personal-credential disqualifier under doc-01 constraint #5.**

**Contracts / change-of-control:**
- **Customer base is largely informal** ("established contractor relationships and phone orders") — this is a double-edged sword. There may be FEW formal long-term contracts to assign (good: little change-of-control consent risk; bad: also little contractual lock-in, so revenue is relationship-glued and can walk at transition — that's a COO/CMO retention issue more than a legal one). Where large contractor master-service agreements DO exist, check for assignment/change-of-control clauses and any "convenience termination" rights.
- **Disposal agreements with the POTW/treatment site** are critical contracts — confirm they are assignable or that the buyer can establish its own disposal access. Loss of disposal access is an operational kill-switch.
- **Yard/real-estate lease** (if leased, not owned): check assignability, change-of-control, remaining term, and environmental condition of the staging yard. If the yard is owned and sold with the business, that shifts the deal toward real estate (and possibly a longer SBA term).
- **Fleet financing / equipment liens:** verify whether trucks/units are owned free-and-clear or carry liens that must be paid off or assumed; UCC search required.

## LIABILITY & DEAL-STRUCTURE POSTURE

**Recommendation: ASSET PURCHASE, not stock.** An asset deal lets the buyer leave behind the seller's historical liabilities — most importantly the **environmental/disposal tail** (any past improper discharge, spill, or disposal-record gap) and unknown tax/employment exposure. Form a NewCo, buy the fleet, units, customer list, goodwill, and assignable contracts; re-permit and obtain a fresh USDOT number. **But beware the "asset deal = inherit nothing" fallacy:** successor liability still reaches the buyer for (a) **environmental/CERCLA** contamination (which can attach to current owners/operators of a contaminated site regardless of deal form — relevant to the YARD where waste is staged and trucks are washed), (b) certain **state successor tax liability** (unpaid sales/payroll tax — defeated only with **tax-clearance certificates** from state agencies before closing), and (c) de facto merger / mere-continuation doctrines if the business simply continues unchanged. So asset structure helps but does not zero the tail.

**Seller liability tail to contain:** pending/threatened litigation (vehicle accidents are the highest-frequency claim in a trucking-heavy business — pull the loss runs and EMR), any environmental enforcement or disposal-violation history, unpaid payroll/sales tax, and **driver misclassification** (if service/delivery drivers are 1099 rather than W-2, that is a wage-hour and payroll-tax bomb — DOL and IRS both pursue it). Contain with: a 10–15% **escrow/holdback** (12–18 months), robust **reps & warranties** (especially environmental, tax, employment, and disposal-compliance reps), an **environmental Phase I** on the yard (Phase II if flagged), **tax-clearance certificates**, **UCC/lien searches**, and a **non-compete** from the retiring owner (enforceable in most states for a sold-business sale, which is the strongest non-compete posture — but confirm state law).

**Personal-guarantee exposure:** SBA 7(a) requires an **unlimited personal guarantee** from every 20%+ owner, typically a lien on personal assets, often a **spousal guarantee** and a **life-insurance collateral assignment**. With SDE ~$700K [ASSUMED] against a ~$2.25M deal, DSCR cushion should be healthy — the hard-asset collateral (fleet) materially reduces the lender's reliance on the PG and improves recovery posture if the business stumbles. Still, the founders are personally on the hook; the PG is real and should be sized against the worst-case downturn (a construction-cycle slump that drops utilization). ## BIGGEST SINGLE RISK

The single issue most likely to re-trade or seriously complicate this deal is the **environmental and wastewater-disposal tail**. This business exists to collect, transport, and dispose of human septage; it operates vacuum trucks that are washed and staged on a yard, and it depends on continuous, permitted access to a POTW or land-application disposal site. Three things compound here: (1) **CERCLA/state environmental successor liability** can attach to the current owner/operator of a contaminated site regardless of whether the deal is an asset purchase — so if the seller's yard (where trucks wash out and units are cleaned) is contaminated, the buyer can inherit cleanup exposure that the deal structure cannot fully wall off; (2) any **history of improper discharge or disposal-manifest gaps** under the Clean Water Act / 40 CFR Part 503 is a regulatory-enforcement tail that may not surface in seller-provided records; and (3) **loss or non-transfer of disposal access** (the POTW agreement plus the state septage-hauler permit) is an operational kill-switch — without a place to legally empty the trucks, the business stops. A mandatory **Phase I environmental site assessment** (Phase II if flagged), confirmed permit-transfer/re-issuance timeline, and an assignable-or-replaceable disposal agreement are non-negotiable pre-closing items; absent satisfactory answers, this is a price re-trade or a walk.

## QUESTIONS FOR REAL COUNSEL & DUE DILIGENCE
- **Environmental:** Commission a **Phase I ESA on the yard** (truck-wash/staging area) and confirm whether any prior discharge, spill, or disposal-violation history exists; quantify CERCLA/state cleanup exposure that survives an asset deal. Confirm the disposal agreement(s) with the POTW/land-application site and whether disposal access is assignable or must be independently re-established.
- **Licensing/DOT:** Confirm with state environmental counsel the **transfer mechanism and timeline for the septage-hauler/waste-transporter permit** to a NewCo, and confirm the plan to obtain a **new USDOT number** and rebuild driver-qualification, drug-testing (49 CFR Part 382), and DOT maintenance files so no truck operates under a dead number at closing.
- **Employment/tax:** Have the CPA/counsel verify whether service and delivery **drivers are W-2 or 1099** (misclassification exposure) and whether all **payroll and sales/use tax is current** — obtain **state tax-clearance certificates** before closing to block successor tax liability.
- **Litigation/insurance:** Pull the **commercial-auto loss runs, workers'-comp EMR, and any pending/threatened litigation** (vehicle accidents especially), and confirm equipment is owned free-and-clear via **UCC/lien searches**.
- **Deal protection:** Confirm an enforceable **seller non-compete** under the governing state's sale-of-business standard, and size the **escrow/holdback (12–18 mo)** against the environmental, tax, and employment reps.

## RECOMMENDATION: RE-TRADE → GO

Structure this as an **asset purchase into a NewCo** with: a **Phase I ESA** (and Phase II if flagged) on the yard as a closing condition; **state tax-clearance certificates** to block successor tax liability; a **12–18-month escrow/holdback (~10–15%)** backed by robust **environmental, tax, and employment reps & warranties**; a planned **permit-transfer + new-USDOT-number sequence** so operations don't lapse on day one; confirmed **assignable or replaceable disposal access**; **UCC/lien searches** on the fleet; and an enforceable **seller non-compete**. With those protections in place, the legal/regulatory posture is acceptable — there is no eligibility or personal-credential deal-killer, the exposures (environmental, DOT, employment) are all knowable and containable, and the hard-asset collateral cushions the SBA personal guarantee. Resolve the environmental and disposal-access questions favorably and this is a **GO**; if the Phase I or disposal-access answers come back ugly, it converts to a price re-trade or a walk.
