LEGAL / REGULATORY REVIEW — Backflow-Prevention Testing & Cross-Connection-Control Compliance Company
(Not formal legal advice — these are issues to take to an M&A attorney, a CPA, and an SBA lender. I am an experienced deal operator flagging landmines, not a licensed attorney resolving them.)

ONE-PARAGRAPH RISK READ
This is, on its face, one of the cleaner legal/deal profiles you will see in a boomer-owned field-service acquisition: a for-profit US operating service company, plainly SBA 7(a)-eligible, with a low-environmental, low-PII, low-litigation footprint and a recurring book driven by a statutory annual-testing mandate (almost universally rooted in EPA Safe Drinking Water Act cross-connection-control requirements implemented through state plumbing/health codes and local water-purveyor ordinances). The single legal issue that dominates everything is the certified-tester credential: revenue is legally contingent on a certified backflow-assembly tester (BAT) performing and filing the tests, and the seller IS that tester — so the deal lives or dies on whether certification can be transferred to or held by an employee rather than the owner. The good news is that backflow-tester certification is almost always a PERSONAL credential held by an individual tester (typically via ASSE 5110 / state plumbing-board / local water-authority registration), NOT a company license tied to the owner — which means a retained or hired certified employee can carry it, making this bridgeable rather than fatal, but it must be confirmed jurisdiction-by-jurisdiction and the transition must be staffed before close.

REGULATORY & SBA-ELIGIBILITY MAP
SBA 7(a) eligibility: clearly eligible. It is a for-profit, US-based, operating service business — not a passive/real-estate-holding, lending, investment, or speculative entity, and nothing on the SBA ineligible-business list (13 CFR 120.110) applies. At ~$650K revenue it is comfortably inside SBA size standards for plumbing/specialty-trade and testing services (NAICS likely 238220 plumbing/HVAC or 541380 testing laboratories — both far under the relevant size caps). The deal must still be a full change of ownership (SBA 7(a) requires 100% acquisition; no partial buy-ins funded by 7(a)), any seller note counted toward the equity injection must be on full standby (typically 2 years, no payments) per SOP 50 10, and every 20%+ owner signs an unlimited personal guarantee. No citizenship/eligibility red flags assuming the founders are US citizens or LPRs.

Applicable regulatory regimes (day-to-day operating burden is moderate-to-low):
- EPA Safe Drinking Water Act (42 U.S.C. §300f et seq.) cross-connection-control mandate — the demand driver. Implemented through state drinking-water programs and codified in state plumbing/health codes.
- Local water purveyors / municipal water authorities — the operative regulator: they require annual testing of backflow assemblies, define who is an approved/registered tester, set the filing format, and maintain the approved-tester list. This is hyper-local and the most important compliance touchpoint.
- State plumbing board / contractor licensing board — some states require the backflow-testing business or its testers to hold a plumbing contractor license, journeyman card, or a backflow-specialty endorsement; others treat testing as a standalone certification distinct from plumbing-contractor licensure. This varies materially by state and is the key diligence variable.
- Tester certification bodies — ASSE International (ASSE 5110), the American Backflow Prevention Association (ABPA), the University of Southern California Foundation for Cross-Connection Control (USC FCCCHR), or AWWA, depending on which standard the local purveyor recognizes. Certifications are personal and renewable (commonly every 1–3 years, often with a gauge-calibration requirement).
- Test-gauge calibration — most jurisdictions require the test kit's differential pressure gauge to be calibrated annually by an accredited lab; calibration certificates are part of a valid filing. Cheap and routine, but a compliance item.
- OSHA — general field-crew safety; low burden for this trade (no confined-space/trenching-heavy work typical; mostly above-ground assembly testing). DOT/FMCSA effectively N/A for light service trucks under 10,001 lbs operating intrastate.

LICENSE & CONTRACT-TRANSFER SUMMARY
This is the heart of the review. There are two distinct license questions:

(1) The TESTER certification (the binding constraint). Backflow-assembly tester certification is, in the overwhelming majority of US jurisdictions, a PERSONAL credential earned by an individual passing a hands-on and written exam (ASSE 5110 / state / water-authority), tied to that person — not a company license and not tied to ownership. Practically, this means the owner's personal certification is NOT an irreducible single point of failure: a certified employee can hold it, and the founders (who will not perform the trade) can own and operate the company while a retained or newly hired certified tester does the field work and signs the filings. This is exactly the founder-profile-compliant "licensed-employee holds the credential" pattern that doc-01 hard-constraint #5 explicitly permits (and instructs to flag, not disqualify). The real exposure is operational-continuity, not legal-impossibility: if the only certified tester is the departing owner and no certified employee is retained or hired before close, the company cannot legally produce its core deliverable on day one and the renewal book bleeds. The mitigation is concrete and must be a closing condition — retain the owner's certified tester(s) on an employment agreement, or have a hired/contracted certified tester in place, plus the owner on a transition consulting agreement long enough to season relationships with the water authorities.

(2) Any BUSINESS/contractor license. If the operating jurisdiction requires the COMPANY to hold a plumbing-contractor or backflow-specialty business license (some states do; many treat testing as certification-only), that license typically requires a "qualifying individual" / qualifying party with the credential — which again can be a qualifying EMPLOYEE, not necessarily the owner. State contractor licenses frequently do NOT transfer with a sale and must be re-issued to the new entity, with the qualifying individual re-attached; this is a re-application, not a transfer, and can carry a gap if not pre-staged. Confirm whether a company license exists, whether it transfers, and who can serve as qualifying party.

Contract / change-of-control posture: benign. The "contracts" here are largely a recurring relationship book (property owners and property managers who re-engage annually) rather than long-dated written master agreements with assignment clauses — good for transferability in substance, but it means revenue is at-will and retention-dependent rather than contractually locked. Watch for: (a) any written multi-property property-management agreements that contain assignment/consent clauses; (b) approved-tester registrations with water authorities, which are typically per-tester and per-company and may require re-registration of the new entity/owner with each authority — administrative, not a deal-killer, but a transition task list; (c) the real-estate lease for the small shop/yard, if any — confirm it is assignable or short/replaceable; (d) the vehicle/test-kit assets — confirm clean title, no liens.

LIABILITY & DEAL-STRUCTURE POSTURE
Recommended structure: ASSET purchase, not stock. There is no compelling reason to buy the entity (the value is the book, the trucks, the kits, and the certified personnel — all assignable in an asset deal), and an asset deal sheds most of the seller's historical liability tail. Standard guards: a tax-clearance / good-standing certificate from the state and a payoff of any state sales/use-tax and payroll-tax obligations (bulk-sales / successor-tax-liability rules in some states can follow the buyer for unpaid sales/payroll tax even in an asset deal — get clearance certificates and an escrow holdback). Successor liability that can survive even an asset deal: (a) successor tax liability per state bulk-sales statutes; (b) certain environmental liability (de minimis here — testing is non-industrial, no tanks, no contamination process; environmental tail is among the lowest you'll see in field service); (c) de facto merger / mere-continuation doctrine if the buyer continues the same business under the same name with the same people — mitigated by a clean asset structure and proper R&W/indemnity. Seller liability tail is genuinely thin: the principal professional-liability exposure is a missed/erroneous test that lets a backflow event contaminate potable water — a real but insurable risk (professional/E&O plus general liability), and the per-test dollar exposure is small; confirm claims history and carry adequate E&O. Reps & warranties should cover certification validity, complete filing history with each water authority, accurate renewal-list/customer disclosure, no undisclosed litigation or regulatory action, tax compliance, and worker classification; back them with a 10–15% escrow/holdback for 12–18 months and a seller non-compete (enforceable in most states for a sold business under the goodwill exception, even in California per B&P §16601). Personal-guarantee exposure: SBA mandates an unlimited PG from every 20%+ owner, likely with a lien on personal assets, a spousal guarantee, and a life-insurance collateral assignment on the key founder. On a ~$650K-loan deal the absolute PG dollar figure is modest by SBA standards, but it is still unlimited and personal — the real question is DSCR cushion, and the thin-add-back / small-absolute-SDE profile means the founders are personally on the hook for a business whose income ceiling depends on growth they must execute.

TOP 3 LEGAL/REGULATORY RISKS
- Certified-tester continuity: if the departing owner is the only certified tester and no certified employee is retained/hired before close, the company cannot legally produce its core deliverable on day one — an operational-continuity landmine even though the credential is legally bridgeable.
- Worker misclassification: boomer-owned field shops frequently pay testers as 1099 contractors; reclassification under the IRS 20-factor / state ABC test plus I-9 verification could create back-tax/penalty and successor exposure.
- Hyper-local water-authority and possible state-contractor re-registration: approved-tester status and any company license may need re-issuance per authority/state rather than transferring, creating administrative gaps if not pre-staged before close.

BIGGEST SINGLE RISK
The deal's single most serious legal/operational risk is certified-tester continuity at and after closing. The entire revenue mechanism is legally contingent on a state/water-authority-certified backflow-assembly tester performing each annual test and filing a valid, calibrated-gauge certification with the local purveyor — and the seller is that tester. The credential itself is bridgeable (personal, employee-holdable per doc-01 #5), so this is not the fatal "owner must personally qualify" trap. But it converts into a hard problem if diligence reveals that the owner is the ONLY certified individual and that the certified field labor was 1099/contract rather than retained employees, or that the owner's personal name is the registered "approved tester" on file with each water authority. In that case the buyer must, as an absolute condition precedent to close, secure certified-tester capacity — by employment agreement with the existing tester(s), by hiring/contracting a certified tester, and by re-registering the new entity as an approved tester with each water authority — and bridge the owner via a transition consulting agreement long enough to re-paper authority registrations and season property-manager relationships. Fail to staff this before close and the renewal book, which is the only thing you are really buying, silently lapses test cycle by test cycle.

QUESTIONS FOR REAL COUNSEL & DUE DILIGENCE
- Confirm with M&A counsel and the relevant state plumbing/contractor board and each water authority: is the backflow-tester certification a PERSONAL credential a retained/hired employee can hold (and re-register under the new entity), or does any jurisdiction require the COMPANY or an OWNER to be a licensed plumbing contractor / qualifying party? Identify every water authority on the route and its tester-registration and re-registration rules.
- Have the CPA run a worker-classification audit (IRS 20-factor / applicable state ABC test) on all field testers and schedulers, plus an I-9 review, and obtain state sales/use-tax and payroll-tax CLEARANCE certificates to cap bulk-sales successor-tax liability before closing.
- Have counsel structure an ASSET purchase with: a 10–15% escrow/holdback (12–18 months); reps on certification validity, complete and accurate authority filing history, renewal-list/customer accuracy, no missed-test/E&O claims, tax compliance, and worker classification; an enforceable seller non-compete; an owner transition-consulting agreement; and confirm E&O + general liability coverage and claims history for missed/erroneous tests.
- Confirm the SBA structure with the lender: full change of ownership, any seller note on full standby toward the equity injection, and the exact personal-guarantee/spousal-guarantee/life-insurance-collateral terms and the DSCR the lender will underwrite at the assumed ~$240K SDE.

RECOMMENDATION: GO (conditioned)
Subject to confirming the tester-credential and worker-classification diligence above, this is a GO. The legal/regulatory posture is unusually favorable for a boomer-owned field-service acquisition: clean SBA eligibility, an asset-deal path, a thin and insurable liability tail, near-zero environmental exposure, and a credential structure that doc-01 expressly permits an employee to carry. The one issue that could re-trade or delay the deal — certified-tester continuity — is bridgeable but must be converted into hard closing conditions (retained/hired certified tester, water-authority re-registration, owner transition agreement) and a worker-classification clean-up, not left as a post-close hope. Structure it as an asset purchase with tax clearances, escrow, robust reps, and a staffed tester plan, and the legal risk lands at acceptable.
