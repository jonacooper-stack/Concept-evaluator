LEGAL / REGULATORY REVIEW — Rampart (AI-first CMMC compliance platform for the Defense Industrial Base)

*Not formal legal advice. I am an experienced operator with strong regulatory pattern-recognition, not your licensed attorney. The items below are issues to brief real counsel on, not legal conclusions you can rely on.*

ONE-PARAGRAPH RISK READ

Rampart is a B2B SaaS sold into one of the most regulation-laden corners of the U.S. economy — but here the regulation is the *customer's* burden, not primarily the vendor's, which is the whole reason the market exists. The platform itself is unlicensed software (no professional license, no state-by-state credential, clickwrap-friendly contracting), and that posture is genuinely clean. The two real fault lines are (1) the platform's repeated promise to "store customers' security documentation and compliance evidence," combined with an explicit admission that "customers may try to upload sensitive material" — which can pull Rampart itself into the CUI-handling and CMMC/FedRAMP scope it is trying to stay out of, the single most important risk in the deal; and (2) advertising/liability exposure from positioning the AI output as "the artifacts themselves, not advice" and promising certification "in weeks," when the actual pass/fail decision belongs to a third-party (C3PAO) assessor and DoD, not Rampart. Both are manageable with deliberate data-boundary engineering and disciplined claims, but neither is free, and the first one is a design decision the founders must make before they write a line of ingestion code.

REGULATORY MAP

- **DFARS 252.204-7012 / NIST SP 800-171 / CMMC (32 CFR Part 170 program rule, effective Dec 2024; 48 CFR/DFARS 252.204-7021 acquisition rule phasing in)** — This is the regime that creates the *demand*, and it lands on the customer, not on Rampart as a baseline software vendor. Regulator: DoD (DoD CIO / DCMA DIBCAC for assessments, the CMMC Accreditation Body / Cyber AB and the C3PAOs for third-party assessment). Burden on Rampart-as-tool: low. BUT the moment Rampart stores actual CUI on behalf of customers, DFARS 7012's flow-down obligations (including the FedRAMP-Moderate-equivalent requirement for cloud service providers that store/process/transmit CUI, plus 72-hour incident reporting to DoD and media-preservation duties) can attach to Rampart as a subcontractor in the data flow. This is the hinge of the entire legal analysis.
- **FedRAMP Moderate (or FedRAMP-Moderate-equivalent per DoD memo) — for any cloud service that stores/processes/transmits CUI** — Regulator: FedRAMP PMO / GSA, with DoD's "equivalency" overlay. Burden: very high (six-to-seven-figure, multi-month authorization effort) *if and only if* Rampart becomes a CUI repository. If Rampart stays strictly a metadata/evidence-*about*-controls store and never a CUI enclave, this does not apply. The one-pager's own hedge ("positioned to store metadata/evidence about CUI handling rather than to be a CUI enclave itself") shows the founders see this — but "customers may try to upload sensitive material" means hope is not a control. This must be enforced technically and contractually.
- **ITAR (22 CFR 120–130) / EAR (15 CFR 730–774) — export-controlled technical data** — Regulator: State/DDTC (ITAR) and Commerce/BIS (EAR). Many DIB customers (aerospace part suppliers, electronics manufacturers, engineering firms named in the one-pager) handle ITAR/EAR technical data. If such data is uploaded into Rampart and Rampart's hosting, support staff, or LLM subprocessors are non-U.S.-person or offshore, that is a potential export violation with strict-liability, per-violation civil penalties and criminal exposure. The "no CUI / no technical-data upload" boundary that solves the CMMC problem also solves most of this — they are the same control.
- **FTC Act §5 — unfair/deceptive advertising (and state "little FTC" acts / UDAP statutes)** — Regulator: FTC + state AGs. Directly relevant because the positioning promises "CMMC-certified in weeks" and frames AI output as "the artifacts themselves — not advice." A customer that buys, follows the tool, and still *fails* a C3PAO assessment is a deception/false-advertising and breach claim waiting to happen unless the claims are carefully qualified (Rampart prepares documentation and a readiness picture; it does not and cannot confer certification). Burden: low if disciplined from day one; reputationally and legally costly if not.
- **FTC ROSCA + state automatic-renewal laws (California ARL Bus. & Prof. Code §17600 et seq.; New York GBL §527-a; and ~20 other states)** — Regulator: FTC + state AGs. Applies because revenue is "annual SaaS, billed yearly up front" and presumably auto-renewing. This is a pure-B2B subscription, which softens but does NOT eliminate auto-renewal disclosure/easy-cancel obligations; California's and the FTC's "click-to-cancel"-style rules increasingly reach B2B. Burden: low — clear renewal disclosure and a simple cancel path, built in once.
- **State data-breach notification laws (all 50 states + DC) + customers' contractual notification clauses** — Regulator: state AGs. Rampart stores its customers' security posture and evidence — a concentrated, attractive target. Even absent CUI, a breach of "how dozens of defense contractors protect themselves" is a serious incident triggering 50-state notification and, via DFARS flow-down, possibly DoD 72-hour reporting. Burden: moderate; standard for any security-adjacent SaaS but elevated by the sensitivity of the data class.
- **Data privacy (CCPA/CPRA, and other state comprehensive privacy laws; GDPR only if EU personnel data)** — Regulator: CA Privacy Protection Agency / state AGs. Low salience: this is B2B, and most ingested data is corporate/technical, not consumer PII. Some employee data (HR procedures, named control owners) flows through, so a standard privacy policy and DPA are needed, but this is not a heavy regime here.
- **Unauthorized practice of law (UPL) — state bar rules, 50 states** — Regulator: state bars / state AGs. Low but non-zero: auto-drafting "policies," an SSP, and a POA&M is document *generation*, not legal advice, and is closely analogous to what Vanta/Drata already do at scale without UPL problems. Stays low as long as Rampart sells software output and does not hold itself out as rendering legal/regulatory opinions or representing the customer before DoD.
- **Unauthorized "assessor" / RPO-RP positioning (CMMC ecosystem rules, Cyber AB)** — The Cyber AB governs who may call themselves a C3PAO, Registered Provider Organization (RPO), or Registered Practitioner (RP). Rampart correctly disclaims being a C3PAO. If it wants to market "readiness" credibly it may want RPO status or to partner with RP/RPO/C3PAO entities; conflict-of-interest rules bar the same entity from both consulting on and assessing the same client. Burden: low and largely optional, but a marketing-claims minefield if sloppy.
- **AI-specific output/IP terms (LLM subprocessor agreements; emerging state AI laws)** — Regulator: contract + emerging state regimes. Rampart rides third-party foundation-model APIs; it must ensure (a) customer uploads are not used to train the provider's base models, (b) the data-residency / U.S.-person posture of the model provider is compatible with the CUI/ITAR boundary, and (c) output IP/indemnity terms are acceptable. Burden: low-moderate, contractual.

LICENSING SUMMARY

No occupational, professional, or industry operating license is required to sell this software. Rampart is not a law firm, not a CPA firm, not a C3PAO, and not a federally regulated financial or healthcare entity — none of those licensing regimes attach to a SaaS tool that generates compliance documentation. The founders' lack of specialized credentials is therefore not a licensing blocker; this is one of the genuinely clean facts of the concept. The relevant "credentials" are *market-credibility* badges, not legal licenses: optionally registering as a CMMC Registered Provider Organization (RPO) with the Cyber AB, and/or staffing/partnering with a Registered Practitioner (RP) or former C3PAO assessor — exactly what the one-pager already plans via a named advisor. None of these is a precondition to operate; they are go-to-market assets and, importantly, the source of the assessor-domain knowledge the founders lack. The one license-like obligation that could appear is not a license at all but an *authorization*: if Rampart elects to become a CUI store, it would need FedRAMP-Moderate-equivalent authorization, which is a heavy, expensive program — and the entire recommendation below is built around *not* triggering that. Standard corporate formation (Delaware C-corp or LLC), an EIN, and ordinary state business registration are the only baseline filings. Net: licensing posture is excellent; the risk lives in data scope, not in any license the founders must hold.

LIABILITY POSTURE

The dominant exposure is **professional-liability / errors-and-omissions**: a customer relies on Rampart's AI-generated SSP, policies, and gap scorecard, presents them to a C3PAO, and fails — or worse, attests to compliance on the strength of Rampart's output and is later found in a False Claims Act posture for a defective affirmation. Even though the failure is ultimately the assessor's and the contractor's call, Rampart's "the artifacts themselves, not advice" framing invites the customer to treat the output as authoritative, which transfers blame upstream. This is handled by three layers in combination: (1) **contract** — a tightly drafted clickwrap/ToS with a clear "software tool, not a guarantee of certification; customer is responsible for accuracy of inputs and for its own affirmations; not legal advice" disclaimer, a limitation-of-liability capped at fees paid, and a disclaimer of warranties; (2) **insurance** — tech E&O / professional liability plus cyber liability, both readily available and, for a pure-software vendor with a clean data boundary, insurable at modest premiums (mid four to low five figures annually [ASSUMED, verify with a broker quoting tech-E&O + cyber for a security-SaaS at <$5M revenue]); (3) **entity** — forming the C-corp/LLC *before* first customer contract so the founders are not personally on the hook. The second exposure is **data-security / breach liability**: Rampart concentrates dozens of defense contractors' security postures in one place. With a clean "no CUI" boundary this is ordinary cyber-insurable SaaS risk; without it, a breach could mean DFARS reporting duties, ITAR/EAR export-violation exposure, customer-contract indemnity claims, and a class of damages that may exceed a fee-capped LoL and strain insurability. The contracts with customers (data-handling addendum, breach-notification terms, acceptable-use prohibiting CUI/ITAR uploads) and with the LLM subprocessor (no-training, residency, indemnity) are where this is contained. Residual after all three layers: the irreducible risk that a court or the FTC treats "in weeks, the artifacts themselves" as a deceptive guarantee, and the irreducible risk that a determined customer uploads prohibited data despite the controls — both reducible but not eliminable.

SCORES (1–10) (higher = lower risk)

1. **Industry regulatory burden: 6** — The DIB is heavily regulated, but the burden falls on the customer (NIST 800-171/CMMC/DFARS); Rampart-as-software sits adjacent and largely outside it — *unless* it stores CUI, which is why this is a 6 and not an 8, since the boundary is a live design choice rather than a settled fact, and the surrounding regime (DFARS 252.204-7012, 32 CFR Part 170) is genuinely heavy.

2. **Licensing requirements: 9** — No occupational, professional, or industry operating license attaches to a compliance-documentation SaaS; the only credentialing is optional Cyber AB RPO/RP *market* status, not a legal license to operate — earned 9 because the specific regimes that could require licensure (state bar/UPL, CPA, C3PAO accreditation under 32 CFR Part 170) all clearly exclude an unlicensed software tool that doesn't hold itself out as an assessor or lawyer.

3. **Data privacy exposure: 5** — Consumer PII is minimal (B2B), so CCPA/CPRA/GDPR salience is low, but the data *class* is unusually sensitive — security postures, evidence, and the standing temptation that "customers may try to upload sensitive material" (CUI/ITAR) under DFARS 252.204-7012 and ITAR 22 CFR 120–130 — which keeps this at mediocre rather than good despite the light consumer-privacy footprint.

4. **Employment & contractor risk: 9** — Two founders, software-only delivery, no field crews, no in-home workers, no multi-state W-2/1099 misclassification engine; classification risk is near-zero because there is no contractor workforce to misclassify — a remote SaaS team is squarely outside the wage-and-hour and multi-state employment landmines the rubric flags as a 1.

5. **Consumer protection complexity: 8** — This is pure B2B with no consumer subscriptions, so the heaviest auto-renewal exposure is muted; the residual is FTC ROSCA and California ARL (Bus. & Prof. Code §17600) reaching the annual auto-renew billing, which is solved once with clear disclosure and an easy-cancel path — named regime, low and bounded burden, hence 8 rather than 9.

6. **IP cleanliness: 6** — Core product IP (mappings, ingestion, workflows) is original and ownable, but the engine *rides third-party LLM APIs*, creating dependency, no-train/residency, and output-IP/indemnity terms that must be contractually secured; the platform also leans on the publicly-available NIST 800-171 control set (public-domain, fine) — the API dependency caps this at 6 per the rubric's "riding restricted APIs" language.

7. **Contract simplicity: 7** — Self-serve customers can be onboarded on clickwrap/ToS with a data-handling and acceptable-use addendum, which is light; but the MSP/MSSP multi-tenant partner tier and any larger DIB accounts will demand negotiated MSAs/DPAs and security questionnaires, so it is not pure clickwrap — good, not great.

8. **Liability exposure: 5** — Tech-E&O and cyber liability are real and insurable at modest premiums for a clean-boundary SaaS, but the "artifacts themselves, not advice" + "certified in weeks" framing concentrates reliance-and-failure (and adjacent False Claims Act affirmation) exposure, and a CUI/ITAR breach could blow past a fee-capped limitation-of-liability — averaging to a mediocre 5 because the exposure is contractually and insurably *manageable* but materially larger than a generic B2B tool's.

9. **Jurisdictional simplicity: 7** — One federal regime (DoD/CMMC) drives the buy nationwide, so there is no 50-state *licensing* multiplication, and operating jurisdiction is wherever the company sits; the multipliers that exist are 50-state breach-notification statutes and state auto-renewal/UDAP laws layered on a federal core — additive paperwork, not licensing cliffs, so a 7.

10. **Regulatory trajectory: 8** — Tailwind, and rare to be able to name it: the 32 CFR Part 170 program rule took effect December 2024 and the 48 CFR/DFARS 252.204-7021 acquisition rule is phasing CMMC into solicitations through the mid-to-late 2020s, so the mandate is tightening *in the customer's direction* (more forced buys), which is favorable to a compliance vendor — held to 8 not 9 because rulemaking timelines, phase-in pace, and any CUI-storage reclassification of vendors could still shift the vendor-side burden.

AVERAGE SCORE: 7.0 / 10

TOP 3 LEGAL/REGULATORY STRENGTHS

- **No occupational/professional license to operate.** A compliance-documentation SaaS sits outside state bar/UPL, CPA, and C3PAO-accreditation (32 CFR Part 170) licensing; the only "credentials" are optional Cyber AB RPO/RP market badges, so the founders' lack of specialized legal/assessor credentials is not a legal blocker (Licensing = 9).
- **Regulatory tailwind, precisely dated.** The 32 CFR Part 170 program rule (effective Dec 2024) and the phasing 48 CFR/DFARS 252.204-7021 acquisition rule are tightening the *customer's* mandate over time, manufacturing recurring, deadline-driven demand — the regulation works *for* the vendor (Trajectory = 8).
- **Clean labor and contracting baseline.** Software-only, two founders, no field workforce means effectively zero employment/misclassification exposure, and a clickwrap-plus-addendum motion covers the self-serve core, with negotiated MSAs reserved for partners/large accounts (Employment = 9, Contracts = 7).

TOP 3 LEGAL/REGULATORY RISKS

- **The CUI-storage boundary.** "Stores customers' security documentation and compliance evidence" + "customers may try to upload sensitive material" risks pulling Rampart into DFARS 252.204-7012's FedRAMP-Moderate-equivalent, 72-hour-reporting, CUI-handling scope — and adjacent ITAR/EAR export exposure — converting a clean SaaS into a heavily-regulated CUI enclave. This is the single biggest risk.
- **Advertising/reliance exposure.** "CMMC-certified in weeks" and "the artifacts themselves — not advice" invite FTC Act §5 / state-UDAP deception claims and breach/E&O claims (with a False Claims Act shadow over customer affirmations) when a customer who relied on the output nonetheless fails a C3PAO assessment that Rampart does not control.
- **Concentration-of-sensitive-data breach.** Holding dozens of DIB contractors' security postures in one repository is a high-value target whose breach triggers 50-state notification, possible DFARS DoD reporting, customer-indemnity claims, and (if CUI/ITAR leaked) export-violation and insurability problems that could exceed a fee-capped limitation-of-liability.

BIGGEST SINGLE RISK

The CUI boundary is the issue most likely to either kill the clean-SaaS thesis or quietly metastasize the cost structure. The product's value proposition pulls in two opposite directions at once: to be maximally useful it wants to ingest "whatever documentation they already have" and become "the system of record" — and the most useful evidence a defense contractor has often *is* CUI, or describes systems handling CUI in ways that are themselves controlled, and a meaningful slice of these customers (aerospace, electronics, engineering) also hold ITAR/EAR technical data. The one-pager's mitigation is a *positioning* statement ("metadata/evidence about CUI handling rather than a CUI enclave") undercut by a candid admission that "customers may try to upload sensitive material." Positioning is not a control. The instant actual CUI lands in Rampart's cloud, DFARS 252.204-7012 can flow down to Rampart as a subcontractor in the data flow, dragging in the FedRAMP-Moderate-equivalent requirement (a six-to-seven-figure, multi-month authorization the bootstrapped thesis cannot absorb), 72-hour DoD incident reporting, media-preservation duties, and — if any of that data is export-controlled and any subprocessor or support person is a non-U.S. person — strict-liability ITAR/EAR penalties. The cruel irony is that a security-compliance vendor that *itself* suffers a CUI breach is reputationally finished in this exact buyer community. The fix is architectural and must be decided before build: enforce the no-CUI boundary technically (upload scanning/blocking, redaction, customer-held-key or customer-tenant storage so Rampart never controls plaintext CUI, U.S.-region-only and U.S.-person-only processing, an LLM subprocessor contractually barred from receiving or training on uploads), and contractually (acceptable-use prohibiting CUI/ITAR uploads, indemnity, breach terms) — and then market on the strength of that boundary. Get this right and the average above holds; get it wrong and dimensions 1, 3, 8, and 9 all degrade and the venture is no longer capital-light.

QUESTIONS FOR REAL COUNSEL BEFORE LAUNCH

1. **CUI scope and DFARS flow-down:** If we architect so customers upload only metadata/evidence-*about*-controls and never raw CUI (with technical blocking and customer-held-key storage), does DFARS 252.204-7012 and the FedRAMP-Moderate-equivalent requirement still flow down to us as a subcontractor in the data flow? Where exactly is the line between "stores CUI" and "stores evidence about CUI handling," and what specific architecture keeps us on the safe side?
2. **ITAR/EAR exposure:** Given that some customers hold export-controlled technical data, what upload controls, U.S.-person staffing rules, and subprocessor data-residency terms do we need so that an accidental upload of ITAR/EAR data is not a strict-liability export violation for us — and should our ToS make customers warrant they will not upload such data?
3. **Advertising claims:** How do we word "CMMC-certified in weeks" and "the artifacts themselves, not advice" so they survive FTC Act §5 and state UDAP scrutiny, given that certification is decided by a C3PAO/DoD and not by us — and what disclaimers must accompany the gap scorecard and SSP so a customer's failed assessment is not actionable against us?
4. **False Claims Act / affirmation reliance:** When a customer signs an annual CMMC affirmation relying partly on our output, what is our exposure if that affirmation is later challenged under the False Claims Act, and how do we contract (responsibility for input accuracy, no-guarantee, indemnity-from-customer) to keep that exposure off us?
5. **Limitation-of-liability and insurance:** Will a fees-paid liability cap hold against a data-breach or failed-certification claim in this sector, and what tech-E&O + cyber limits and premiums should we expect from a broker quoting a security-SaaS that explicitly does *not* store CUI? (Get this quoted to replace the [ASSUMED] premium figure.)
6. **UPL / assessor positioning:** Does auto-generating policies, an SSP, and a POA&M risk unauthorized practice of law in any state, and do Cyber AB rules (RPO/RP status, the consult-vs-assess conflict-of-interest bar) constrain how we market "readiness" or partner with C3PAOs?
7. **LLM subprocessor terms:** What contractual no-training, data-residency, U.S.-person, deletion, and indemnity terms must we secure from our foundation-model provider for uploaded customer documents to be compatible with both the CUI/ITAR boundary and our customer DPAs?
8. **Auto-renewal compliance:** Confirm whether FTC ROSCA and California ARL (Bus. & Prof. Code §17600) and the newer "click-to-cancel" rules reach our B2B annual auto-renew, and specify the disclosure and cancellation mechanics we must build in.

RECOMMENDATION: GO (conditional on the CUI-boundary decision being made and engineered before build)

From a purely legal/regulatory standpoint this is a favorable concept and I recommend GO, with one gating condition. The structural facts are strong: no occupational or professional license to operate (Licensing 9), effectively zero employment/contractor exposure (9), a precisely-dated regulatory tailwind that manufactures recurring demand rather than threatening the business (Trajectory 8), and a B2B contracting and consumer-protection posture that is light and well-understood. None of the heavy regimes that would normally sink a bootstrapped, non-credentialed pair — bar/UPL, CPA, C3PAO accreditation, multi-state occupational licensing — attach to a compliance-documentation SaaS. The reason this is "conditional GO" rather than unqualified is that the concept contains one design fork that determines its entire risk profile: whether Rampart stores actual CUI/ITAR data or only evidence-about-controls. That is not a legal question to resolve after launch; it is an architecture decision to make first. The required structural choices are clear and do not change the customer, the revenue mechanism, or the delivery model (so this stays a GO, not a REFINE): (1) architect a hard, technically-enforced no-CUI/no-ITAR boundary (upload blocking/redaction, customer-held-key or customer-tenant storage, U.S.-region/U.S.-person processing, an LLM subprocessor barred from receiving/training on uploads); (2) discipline every advertising claim so "certified in weeks / the artifacts themselves" is qualified to "prepares your documentation and readiness; certification is decided by your C3PAO"; (3) stand up entity, tech-E&O + cyber insurance, a fees-capped LoL, and an acceptable-use/indemnity ToS before the first paid contract. Do those three and the 7.0 posture holds and the business is genuinely capital-light; skip the first and the company drifts into FedRAMP-equivalent, ITAR, and breach exposure that breaks the bootstrapped thesis.
