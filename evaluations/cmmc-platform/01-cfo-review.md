CFO REVIEW — Rampart (AI-first CMMC compliance platform for the U.S. defense industrial base)

ONE-PARAGRAPH FINANCIAL READ

The numbers pencil, and they pencil unusually well for a bootstrap: this is annual-upfront SaaS at a ~$12K [ASSUMED] ACV selling into a regulation-forced, deadline-driven buy, which is the single best cash-flow shape a two-founder business can have — customer pays a year in advance before most costs are incurred, gross margin sits at software-grade 80%+, and growth self-funds. The ladder target (~$300K/founder by month 12, ~$500K+ by month 24) is reachable at roughly 50 and 90 paying accounts respectively, which against a [ASSUMED] ~76K–80K-org TAM is a rounding error of the market, so the obtainable-market and income-path math are not the binding constraints. The real financial fragility is not in the model's structure but in two specific places: (a) the optional onboarding revenue ($5K–$15K) and any "hands-on readiness help" is disguised services that, if it becomes the actual delivery, quietly converts this from 80%-margin SaaS into a 40–55%-margin consultancy capped by founder-hours; and (b) churn/retention is genuinely uncertain because a meaningful slice of customers may treat Phase 1 as a one-time "get-certified" purchase and lapse after the badge is in hand, which would gut the recurring thesis that the whole valuation and income ramp depend on.

THE MATH

Income ladder (relabeled per override — scoring against the ladder, NOT "$2M in 24 months"):

Combined founder income target, month 12: ~$600K (two founders × ~$300K). Month 24: ~$1.0M+ (two × ~$500K+).

A bootstrapped two-founder SaaS with no outside team can plausibly run at a very high owner-earnings margin early because the founders ARE the cost base (they build, sell, and support). Assume the business can distribute roughly 50–60% of revenue to the two founders in year 1 (lean, no payroll beyond themselves, hosting + tooling + one fractional CMMC advisor as the main non-founder cost), tightening toward ~45–55% by year 2 as they add a first support/customer-success hire and pay the advisor more.

- To put ~$600K combined in founders' pockets at ~55% owner margin → need ~$1.09M ARR by month 12. At $12K ACV → ~91 accounts. [The one-pager's onboarding fees would lift this; on pure SaaS, ~90 accounts by month 12.]
- A gentler reading: if early owner margin is closer to 65% (truly just two people, advisor on revenue-share, minimal tooling), ~$600K needs ~$923K ARR → ~77 accounts by month 12.
- Month 24, ~$1.0M combined at ~50% owner margin → ~$2.0M ARR → ~167 accounts at $12K, or ~133 accounts if ACV drifts up to $15K with the MSP/multi-tenant tier and larger contractors.

So the honest landing zone:
- Month 12: roughly 75–95 paying accounts to hit ~$300K/founder. Aggressive but not heroic for a forced, budgeted buy with founder-led sales.
- Month 24: roughly 130–170 accounts (or fewer at higher ACV) to hit ~$500K+/founder.

TOP 3 FINANCIAL STRENGTHS

- Best-in-class cash shape: annual-upfront billing on an 80%+ margin product with sub-6-month payback and no inventory/AR/deposits means the business has a negative working-capital cycle and self-funds its own growth — roughly $1.08M collected on ~90 year-1 accounts against a two-person cost base.
- Regulation-forced, budgeted demand: CMMC Level 2 is becoming a contractual gate for DoD/prime revenue that is the core of the customer's business, so the buy is a painkiller with a deadline and an existing budget line — not a discretionary SaaS seat the founders have to talk anyone into wanting.
- Income ladder is reachable on a rounding-error market share: ~75–95 accounts hits ~$300K/founder by month 12 and ~130–170 hits ~$500K/founder by month 24, i.e. under ~1.1% of even a conservatively-sized serviceable subset of the [ASSUMED] 76K–80K orgs that must comply — so the financial target does not require winning the market, only a sliver of it.

TOP 3 FINANCIAL RISKS

- Retention is the entire LTV thesis and it is unproven: if customers treat Phase 1 ("get certified") as a one-time event and lapse once the badge is in hand, LTV roughly halves ($66K → ~$28K), the recurring story collapses toward a transactional one, and the exit/financeability multiple compresses with it.
- Services creep masquerading as SaaS: the optional $5K–$15K onboarding and any "hands-on readiness help" can silently become the real product; if delivery shifts to founder-hours, blended gross margin slides from ~83% toward 40–55% and revenue gets capped by two people's calendars, which is the field-ops/manual-model trap the objectives explicitly penalize.
- Single-program concentration: 100% of revenue rides one regulatory regime (CMMC) and one buyer ecosystem (DoD/DIB); a rollout-phasing slip, an enforcement delay, or a rule change hits every account in the book simultaneously — diversified across customers but not across the demand driver.

BIGGEST SINGLE RISK

The financial issue most likely to kill the thesis is retention erosion driven by the "compliance-as-an-event" mindset, because every favorable number in this review — the ~$66K LTV, the 20x+ LTV/CAC, the clean recurring-SaaS exit multiple, and the month-24 income rung — is load-bearing on the assumption that a customer who paid $12K to get CMMC-certified will keep paying $12K/year indefinitely to "stay" certified. The structural case for stickiness is real (annual affirmations, triennial reassessment, the platform holds the system of record and the living artifacts), but so is the counter-pressure: a 25-person machine shop that just got its badge, is cash-conscious, and feels "done" may decide it can maintain its SSP in the same spreadsheets it always used and re-engage a consultant only at reassessment time — especially if a cheaper "we already did it once" instinct sets in. If real gross retention lands at 60–70% rather than the assumed 85%, the founders are on a leaky-bucket treadmill: they must sell ~30–40% of the prior cohort's revenue every year just to stand still, CAC effectively doubles per net-retained dollar, the month-24 ~$500K/founder rung slips out by quarters, and the asset reprices from "recurring vertical SaaS" toward "lumpy compliance reseller." The mitigant — making the Phase-2 hub genuinely indispensable so leaving means going dark on continuous evidence and risking the next affirmation — is a product/positioning bet that has to be proven with real renewal cohorts, not assumed; until there is even one renewal cohort to look at, an 85% retention input is a hope, and I would underwrite this at 70% until shown otherwise.

QUESTIONS THE FOUNDERS MUST ANSWER BEFORE I'M COMFORTABLE

- What is the real ACV and tier mix? Validate the [ASSUMED] $7.5K–$24K range with 10–15 priced conversations or signed LOIs from actual DIB contractors — and tell me what fraction of buyers self-select into the $5K–$15K onboarding add-on, because that mix determines blended margin and whether this stays SaaS or drifts to services.
- What is your defensible gross-retention assumption, and on what evidence? I'm underwriting 70% until proven; what is the Phase-2 mechanism that makes leaving painful enough to hold 85%+, and how soon can you produce even one real renewal cohort (even a design-partner cohort) to test it?
- How much human labor does Phase 1 actually require per account once the AI does its job? Run a full ingestion on a real (messy) DIB document package and measure founder-hours from upload to assessment-ready set — if it's 5 hours, this is software; if it's 40+, this is a consultancy with an AI front-end and the margin/scaling scores fall.
- How concentrated and how timing-sensitive is the demand? What is your evidence that contracts are requiring CMMC status now (named solicitations, dated clauses), and what is the revenue exposure if the 48 CFR/DFARS phase-in slips 12–24 months — i.e., how much of your month-12 plan depends on a deadline you don't control?
- What does the C3PAO assessor relationship cost you, and who owns the liability if a Rampart-produced SSP/POA&M fails an assessment? A failed assessment that the customer blames on your artifacts is both a churn event and a potential refund/credibility cost — is that priced and contractually bounded?

RECOMMENDATION: GO

On the financials alone this is a GO. The model has the rare combination the objectives doc is hunting for: a regulation-forced, deadline-driven, already-budgeted must-have buy; annual-upfront billing that produces a negative working-capital cycle and self-funds growth; software-grade 80%+ gross margins; sub-6-month payback; an income ladder reachable on under ~1.1% of a conservatively-sized market; and a clean, acquirable recurring-revenue asset at exit. The two things that would move my conviction from "GO with eyes open" to "GO with both hands" are evidence on the only two numbers that can break it — gross retention (prove the Phase-2 hub holds 80%+, not the badge-and-bail 60–70% I'd otherwise underwrite) and per-account human labor (prove Phase 1 is ~hours of founder time, not ~weeks, so margin stays software-grade and the model doesn't quietly become the capped field-ops consultancy the objectives penalize). Neither is a structural flaw in the concept; both are testable cheaply and fast (priced sales conversations, one real ingestion run, a design-partner renewal cohort) before meaningful capital or time is committed. I would not REFINE the model — its bones are right — but I would gate scale-up spend on those two validations.
