CMO REVIEW — Member Engagement & Dues-Collection Overlay for Labor Unions

ONE-PARAGRAPH MARKET READ
This is one of the cleanest must-have wedges I have seen in a while: when a state flips to right-to-work or passes paycheck-protection, the affected unions face an immediate, existential, budget-line crisis — they literally cannot collect dues — and they will pay to fix it because the alternative is organizational death. The problem is real, urgent, recurring, and already budgeted (dues are the union's entire P&L). The hard part is not whether it can be sold — it is whether it can be reached and trusted: this is a relationship-driven, politically suspicious, slow-procurement, vendor-of-record-loyal buyer set, and the total number of buyers is small (a few thousand entities, concentrated in a handful of states). It can absolutely be sold and positioned, but the distribution motion is high-touch founder-led sales into a tight, referral-gated community, not a marketing-led inbound flywheel — and the founders need to prove they can get into the room.

POSITIONING DRAFT
"When the state takes away payroll dues deduction, we are the system that lets your union reach every member directly and collect dues by card or ACH within weeks — bolted onto the database you already run, not replacing it." That is a sharp, customer-recognizable sentence with real edges: it names the trigger event (loss of payroll deduction), the stakes (every member, direct collection), the speed (weeks), and the non-threatening posture (overlay, not rip-and-replace). It does not sound like generic SaaS. The one risk in the positioning is that it currently leads with the painkiller (dues rail) but bundles in "engagement/mobilization," which is a vitamin to a CFO-minded secretary-treasurer — I would keep the dues-rail spear sharp and treat engagement as the land-and-expand.

THE WEDGE
The first 100 customers do NOT come from ads or SEO. They come from a single concentrated event-driven motion: target the locals and state federations in states that have most recently passed or are about to pass right-to-work / paycheck-protection (recent flashpoints include the litigation and legislation around states like Michigan — which repealed RTW in 2023 — and ongoing pressure in states like Missouri, Wisconsin, Kentucky, and the post-Janus public-sector universe nationwide after Janus v. AFSCME, 2018, which effectively made every public-sector union "right-to-work" overnight). Janus is the real wedge: it created millions of public-sector members from whom unions can no longer assume agency-fee/automatic collection, an event that hit AFSCME, SEIU, NEA, AFT, and their thousands of locals simultaneously. The tactic: (1) founder-led outbound to organizing directors and secretary-treasurers via the labor-tech and labor-conference circuit — AFL-CIO Tech, the Labor Notes conference, state federation conventions, the National Association of Letter Carriers / building-trades councils; sponsorship/booth cost [ASSUMED $3K–$15K per event]; (2) one marquee reference local in a flagship international, then ride the international's mandate downward — the single highest-leverage channel here is selling the international/state body once and having them push adoption to locals; (3) warm intros through labor-friendly consultants, law firms (e.g., union-side firms like Bredhoff & Kaiser), and the AFL-CIO's tech affiliates. CAC will be high in dollars-of-founder-time but low in cash. No paid-acquisition wedge exists and that is fine for a market this concentrated.

COMPETITIVE PICTURE
Five names that matter. (1) UnionWare — the entrenched membership-management database of record for many North American unions; best at compliance, dues ledger, grievance tracking; desktop-heavy, slow, weak at modern SMS/engagement. (2) Union Link / UnionLink — similar legacy MMS positioning, system of record, weak real-time comms. (3) Action Network — the dominant progressive/labor digital organizing and email/SMS engagement tool; best at mass communication and mobilization, used widely across labor and advocacy; but it is NOT a dues-collection rail and NOT a system of record. (4) Hustle / Scale-to-Win / Spoke — peer-to-peer SMS texting tools heavily used in labor and political organizing; best at outbound texting at scale; no dues rail, no membership database. (5) The DIY/incumbent payments stack — unions stitching together Stripe/PayPal/ACH plus their MMS, or specialized dues processors and the older "Unionware payments" modules. The air to breathe is precisely the seam none of these own: a fast-deploy overlay that (a) sits on the legacy DB rather than replacing it, (b) closes the contact-data gap, AND (c) provides a member-paid dues rail in one motion. Action Network can communicate but cannot collect dues; UnionWare can hold the ledger but cannot mobilize or rapidly recapture contact data. The differentiated wedge is the combination plus the trigger-event timing. The competitive worry is Action Network or a payments player adding the adjacent capability — but both are sleepy/mission-led, not aggressively commercial, which fits the founders' edge.

AVERAGE SCORE: 6.9 / 10

TOP 3 MARKET STRENGTHS
- A true painkiller tied to a discrete, externally-imposed trigger event (RTW / paycheck-protection / Janus) that threatens the buyer's entire revenue base — this is must-have, not vitamin, and the budget is the union's most defended line.
- A defensible unowned seam between the sleepy legacy databases of record (UnionWare, Union Link) and the comms-only tools (Action Network, Hustle), with a sell-once-mandate-down distribution lever via internationals/state federations.
- Transaction/take-rate monetization that becomes the dues rail — high switching cost, revenue scales with member volume automatically, and freemium removes the procurement-friction objection that kills slow union budgets.

TOP 3 MARKET RISKS
- Buyer access is relationship- and politics-gated: unions are suspicious of outside vendors, procurement is slow and consensus-driven, and two generalist tech founders with no labor pedigree may struggle to get into the room at all.
- The market is small and concentrated (a few thousand entities, clustered in specific states/sectors), so a swing of even a few large international mandates determines whether the income ladder is reached — lumpy, not smooth.
- Incumbents or adjacent players (Action Network adding payments, a payments vendor adding union templates, UnionWare modernizing) could close the seam; each piece is individually copyable within 6–9 months, so the moat is the relationship and the rail, not the tech.

BIGGEST SINGLE RISK
The biggest single marketing/competitive risk is buyer access and trust, not the existence of the pain. Labor unions are among the most insular, identity-driven, politically careful buyers in the U.S. economy; they buy from people they know, from vendors with movement credibility, and through long consensus-driven and sometimes board-approved procurement cycles tied to convention calendars. Two remote, bootstrapped, generalist software founders with strong enterprise-SaaS and compliance backgrounds but (per the brief) no demonstrated labor-movement relationships are precisely the profile a skeptical organizing director screens out — "a fintech wants to take a cut of our members' dues" is an easy sentence for an opponent on the executive board to weaponize, especially since the product literally inserts a payment take-rate between the union and its members' money. If the founders cannot rapidly acquire movement credibility (a named labor advisor, a former union staffer, an AFL-CIO tech affiliation, a marquee international as design partner), the wedge stays theoretical and the small, concentrated buyer universe becomes impenetrable rather than addressable. The pain is real; the question is whether these founders can be trusted to touch the most sacred number on the union's balance sheet.

QUESTIONS THE FOUNDERS MUST ANSWER BEFORE I'M COMFORTABLE
- How many secretary-treasurers, organizing directors, or international staff have you actually interviewed, and what did they say specifically about taking a take-rate on dues — is a percentage of member dues acceptable, or does it have to be a flat per-member fee to be politically survivable?
- What is your credibility path into this community: do you have (or can you name and recruit within 60 days) a labor-side advisor, former union staffer, or AFL-CIO tech affiliation that gets you into the room and onto a convention stage?
- How concentrated is the realistic near-term market — which specific internationals/state federations in which states are actively bleeding dues right now, how many locals could a single mandate cover, and what is the average dues volume per local (which sets your take-rate revenue)?
- Can a single international actually mandate adoption down to autonomous locals, or do locals retain procurement autonomy — i.e., is "sell once, deploy to hundreds" real or wishful?
- What is the sales cycle length tied to the convention/board-approval calendar, and how many quarters of runway does that imply before the first meaningful take-rate revenue lands?

RECOMMENDATION: REFINE
The core is genuinely strong — a must-have painkiller, an unowned competitive seam against sleepy incumbents (UnionWare, Union Link) and comms-only tools (Action Network, Hustle), a sticky take-rate rail, and a sell-once-mandate-down lever — and it scores at the top end of the band. I am withholding a clean GO for two specific reasons that are fixable with refinement, not redesign. First, customer truth is unproven (scored 5): the founders need ten real conversations with secretary-treasurers and organizing directors before this is more than a sharp hypothesis, and those conversations should specifically pressure-test the take-rate-on-dues monetization, which may need to become a flat per-member fee to be politically acceptable. Second, the access/credibility gap must be closed structurally — name a labor-side advisor or former-staffer co-conspirator and target one marquee international as a design partner — because in this insular, politics-gated market, distribution credibility, not product, is the binding constraint. Fix those two and this becomes a GO.
