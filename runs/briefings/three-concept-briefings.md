# Three Concept Briefings — Plain-Language Edition

*How to read this: each business is scored by a five-person "council" — a money
expert (CFO), a marketing expert (CMO), an operations expert (COO), a legal/rules
expert (Legal), and a tech expert (CTO). Each gives a score from 1 to 10 on ten
questions, then averages them. A 5 means "okay/average," a 7 means "good," and a 9
means "rare and excellent." For a business to make our official shortlist it has to
clear a high bar: the five experts' averages have to average **above 8.0**, AND **no
single one of the fifty sub-scores can be below 7**, AND it has to score **80 out of
100** on our goals checklist. None of these three cleared that bar — but these were
the three best of the twelve we studied, and each is one or two fixes away from being
much stronger.*

# 1. SecureControls Defense Tier-2
### Managed NIST 800-171 / SPRS compliance for small defense suppliers

## The business

**The problem (in plain terms).** Lots of small factories and shops — the people who
make parts for big defense companies like Boeing, Lockheed, and Raytheon — are
required by the U.S. government to follow a long cybersecurity rulebook (it has 110
rules, called "NIST 800-171"). They also have to keep a security "report card" (an
"SPRS score") up to date and answer security surveys their big customers send them.
If their report card is missing, old, or too low, the big company simply stops giving
them work. These small shops usually have nobody on staff who understands any of this
— it's the owner or an office manager trying to figure it out at night. Getting it
wrong can mean losing their biggest customer, which can sink the whole business.

**The solution.** SecureControls is a "we handle it for you" subscription. You pay a
flat yearly price, and the company keeps all your security paperwork correct and
up-to-date for you, all year, forever:
1. **Setup:** they check your shop against all 110 rules, list what's missing, and
   make a plain-English fix-it plan.
2. **A simple online dashboard** that stores all your security proof, policies, and
   report-card info in one place.
3. **Every few months,** they review your controls, refresh the evidence, recalculate
   and re-post your report-card score, and fill out any security surveys your
   customers send — so you never get dropped.
4. **Backup when an auditor shows up** to check you.

The clever part: software does the boring, repeated work (reminders, forms,
reports), and a human handles the judgment calls and talks to the big customers.
There's no fieldwork — it's all done remotely.

## How it makes money (economic model)

- **Setup fee** when you join (a few thousand dollars).
- **Yearly subscription** of roughly **$15,000–$40,000 per shop** (bigger shops with
  more sensitive data pay more), billed up front.
- Because the government rule never goes away, customers basically never leave — once
  you're set up, canceling would put your defense contracts at risk. So the money
  **repeats every year** and is very "sticky."
- **The math:** about **25 shops** signed up gets each founder to ~$300K/year of
  income by month 12. About **40–70 shops** gets to ~$500K each by month 24. There
  are tens of thousands of these suppliers, so the founders would barely scratch the
  surface of the market.
- Money comes in **before** the work is done (paid up front), so the business funds
  its own growth without needing a loan — a very healthy money pattern.

## Who else is out there (competitive landscape)

- **Sleepy consultants and generalist IT shops** — the main competitors. They do a
  one-time project, bill by the hour, then disappear. They don't keep your report card
  current. These are exactly the kind of weak, old-fashioned competitor the founders
  are good at beating with better marketing and a cleaner product.
- **Summit 7, PreVeil, Exostar** — real players in the defense-security world, but they
  mostly serve bigger or more technical customers, or only handle one slice (like
  secure email).
- **Vanta and Drata** — modern, well-funded software companies. Today they sell
  self-service security software (a dashboard you run yourself). The danger: they could
  add defense-rule features and move "down-market" toward these small shops. They're the
  one competitor to watch, because they have lots of money and a strong brand.
- **The opening:** small, non-technical shops will never run a software dashboard
  themselves. They want someone to *do it for them* and stand next to them when the big
  customer asks questions. That "done-for-you, human-on-the-phone" service is the gap.

## Strengths

- **It's a true must-have, not a nice-to-have.** No report card = no purchase orders.
  The customer feels the pain immediately and already has budget for it.
- **It repeats and almost never cancels** — the rule is permanent.
- **It's cheap and fast to start** and pays for itself quickly.
- **Perfect fit for the founders'** background (running security/compliance programs)
  and their strength in marketing and selling.

## Weaknesses

- **Legal danger:** the company is helping a customer make official promises to the
  U.S. government about its security. If a customer's score turns out wrong, the
  government can investigate (this is real — there's a federal "Civil Cyber-Fraud"
  effort), and the helper company can get dragged into it. Also, the dashboard would
  hold sensitive government-controlled information ("CUI"), which is a juicy target for
  hackers and triggers heavy data-handling rules.
- **Copycats:** the service itself isn't hard to copy; the edge is relationships and
  reputation, not secret technology.
- **Money ceiling:** the hands-on setup work is heavy, so past ~60–70 shops you have to
  keep hiring experts, which slows the climb.

## How the council scored it

**Average of the five experts: 7.56 out of 10** *(after the council argued the
optimistic scores back down — the "red team")*. This was the **highest score of all
twelve businesses we studied**, and its goals-checklist total (79.5) was the closest
of any to passing.

| Expert (what they judge) | Average score | Quick read |
|---|---|---|
| **CFO** (money) | 7.7 | Excellent — pays up front, high profit margins, funds its own growth. Best part of the whole business. |
| **CMO** (marketing) | 7.6 | Sharp pitch ("keep your defense contracts safe"), real ways to find customers, but the product is copyable. |
| **COO** (operations) | 7.5 | Clean to run, no fieldwork — but the deep expert work piles onto one specialist, and a mistake is serious. |
| **Legal** (rules/risk) | 7.3 | No license needed and rules are stable, BUT two scary low spots (below). |
| **CTO** (technology) | 7.7 | Boring, easy-to-build software; can earn money before it's even finished. |

**The two scores that sank it (both from Legal):**
- **Data privacy: 5** — the dashboard would hold sensitive government information.
- **Legal liability: 5** — risk of being pulled into a government fraud investigation if
  a customer's score is wrong.

These two 5s break the "no score below 7" rule by themselves, no matter how good
everything else is.

**Biggest strengths:** must-have demand, money model, founder fit.
**Biggest weaknesses:** the government-data/legal-risk pair, plus copycat risk.

### Goals checklist (our criteria) — total **79.5 / 100** (need 80 to pass)

|---|---|---|---|
| Must-have problem | 20 | 9 | Government forces the buy; no selling needed. |

| Competition | 15 | 6 | Sleepy incumbents (good) but well-funded Vanta/Drata could move in. |
| Path to founder income | 15 | 7 | Reaches the income goals, but the $500K step needs hiring. |
| Likely to work | 10 | 8 | Demand is proven and obvious. |
| Cheap to start | 10 | 9 | Very low startup cost. |
| Easy to operate | 5 | 8 | Remote, no trucks/fieldwork. |
| Room to grow | 5 | 7 | Can grow, but expert-hiring caps the speed. |
| Fits the founders | 5 | 8 | Squarely in their wheelhouse. |

**One-fix-that-matters:** if the dashboard is redesigned so it **never holds the
sensitive government data** (artifact-only) and the legal risk is insured, those two
Legal 5s could rise to 7 — which would push both the average and the goals total over
the line. This is the single closest business to passing in the whole project.

# 2. FoundationFile
### Shared grantee due-diligence network for private foundations

## The business

**The problem (in plain terms).** Charitable foundations (the organizations that give
away grant money) are required by IRS rules to check out every group they give money to
*before* they send the check. They have to confirm the group is a real, qualified
charity, run it against terrorist/sanctions watch-lists, and — for grants to unusual or
foreign groups — do extra paperwork called "expenditure responsibility" and keep
collecting reports for years. If they mess this up, they owe special taxes and it shows
up as a black mark on their public tax form. Today, foundations do this with
spreadsheets, a part-time helper, and their accountant, and they live in fear of
accidentally funding the wrong group. Here's the kicker: **thousands of foundations give
money to the same popular charities and each one re-does the exact same checks every
year** — massive wasted effort.

**The solution.** FoundationFile is a "we handle your grant-checking" subscription plus
a clever shared library. It keeps all your grant-compliance paperwork correct and ready
for an audit. The clever part: once a charity has been checked, that verified check can
be **reused by every foundation that funds it** — instead of everyone re-checking the
same charity from scratch. So the more foundations join, the bigger and more useful the
shared library of pre-checked charities becomes.

The foundation still makes every grant decision and signs its own tax forms — the
service just does the checking and keeps the records audit-ready.

## How it makes money (economic model)

- **Yearly subscription** of roughly **$9,000–$15,000 per foundation**, paid up front,
  with extra fees for foundations that make lots of complex or foreign grants.
- It repeats every year because the checking never stops, and the records become the
  foundation's system of record (painful to leave).
- **The math:** roughly **160 foundations** would get the founders to their year-1
  income goal; about **270** by year 2. There are tens of thousands of foundations, so
  that's well under 2% of the market.
- **Very cheap to build:** the core data (IRS charity lists, government watch-lists) is
  **public and free**, and a tax expert handles the tricky judgment calls part-time.

## Who else is out there (competitive landscape)

- **Foundant, Fluxx, Blackbaud, Submittable** — grant-management software. They help
  foundations *track* their grants, but they don't *do the checking* for you.
- **Candid / GuideStar "Charity Check"** — sells the basic charity-status data lookup.
  (Risk: part of FoundationFile's simplest feature is a thin wrapper around what Candid
  already sells.)
- **NGOsource (owned by Candid/TechSoup)** — this is the important one. NGOsource
  **already runs a "check once, reuse across many foundations" service** for the
  hardest part (foreign-charity equivalency). So the single most valuable piece of
  FoundationFile's "shared network" idea is **already owned by an established player.**
- **The opening:** nobody packages the *whole job* — the checking, the ongoing
  paperwork, the audit-ready file, all done for you — as one managed service for
  small/mid foundations. That bundle is real and unoccupied.

## Strengths

- **Required by law, repeats forever, and the cost of a mistake (taxes, public black
  mark) is much bigger than the fee** — a genuine must-have.
- **This was the one business where the legal/risk picture was clean.** The legal
  responsibility stays with the foundation (they sign their own forms), and there's a
  recognized legal "safe harbor" for the trickiest work. No license needed.
- **Super cheap to start** — runs on free public data.
- **A real "gets better as it grows" engine** *if* the shared library works: each new
  charity checked helps every member.

## Weaknesses

- **The "shared library" is a chicken-and-egg problem.** On day one the library is
  empty, so there's no reuse benefit yet — and the single hardest, most valuable piece
  is already owned by NGOsource.
- **Will foundations actually trust someone else's check?** Their lawyers may insist
  each foundation re-do its own homework anyway — if so, the service saves time but not
  the full cost, and the price has to drop.
- **The buyer is slow.** Foundations move at a committee/board pace, so signing
  customers takes a while.

## How the council scored it

**Average of the five experts: 7.24 out of 10** (after the red-team). Notably, this was
the **only one of the twelve with a clean legal score** — its weakness was somewhere
else entirely.

| Expert | Average score | Quick read |
|---|---|---|
| **CFO** (money) | 7.1 | Great cash pattern and cheap to start, but worried the "network" doesn't truly cut cost, just time — which lowers the price. |
| **CMO** (marketing) | 6.7 | Good pitch and clear customers, but the "shared network" promise is empty at launch and partly already owned. |
| **COO** (operations) | 7.4 | Very clean to run; ~70% of the work is automatic; one part-time expert handles the rest. |
| **Legal** (rules/risk) | 7.5 | **Clean** — responsibility stays with the foundation, no license, safe harbor exists, low-risk data. |
| **CTO** (technology) | 7.5 | Easy build on free public data; charity ID number is a clean way to match records. |

**The score that sank it:**
- **Defensibility (from marketing): 5** — the "check once, reuse everywhere" moat is a
  cold-start *and* its best piece is already owned by NGOsource. The honest defensible
  advantage is just "we keep your audit file," which is real but modest.

**Biggest strengths:** clean legal picture, cheap build, must-have demand, real
repeating revenue.
**Biggest weaknesses:** weak/occupied moat, slow buyers, price might compress.

### Goals checklist — total **73 / 100** (need 80)

| Our goal | Weight | Score | Plain reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Must-have problem | 20 | 8 | IRS forces it; mistakes cost taxes + public black mark. |
| Repeating revenue | 15 | 8 | Checking never stops; sticky records. |
| Competition | 15 | 6 | Sleepy software incumbents, but NGOsource already owns the best piece. |
| Path to founder income | 15 | 6 | Reachable, but slow buyers and possible price compression. |
| Likely to work | 10 | 6 | Demand is real; the "reuse" assumption is unproven. |

**One-fix-that-matters:** before building the "network," cheaply test two things using
free public tax-form data — (1) do foundations actually fund enough of the *same*
charities for reuse to matter, and (2) will their lawyers let them rely on a shared
check? If both are "yes," the defensibility score climbs and this becomes the best
candidate to re-submit. If "no," reposition it as "we keep your audit file" and price
accordingly.

# 3. InsuranceCarrierAuditOps
### Managed workers'-comp premium-audit defense

## The business

**The problem (in plain terms).** Almost every business with employees has to buy
workers'-compensation insurance (it covers workers who get hurt on the job). Once a year
the insurance company does a "premium audit" — it checks the business's payroll and
decides the final bill. Here's the catch: the insurance company's auditor has **no
reason to catch mistakes in the business's favor.** Workers get put in the wrong (more
expensive) job category, overtime gets counted wrong, and a thing called the
"experience-mod" (a multiplier based on past injuries) often has errors. The result:
businesses routinely **overpay by 5–15% every single year** — that can be $15,000 to
$45,000 a year on a $300,000 policy — and most owners have no way to know, because
nobody on their team reads insurance class-code rules.

**The solution.** A "we defend your audit" subscription. The company manages the yearly
premium audit for the business, fixes wrong job classifications and experience-mod
errors, disputes the overcharges, and wins back money the business never owed. It
combines software (a class-code system plus a growing record of what disputes actually
win) with a former insurance-company auditor's expertise — and gets paid partly from the
money it saves you.

## How it makes money (economic model)

- A **yearly subscription** (mid-four to low-five figures) **plus a cut (around 20–30%)
  of the money it recovers or saves** ("contingency fee").
- The "we mostly get paid from what we save you" offer makes the sale easy — the
  customer risks little.
- It repeats because the audit happens **every year** and the experience-mod has to be
  watched every year.
- **The math:** roughly **20–25 customers** in year 1 gets each founder near ~$300K;
  about **40+** gets toward ~$500K. Very high profit margins because there's no product
  to ship — just expertise and software.

## Who else is out there (competitive landscape)

- **One-off "premium recovery" consultants** — they swoop in after a bad bill, take a
  cut, then leave. They prove the demand is real but are sleepy, reactive, and not set
  up as an ongoing service.
- **Insurance brokers** (Hub, Marsh McLennan Agency) — they sell the policy but usually
  don't police the audit, because it's unpaid work that can annoy the insurance company
  they depend on. That neglected job is the opening.
- **PEOs** (companies that bundle payroll + benefits) — they remove the problem for
  businesses that use them, so they shrink the target market a bit.
- **The opening:** a recurring, software-backed "watchdog" that defends the audit *every
  year* — nobody really owns that lane today.

## Strengths

- **The value is in real dollars the customer already feels** — money back, not a vague
  benefit. Easy to prove ROI.
- **Excellent money model:** very high margins, low startup cost, and the cut-of-savings
  offer makes selling easier.
- **Strong founder fit** — the founders have deep insurance-industry background and can
  get licensed easily (they're plugged into a network of licensed people).
- **A possible data advantage:** a growing record of "what disputes actually win"
  across many clients could become a real edge over time.

## Weaknesses

- **Legal license trap (the big one):** giving paid advice about insurance premiums and
  class codes can count as "insurance consulting" that **requires a license** in states
  like California, Florida, and New York — and the cut-of-savings fee makes regulators
  *more* likely to see it that way. *(Note: the founders say this is easily solved — they
  can get licensed and are plugged into licensed partners. If true, this is the single
  biggest fix and removes the scariest problem — see below.)*
- **"Hidden" demand:** the customer doesn't *know* they're overpaying, so you first have
  to convince them the problem exists. And the category sits next to spammy "we'll lower
  your bills!" cold-callers, so trust must be earned.
- **One-expert bottleneck:** the deep class-code judgment leans on one scarce specialist,
  and audits bunch up at certain times of year (when many policies renew at once).

## How the council scored it

**Average of the five experts: 6.60 out of 10** (after the red-team). This was a FAIL,
mostly dragged down by the legal-license issue.

| Expert | Average score | Quick read |
|---|---|---|
| **CFO** (money) | 7.7 | Excellent margins, low startup cost, easy ROI sale. Strongest part. |
| **CMO** (marketing) | 6.7 | Strong "free look at your last audit" wedge, but the customer doesn't know they have the problem, and the offer is copyable. |
| **COO** (operations) | 5.9 | Worried: one scarce expert, audits bunch seasonally, heavy setup per client. |
| **Legal** (rules/risk) | 5.6 | **The killer** — likely needs an insurance-consultant license; the cut-of-savings fee makes it worse. |
| **CTO** (technology) | 7.3 | Easy to build; expert stays in the loop so no risky automation. |

**The scores that sank it (from Legal):**
- **Industry rules burden: 4**, **Licensing: 4**, **Jurisdictional (state-by-state): 4**
  — all about the licensing question.

**Biggest strengths:** money model, dollar-denominated ROI, founder fit.
**Biggest weaknesses:** the licensing trap, hidden demand, single-expert bottleneck.

### Goals checklist — total **≈69 / 100** (need 80)

| Our goal | Weight | Score | Plain reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Must-have problem | 20 | 6 | Real money at stake, but the buyer doesn't know they have the problem (hidden demand). |
| Repeating revenue | 15 | 7 | Audit recurs yearly, but value is front-loaded. |
| Competition | 15 | 6 | Sleepy one-off recovery firms; brokers neglect the job. |
| Path to founder income | 15 | 8 | Reaches the income goals at a low customer count. |
| Likely to work | 10 | 6 | Demand is real but must be "manufactured" at the top. |
| Cheap to start | 10 | 8 | Low startup cost, high margin. |
| Easy to operate | 5 | 5 | One-expert bottleneck + seasonal bunching + heavy setup. |
| Room to grow | 5 | 6 | Scales, but expert hiring is the cap. |
| Fits the founders | 5 | 7 | Deep insurance background — strong fit. |

**One-fix-that-matters (two, actually):** (1) **Get licensed** — the founders say this
is easy and they're plugged into licensed partners; this lifts the three Legal 4s and
removes the scariest, near-fatal problem. (2) **Use public data to find likely
overpayers** (for example, public workplace-injury data showing a company with very few
injuries but a high bill, plus published class-code rate tables) — this turns "hidden
demand" into "we already think you're overpaying about $X, send us your audit to
confirm." Together these attack the two biggest problems and could also seed a real data
advantage. Even so, the one-expert operations bottleneck and the copyable core would
still need work to clear the full bar.

# Side-by-Side Comparison

| **What's blocking it** | Gov't-data + legal-risk pair | Weak/occupied "network" moat | Licensing trap + hidden demand |

| **Bottom line** | Closest to passing; one legal redesign away | Cleanest legal; needs a real moat | Best money model; license fix is the unlock |

**How to read the comparison:** all three have **strong money models** and are **true
must-haves** — that's why they rose to the top. They each fail the bar for a *different*
single reason: SecureControls on **legal risk from holding government data**,
FoundationFile on a **weak/already-taken moat**, and InsuranceCarrierAudit on a
**licensing rule** (which the founders believe they can clear). The pattern across the
whole project: a business in this space tends to have either **clean legal risk OR a
strong moat — but rarely both at once**, and the bar requires both.
