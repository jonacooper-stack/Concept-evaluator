CLEAN-ROOM ONE-PAGER — Certified-Payroll & Prevailing-Wage Compliance for Public-Works Contractors

CUSTOMER
Small-to-mid construction subcontractors and prime contractors that perform public-works
projects — government-funded building, roads, schools, utilities, transit, and federally
funded private projects. The core buyer is the owner, controller, or payroll
administrator at a specialty subcontractor (electrical, mechanical, concrete, drywall,
painting, landscaping, glazing, etc.) with roughly 5–150 field employees who runs anywhere
from a handful to dozens of public jobs a year. These firms are numerous, regional, and
fragmented; most run lean back offices where one bookkeeper or office manager wrestles
prevailing-wage paperwork by hand. Secondary buyer: prime/general contractors who must
collect and vet their subs' certified payrolls before the agency will pay them.

THE MUST-HAVE / FORCING FUNCTION (critical input to getting paid)
On any public-works job covered by the federal Davis-Bacon Act or a state "little
Davis-Bacon"/prevailing-wage law, the contractor is legally required to (1) pay
government-set prevailing wages and fringe benefits by worker classification, and (2)
submit a CERTIFIED PAYROLL REPORT every single week for every worker on the job — federally
on form WH-347, and through state systems (for example California's DIR electronic
certified-payroll portal, plus New York, Washington, and many others, each with its own
form and rules). The reports must reconcile hours, classifications, wage rates, fringe
credits, and apprenticeship ratios exactly. Here is the forcing function: if the certified
payroll is late, missing, or wrong, the awarding agency or the prime WITHHOLDS the
contractor's progress payment until it is fixed — and repeated or willful violations bring
penalties, back-wage assessments, and debarment from future public work. In other words, no
compliant certified payroll means the contractor does not get paid for work already
performed, and can lose the right to bid public jobs at all. The buy is forced (you cannot
legally run the job without it), weekly and recurring (every payroll period, every project),
already budgeted as a cost of doing public work, and bought without anyone needing to be
convinced the problem exists — every public-works contractor already lives this pain.

PRODUCT
A productized "certified payroll, handled every week" subscription. The platform ingests the
contractor's existing payroll data (via integration with common payroll/accounting systems
or a simple weekly import), applies the correct prevailing-wage determinations and fringe
rules by project and worker classification, validates the data against the rules (catching
the classification, overtime, fringe, and apprenticeship-ratio errors that get payments
withheld), generates the exact federal and state certified-payroll forms required for each
job, and files or pushes them into whatever destination the agency mandates (federal WH-347,
state portals, or prime-contractor collection systems). It maintains the audit trail, flags
missing weeks, and gives the contractor a single dashboard showing "every job is filed and
clean / here is what is at risk this week." A thin managed-service layer (founder-led,
scaffolded by a named prevailing-wage specialist) handles onboarding setup of wage
determinations and supports edge-case classifications.

REVENUE MODEL
Recurring SaaS subscription, priced per active public-works project per month plus a base
platform fee — roughly $300–$1,200/month for a typical small-to-mid contractor depending on
the number of active jobs and field employees, billed monthly or annually. An onboarding fee
(~$500–$2,000) covers initial payroll-system connection and wage-determination setup. Revenue
recurs for as long as the contractor does public work (i.e., continuously), and switching
mid-project is painful because the audit trail and filings live in the system.

POSITIONING
"Certified payroll, done right, every week — so you get paid on every public job." The
done-for-you prevailing-wage compliance system built for the contractor's back office,
replacing error-prone manual WH-347 spreadsheets, generalist bookkeepers who don't know
prevailing wage, and clunky agency-side portals that were built for the government to collect
forms, not for the contractor to produce them correctly. It turns a weekly paperwork
landmine that holds up six-figure progress payments into a quiet, reliable, fixed-price
routine.
