LEGAL / REGULATORY REVIEW — Compressed Air Optimization Service

(Note: I am not a licensed attorney and this is not formal legal advice. This is an experienced operator's regulatory pattern-recognition, intended to flag what the founders should brief real counsel on before launch.)

ONE-PARAGRAPH RISK READ

This is one of the cleaner regulatory profiles I evaluate: a B2B technical-services business — surveying air leaks, writing reports, and selling a recurring inspection contract — with no licensed trade, no regulated industry, and essentially no consumer-protection exposure because the buyer is an industrial plant, not a consumer. The genuine legal work here is mundane and front-loadable: well-drafted commercial service agreements that carve out warranty and savings-guarantee liability, general/professional liability insurance, OSHA-aware field-safety practices, and clean contractor classification if technicians are 1099. The two risks that actually need adult supervision are (a) the "shared-savings" / "projected savings" claims, which convert a low-risk service into a quasi-financial-guarantee with potential deceptive-advertising and breach-of-contract exposure, and (b) the founders themselves walking through energized industrial machinery on customer sites, where a worker injury creates real liability that must be insured and contractually allocated.

REGULATORY MAP

- No industry-specific licensing regime. Compressed-air leak surveying is not a licensed trade. There is no federal or state "energy auditor" or "compressed-air specialist" license required to walk a plant, point an ultrasonic camera at fittings, and write a report. This is unlike electrical, HVAC, plumbing, or general contracting, which trigger state contractor boards. Confirm only that the scope stays at survey/report/coordination and does NOT cross into the founders themselves performing licensed repair work.
- OSHA (Occupational Safety and Health Administration, 29 CFR 1910) — General Industry standards. This applies to the WORKERS performing surveys on industrial floors (walking near rotating machinery, energized equipment, compressed-air systems, lockout/tagout zones under 29 CFR 1910.147, hearing protection under 1910.95, PPE under 1910.132). The host plant carries primary OSHA duty for its own site, but a contractor whose employee is injured triggers workers' comp and potential multi-employer worksite liability. Burden: moderate, procedural. This is the most operationally real regime here.
- FTC Act §5 (15 U.S.C. §45) and state UDAP statutes — advertising substantiation. Claims like "recover tens of thousands of dollars," "20–30% leak losses," and "projected energy $ savings" are quantified efficacy claims and must be substantiable. In a B2B context the FTC enforcement risk is low relative to consumer marketing, but state UDAP statutes and ordinary breach/misrepresentation claims are the live exposure if a "guaranteed savings" pitch underdelivers. Burden: low if claims are framed as ranges/estimates and the shared-savings metering methodology is contractually defined.
- Utility rebate / energy-efficiency program rules (state public utility commissions + individual utilities + program administrators, e.g., measurement-and-verification protocols like IPMVP). If the company prepares or submits rebate paperwork on the customer's behalf, it becomes responsible for the accuracy of metered-savings claims to a utility. Misstating savings to capture a rebate is the one place this business could touch something resembling fraud against a regulated utility program. Burden: low-to-moderate, manageable with honest M&V; keep the customer as the named rebate applicant where possible.
- Worker classification — IRS common-law test, U.S. DOL FLSA 2024 independent-contractor economic-reality rule, and state tests (California ABC test under AB 5 / Labor Code §2775; Massachusetts, New Jersey similar). Routing technicians on fixed schedules in company-branded survey work looks a lot like employment; calling them 1099 in an ABC-test state is the classic misclassification trap. Burden: low if W-2; elevated if 1099 in strict states.
- Data privacy — minimal. The data collected is industrial operational data (CFM loss, equipment location, kWh) plus ordinary B2B contact info. This is NOT personal data in any special category. No HIPAA, no biometric (BIPA), no children's data (COPPA), no GLBA. CCPA/CPRA applies only to California consumer/employee/B2B-contact personal information at scale and only if thresholds are met. Burden: negligible.
- General commercial regimes — entity formation, sales/use tax on services (varies by state), standard commercial contracting. Routine.

LICENSING SUMMARY

No specialized occupational or trade license is required to perform the core service as scoped — survey, detect, report, and coordinate. There is no national or state license for "compressed air optimization," "leak detection," or "energy surveying." This is the single biggest legal advantage of the concept and it is explicitly consistent with the founder profile, which forbids businesses requiring specialized credentials. The one boundary to police: the moment a technician personally turns a wrench to FIX a leak (replacing piping, fittings, valves, drains, or doing electrical work on compressors), the company may step into state contractor/mechanical/electrical licensing territory, which varies by state and is exactly the multi-state cliff to avoid. The one-pager already scopes this correctly — "optional coordination/verification of repairs" rather than performing them — and that scoping should be a hard contractual line. Each operating state will need a routine business registration / foreign-entity qualification, and a local business license in some municipalities; these are paperwork, not gatekeepers. Per state, expect $50–$800 in registration/qualification fees, no exams, no continuing education.

LIABILITY POSTURE

The entity's exposure clusters in three buckets. First, BODILY INJURY / PROPERTY DAMAGE on customer premises — a technician trips a control, leans on a valve, or is injured near machinery. This is handled by a Commercial General Liability policy (typically $1M/$2M, a few thousand dollars a year for this risk class), workers' compensation for any employees (mandatory in nearly every state once you have W-2 staff), and contractual provisions: the customer should provide site safety orientation and the service agreement should mutually allocate liability. Second, PROFESSIONAL / ECONOMIC liability — the report's savings projections are wrong, the customer fixes the wrong things, or promised savings don't materialize. This is addressed by Professional Liability / Errors & Omissions insurance and, more importantly, by contract language: present savings as estimates, disclaim consequential damages, cap liability at fees paid, and define any shared-savings calculation with an explicit, agreed M&V methodology. Third, PERSONAL exposure pre-entity — until an LLC or corporation is formed, the founders are personally on the hook for everything above. Forming the entity, getting CGL + E&O + workers' comp, and using a lawyer-reviewed master service agreement essentially closes the gap. There is no catastrophic, uninsurable tail risk here. That is a strong posture.

BIGGEST SINGLE RISK

The single issue most likely to cause real damage is the savings guarantee, especially the optional shared-savings component. The entire sales pitch is ROI-anchored — "recovers most of it," "pays for itself several times over," "verified, metered energy reduction" — and the moment those numbers move from marketing puffery into a contractual term, the company has taken on an obligation whose performance depends on factors outside its control: whether the customer actually fixes the leaks the report identifies, how the plant runs its shifts, electricity price volatility, and how energy savings are metered and attributed. A shared-savings deal is effectively a bet on a number the company does not fully control, and disputes over the measurement-and-verification methodology are the predictable battleground. The exposure is twofold: a breach-of-contract / misrepresentation claim from an unhappy customer, and a state UDAP or FTC Act §5 deceptive-claims theory if marketed savings are not substantiated. None of this is fatal, but it is the difference between a clean liability profile and one that ends up in fee disputes. The fix is contractual and disciplined: present savings as estimates with disclaimers, define the M&V methodology precisely (anchored to an objective protocol such as IPMVP), cap liability at fees paid, exclude consequential damages, and tie any shared-savings payout to an agreed, auditable baseline.

QUESTIONS FOR REAL COUNSEL BEFORE LAUNCH
- Does our scope of work (survey, report, coordinate repairs but never perform them) keep us fully outside state contractor, mechanical, and electrical licensing in our initial target states — and where exactly is the line we must not cross if a technician ever physically adjusts a fitting or valve?
- How should the master service agreement frame savings projections and any shared-savings component to be enforceable yet shield us from breach and misrepresentation claims — liability caps, consequential-damages waivers, defined M&V — and do our advertised figures meet UDAP/FTC §5 substantiation standards in B2B?
- For field technicians on industrial floors, what is our correct worker classification in each target state given the ABC test (California §2775, Massachusetts, New Jersey), what workers' comp is mandatory, and how do we contractually allocate OSHA multi-employer-worksite and premises-injury risk with the host plant?
- What insurance program (CGL limits, E&O, workers' comp, inland marine for leased imaging equipment) and entity structure (LLC vs. S-corp) best ring-fence personal liability before the first survey?
- If we prepare utility rebate paperwork on a customer's behalf, what liability do we assume for the accuracy of metered-savings claims, and how do we keep the customer as the named applicant?
