CFO REVIEW — Water Utility Revenue-Recovery Program

ONE-PARAGRAPH FINANCIAL READ
The unit economics here are genuinely attractive on paper — annual recurring contracts of $25K–$75K against a mostly-labor cost base, with a self-funding pitch that should ease the close. The fatal arithmetic problem is the $2M-per-founder-in-24-months target: at a blended ~$45K ARPU, you need roughly 89 paying utilities under contract inside two years to clear ~$4M revenue, and even at that revenue the gross dollars after field-verification labor leave thin distributable income for two founders. The real constraint is not margin or capital — it is the sales cycle. Public water utilities buy through boards, budget cycles, and procurement processes measured in many months to over a year, so reaching ~90 signed multi-year contracts in 24 months is a heroic, not conservative, assumption.

TOP 3 FINANCIAL STRENGTHS
- Capital-light launch: low-five-figure startup vs $100K–$250K self-funding capacity; no pre-revenue capital gates viability.

TOP 3 FINANCIAL RISKS
- The 24-month income target is missed by a wide margin: ~137–178 signed contracts needed vs a realistic 15–40 given municipal sales cycles.
- Margin is mid-tier, not high: field-verification labor and travel hold blended margin to ~55–65% [ASSUMED].
- Renewal economics decay: year-1 recovers the easy dollars; years 2+ have less to find, pressuring renewal price and the value story.

BIGGEST SINGLE RISK
The single financial issue most likely to kill the income thesis is the mismatch between the customer-count required and the public-sector sales cycle. Reaching $4M of distributable founder income in 24 months requires on the order of 137–178 signed multi-year contracts, i.e., closing 6–8 new utilities every month from a standing start. But the buyers are municipal/quasi-municipal bodies that approve new vendor spend through board/council votes, annual budget cycles, and often formal procurement — a process that for a $40K new line item commonly runs 6–12+ months per deal. That cadence makes the customer count arithmetically unreachable on the stated timeline; deals signed late in year 2 barely lift run-rate before month 24. This does not make the business bad — it makes it a $1.5M-to-$3M-revenue business by month 24, ~$300K–$600K per founder, a strong outcome but roughly a third of the stated $1M-each target. The thesis-level risk is not insolvency; it is a target off by a multiple because of how slowly the chosen customer buys.
