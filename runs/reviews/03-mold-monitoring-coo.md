COO REVIEW — White-Label Moisture & Mold Monitoring for Restoration Firms

ONE-PARAGRAPH OPERATIONS READ
This is operationally a two-sided physical-plus-software business, and the physical side is the part that worries me. The software (cloud IoT ingestion, alerting, white-label dashboards) is genuinely two-founder-buildable and low-touch once running. But the revenue spine — sensors physically installed in basements and crawlspaces, phoning home reliably over wifi/cellular for years — drags in hardware logistics (procurement, MOQs, RMA/returns, battery replacement), connectivity flakiness (a basement is the worst RF environment in a house), and a channel partner who actually does the installs but whose incentives and competence you do not control. The founders never touch a single end-home, which is good for their travel/labor load, but it means their entire delivery quality is outsourced to thousands of independent contractors who will install inconsistently and churn. Two people can build and run the platform; whether two people can wrangle the hardware supply chain and a long-tail contractor channel into a clean, repeatable operation is the real question.

THE TUESDAY-IN-MARCH WALKTHROUGH
It is Tuesday, March 17, ~12 months post-launch. [ASSUMED] ~40 contractors signed and ~1,800 homes under monitoring (verify against actual pipeline; generous-but-not-heroic). Mike starts in the contractor-side dashboard and his inbox. Three things are queued. First, a waterproofing contractor in Cleveland emailed at 6am: eight of his monitored homes "went dark" over the weekend. Mike must diagnose remotely — dead batteries? homeowner unplugged the gateway? wifi password changed after a router swap? cellular sensor out of coverage in a concrete basement? He cannot see the device; he can only see the absence of data and email the contractor to send a tech back out, which the contractor resents as unbilled truck-roll. Second, a "moisture danger" alert fired overnight in Pittsburgh; the homeowner got a branded 2am push, panicked, called the contractor at 7am — but it was a false positive from a sensor bumped near a dryer vent. The contractor is angry that "your system" scared his customer; Mike spends 40 minutes calming him and tuning the threshold. Third, a franchise group wants a demo and a data-security questionnaire. Meanwhile Jon deals with supply chain: a 500-unit sensor reorder is two weeks late, a newly signed contractor needs 60 units to start, and Jon is hand-packing white-label sensor kits from a garage or 3PL. He also pushes a dashboard fix (a contractor's logo renders wrong on the monthly report). By mid-afternoon the recurring work — billing 40 contractors per-home, reconciling who added/removed homes, chasing a declined card — eats two hours. Nobody installed anything; nobody drove anywhere. The day is hardware failure triage, false-positive management, channel hand-holding, and billing reconciliation — and it scales linearly with homes-under-monitoring and number of contractors.

- False positives: [ASSUMED] 1/home/year → ~240/week, each a potential angry-contractor call.
- Billing/account ops: 12,500 homes across [ASSUMED] 150–250 contractors = 150–250 monthly invoices, dunning, add/remove reconciliation.
Total founder-touch events at scale: ~100–150/week. At ~20 min each = 35–50 hrs/week of pure support/ops — one full person before any selling or building. Requires a hire (~$60–80k CS/support-ops + a 3PL). Affordable against $3M ARR. The bottleneck is NOT founder labor at steady state; it is (a) the front-loaded sales motion (signing 150–250 contractors one at a time) and (b) hardware fleet reliability, which determines whether support load is 100 or 500 events/week.
Throughput verdict: steady-state platform runnable by 2 founders + 1–2 support/ops + a 3PL (within "lean team"). But reaching 12,500 homes requires ~150–250 signed contractors averaging ~50–80 homes each, or fewer large franchise groups — a contractor-acquisition count, by founders who don't love high-volume closing, that is the throughput risk.

Quality control simplicity: 3 — Quality is set in the basement by a third party and by RF physics, both outside founder control; a false-positive scares a homeowner and a false-negative (mold regrows undetected behind a wall the system was sold to watch) is a liability-grade failure that lands on the contractor's brand and back on the founders — high variance, hard to standardize.

AVERAGE SCORE: 5.0 / 10

TOP 3 OPERATIONAL STRENGTHS
- Founders own zero field labor and zero truck-rolls — installs ride the contractor's existing job, so no national install fleet, no scheduling/routing nightmare, no W-2 field techs across 50 states. The single best operational feature.
- The first hire is a cheap, available generalist (CS/support-ops [ASSUMED] $60–80k), not scarce specialized labor; with a 3PL, the steady-state team stays genuinely lean against $3M ARR.
- Per-home recurring billing is seat-like and expands automatically as a contractor adds homes — once bought in, account growth needs no new founder selling.

TOP 3 OPERATIONAL RISKS
- Hardware reliability and connectivity in the worst RF environment (concrete basements, crawlspaces). "Went dark" sensors and false positives generate support load that scales linearly with the fleet and lands on the contractor's brand, degrading the very relationship the product exists to protect.
- The contractor channel is both the customer and the uncontrolled delivery agent. Install quality, homeowner messaging, and retention all sit with thousands of independent shops the founders cannot train or supervise; one bad install becomes "your system failed" and the contractor can churn the entire home book at once.
- A false-negative is liability-grade. Sold as "your home is still dry," a missed moisture event that lets mold regrow behind a wall is not a refund — it is a reputational and potential-liability event that can poison contractor confidence in the whole program.

BIGGEST SINGLE RISK
The risk most likely to make me say "I'm not sure two people can do this" is that the founders own the consequences of a physical sensor fleet they neither install nor can see. Every device sits in someone else's basement, installed by someone they didn't train, on a wifi network they don't control, reporting (or failing to report) over RF through concrete. At 12,500 homes that is ~37,500 devices generating a steady drumbeat of dead-zone failures, dead batteries, bumped sensors, and false alarms — each a ticket that flows back to the founders because the contractor will not eat unbilled truck-rolls to chase the founders' hardware problems. The deeper trap is asymmetry of blame: the product's entire promise is "we'll catch the moisture before it becomes damage," so a single false-negative behind a finished wall converts directly into "your monitoring failed and now I have mold," which torches the contractor's brand (the brand on the product) and, with it, the founders' recurring book through that channel. Software businesses fail softly; a sensor business sold as a safety promise fails loudly and litigiously. Two founders can build the platform, but they are signing up to be the silent backstop for the field reliability of tens of thousands of devices and the install competence of a long tail of contractors — heavier than the one-pager acknowledges.

QUESTIONS THE FOUNDERS MUST ANSWER BEFORE I'M COMFORTABLE
- What is the real-world sensor reporting-failure rate in actual basements/crawlspaces over 90 days, and the false-positive AND false-negative rate of the alerting threshold? (Run a 25-home pilot across 3 contractors; everything in the capacity math keys off this.)
- Who physically handles a hardware failure, and who pays for the truck-roll — contractor's unbilled cost, billable homeowner visit, or founder-supplied part via 3PL — and have you confirmed contractors accept that before they sign?
- What is the contractor concentration and churn assumption? How many contractors to reach 12,500 homes, realistic average homes-per-contractor, and what happens to that book when a mid-size contractor churns or goes out of business?
- Supply chain: single-sourced sensor/gateway? MOQ, lead time, deposit, and dual-source/inventory-buffer plan when a 500-unit reorder slips while a new contractor waits on 60 units?
- Liability: who carries the risk and insurance when monitoring misses an event and the homeowner suffers mold damage — contractor under their brand, or founders as the platform — and is it written into the contractor agreement?
