LEGAL / REGULATORY REVIEW — Fractional Data & Analytics Leader for Mid-Market Companies

(I am not a licensed attorney and this is not formal legal advice. Experienced operator flagging issues for real counsel.)

ONE-PARAGRAPH RISK READ
This is a low-burden, B2B professional-services business with no industry licensing requirement and no consumer-facing regulatory exposure — fundamentally a healthy posture. The real risk does not sit in "is this industry regulated" (it is not) but in the data the engagement touches: by design the advisor connects to and centralizes the client's ERP, CRM, payroll, and POS, which means the founders will be handling payroll/HR data (Social Security numbers, wages, possibly health-plan deductions), customer PII, and — where clients are in healthcare services — potentially PHI subject to HIPAA via Business Associate obligations. The other meaningful exposure is professional liability: this is advisory work where a client makes "seven-figure decisions" partly on the founders' dashboards and recommendations, so a wrong number or a bad call invites an errors-and-omissions claim. Both risks are well-understood, contractually and insurably manageable, and not category-fatal — but they require disciplined contracting and a deliberate decision about which client data the founders are willing to touch.

REGULATORY MAP
- No industry licensing regime. Data analytics / management consulting is not a licensed profession in any US state. There is no analog to CPA, contractor, real-estate, or insurance licensing. Standard business entity registration and a local business license is the full extent of "licensing."
- State data-breach notification statutes (all 50 + DC; e.g., California Civ. Code 1798.82, NY SHIELD Act Gen. Bus. Law 899-aa, Massachusetts 201 CMR 17.00). Because the founders hold employee SSNs and other "personal information," a breach triggers mandatory notification in every state where affected individuals reside. SHIELD and 201 CMR 17.00 specifically mandate a written information security program when you hold MA/NY resident data.
- CCPA/CPRA (California Civ. Code 1798.100 et seq.). The founders act as a "service provider" to their business clients, not a "business" — the favorable role: obligations flow primarily through a compliant service-provider data-processing addendum and a prohibition on using client data for the founders' own purposes. CPRA also reaches employee/B2B personal information, which matters because the founders touch payroll data.
- HIPAA (45 CFR Parts 160/164) — conditional. If a client is a healthcare-services provider (a "covered entity") and the centralized data includes PHI, the founders become a "Business Associate" and must sign a BAA, implement Security Rule safeguards, and accept direct liability under HITECH. The single regime that can convert a light engagement into a heavy one. High for healthcare clients; zero if healthcare clients are excluded or firewalled from PHI.
- GLBA / financial-data rules — generally not applicable unless a client is a financial institution; mid-market distribution/manufacturing/logistics do not trigger it. Monitor only.
- Employment/contractor law (FLSA; state ABC tests, esp. California Labor Code 2775 / AB5). Applies to the founders' own use of "offshore/contract analysts." Misclassifying domestic analysts who work full-time under direction as 1099 is the live risk; offshore contractors are lower-risk but raise cross-border data-transfer questions.
- FTC Act Section 5 — applies to marketing claims ("executive-grade," ROI promises). Low burden; don't promise specific financial outcomes you can't substantiate.
- Contract law / professional liability — common-law, the dominant practical regime. Governs engagement terms, limitation of liability, IP ownership of dashboards, and confidentiality.

LICENSING SUMMARY
No professional or occupational license is required to operate as a fractional data/analytics advisor in any US jurisdiction. Management consulting and data/BI services are unlicensed professions. The founders need only: (1) a business entity (LLC/S-corp), (2) any local business license, and (3) — not a license but a practical gate — vendor/security questionnaires from larger clients. No exams, no CE, no per-state credentialing, no renewal beyond ordinary entity maintenance. One of the cleanest licensing profiles a service business can have, squarely inside the founder profile. The only "license-like" obligations are contractual: software vendors' terms must permit use on behalf of clients, which standard commercial SaaS terms do.

LIABILITY POSTURE
Three vectors. First, data custody: centralizing payroll, CRM, POS, and ERP data makes the founders custodians of employee SSNs, wage data, and customer PII. A breach triggers state notification statutes, potential CCPA statutory damages ($100–$750 per consumer per incident under Civ. Code 1798.150 for certain breaches), and contractual indemnity from the client. Insurable via cyber/tech-E&O; contractually capped via limitation-of-liability and the client bearing primary controller responsibility — but it is the heart of the risk. Second, professional liability/E&O: the client makes large decisions partly on the founders' numbers; a materially wrong dashboard (numbers don't tie out — ironically the exact pain being sold against) and a reliance-damages claim is an E&O claim. Insurable via tech/miscellaneous professional E&O (low five figures annually at this scale), contractually mitigated by an LoL cap at fees paid and an explicit "client retains decision-making responsibility" clause. Third, personal exposure pre-entity: until an LLC/corp is formed and respected, the founders are personally liable. Trivially fixed by forming the entity before the first engagement. Net: all three are standard, insurable, and contractually addressable; none catastrophic or uninsurable. The discipline required is a real MSA + DPA + BAA-where-needed and actual cyber/E&O coverage from day one — not "we'll put it in the terms of service."

TOP 3 LEGAL/REGULATORY STRENGTHS
- No licensing regime whatsoever — data/BI advisory is unlicensed in all 50 states (no exams, no per-state credentialing, no renewals), fitting the founder profile's exclusion of credential-heavy businesses perfectly.
- Pure B2B with negotiated retainers sidesteps the most punitive consumer regimes (FTC ROSCA, state auto-renewal laws, TCPA), removing an entire class of statutory landmines.
- Favorable data role: as a CCPA/CPRA "service provider" rather than a "business," and a HIPAA "Business Associate" only where healthcare clients are taken, the founders sit in the lower-liability position with obligations flowing through well-understood instruments (DPA, BAA).

TOP 3 LEGAL/REGULATORY RISKS
- Data custody: centralizing payroll SSNs, customer PII, and possibly PHI makes the founders breach-notification and CCPA-damages exposed across every state their clients touch; a single breach is the most expensive realistic event.
- Professional liability / E&O: clients make seven-figure decisions on the founders' numbers, so a materially wrong dashboard or recommendation invites a reliance-damages claim — the exact failure mode (numbers not tying out) being sold against.
- Contractor classification and cross-border data handling: the "offshore/contract analyst" lever raises FLSA/state ABC misclassification risk for domestic contractors and data-transfer/confidentiality questions for offshore staff with access to client systems.

BIGGEST SINGLE RISK
The biggest single risk is data-custody liability arising from centralizing the client's most sensitive systems — specifically payroll (Social Security numbers, wages, possibly health-plan and garnishment data) and, for any healthcare-services client, PHI. The moment the founders wire these systems into a single warehouse they hold, they become a data custodian whose breach triggers mandatory notification under all 50 states' statutes (California Civ. Code 1798.82, the NY SHIELD Act, Massachusetts 201 CMR 17.00, which also mandates a written information security program), potential CCPA statutory damages of $100–$750 per affected consumer per incident under Civ. Code 1798.150, and contractual indemnity from clients whose employees/customers were exposed. For healthcare clients, a PHI breach additionally triggers HIPAA Breach Notification obligations and direct Business Associate liability under HITECH, with civil penalties scaling into six and seven figures. Not fatal — insurable through cyber/tech-E&O and contractually mitigated by LoL clauses, a robust DPA, and the client retaining primary controller responsibility — but the issue most likely to produce a genuinely expensive event, demanding real security hygiene (encryption, access controls, incident-response plan, written information security program) in place before the first system is connected, not retrofitted later. The founders cannot "put it in the terms of service" their way out of a breach; statute governs notification and CCPA damages regardless of contract.

QUESTIONS FOR REAL COUNSEL BEFORE LAUNCH

2. Draft our MSA, DPA, mutual NDA, and limitation-of-liability framework: what LoL cap (fees paid? a multiple?), what indemnity allocation, and what "client retains decision-making responsibility" and data-controller language do we need so a wrong number or a breach does not produce uncapped exposure?
3. What cyber-liability and technology/professional (E&O) insurance limits and policy language do we need in force before the first engagement, and what do typical mid-market client security/insurance questionnaires require us to attest to?
4. How do we classify and contract with offshore and domestic contract analysts to avoid FLSA/state ABC misclassification (California Labor Code 2775) and lawfully permit offshore personnel to access client systems containing US personal data — including data-transfer, confidentiality, and IP-assignment terms?
5. Who owns the dashboards, data models, and pipeline configs we build — us (reusable IP) or the client (work-for-hire) — and how should the MSA define deliverable ownership, license-back, and our right to reuse generalized methods?
6. What minimum written information security program do MA (201 CMR 17.00) and NY (SHIELD Act) require given we hold their residents' personal information, and does our warehouse/BI stack's data residency and subprocessor list satisfy our clients' obligations?
