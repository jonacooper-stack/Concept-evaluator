LEGAL / REGULATORY REVIEW — Water Utility Revenue-Recovery Program

(Not formal legal advice. Experienced operator with regulatory pattern-recognition, flagging issues for retained counsel.)

ONE-PARAGRAPH RISK READ
This is a B2G (business-to-government) data-and-field-services business serving public and quasi-public water utilities — and the core legal exposures live not in the technical work (lightly regulated) but in three places: public procurement law (how a small town/district can lawfully buy this, and whether sole-source is permitted), the performance-linked "share of recovered revenue" fee (contingent fees tied to public funds are a classic procurement and public-finance landmine that some states and municipal charters prohibit or restrict), and the fact that the work directly alters how a public body bills residents under a board-approved or PUC-approved rate tariff (correcting "mis-rated" accounts and re-billing customers can trigger consumer-protection, utility-tariff, and statute-of-limitations-on-back-billing constraints). The data is utility billing/meter data — it includes customer PII (names, service addresses, account numbers, usage, possibly payment history), materially more sensitive than the one-pager implies, engaging state data-breach and possibly public-records regimes. None individually fatal, but the procurement-plus-contingent-fee-plus-back-billing cluster is where this concept either gets clean or gets stuck, and it varies state-by-state and even charter-by-charter — the opposite of the clean single-jurisdiction posture I score highest.

REGULATORY MAP
- Public procurement / competitive bidding law (state + local): each state has a public-contracting statute; municipalities/districts layer purchasing ordinances/charter rules. A $25K–$75K/year multi-year contract frequently exceeds local "small purchase" thresholds (often $10K–$50K), triggering competitive bidding, RFP, or formal board approval. Oversight: governing board/council + state comptroller/AG + taxpayer-suit exposure. Burden: moderate-to-high and idiosyncratic per buyer — the central operational-legal cost.
- Contingent/performance-based compensation tied to public funds: the "share of first-year recovered revenue" collides with public-finance rules in several states that restrict or bar contingent-fee public contracts (anti-kickback, gift-of-public-funds, improper-incentive doctrines). Some states explicitly prohibit contingent-fee arrangements for governmental revenue/tax recovery. Burden: potentially fatal to that specific fee structure in some jurisdictions; flat annual subscription is the safe harbor.
- Utility rate/tariff law and back-billing rules (state PUC for regulated systems; board-set tariffs for municipals): municipal systems typically NOT PUC-rate-regulated, but many quasi-municipal/private/investor-owned and some district systems ARE. Correcting rate codes and re-billing for under-billed service is governed by tariff rules and back-billing statutes capping how far back recovery may go (commonly 3–24 months) with notice. Burden: moderate; affects the "recovered dollars" math and the re-billing step.
- Consumer protection on re-billing residents (state UDAP + utility shutoff/billing-dispute rules): re-billing residents for past unbilled water — especially with shutoff leverage — is exactly what state utility consumer-protection rules and UDAP statutes police. Oversight: state AG consumer-protection, PUC consumer bureau. Burden: moderate and politically sensitive (low-income ratepayers, press risk).
- Data privacy / breach notification (all 50 states' statutes; possibly comprehensive privacy laws): utility billing files contain name + address + account number + usage, possibly bank/ACH or card data. Every state has a breach-notification statute; payment-card data → PCI-DSS; ACH/bank numbers are "personal information." As a vendor/processor you sit downstream. Burden: moderate.
- Public records / open-government law (state FOIA/sunshine + retention): data you hold for a public body and your deliverables may be public records subject to disclosure/retention; contract terms cannot override a state public-records statute. Burden: low-moderate.
- Employment / contractor classification (state + FLSA): "trained local techs" doing field verification across states raise 1099-vs-W-2 risk (acute under California's ABC test / AB5), workers' comp, and whether on-site work needs any state water-operator certification. Burden: moderate, multiplies with geography.
- Insurance / bonding for public contracts: public contracts routinely require GL, professional E&O, cyber, sometimes a performance/payment bond and additional-insured status. Burden: low-moderate cost line.

LICENSING SUMMARY
There is most likely NO occupational license the founders must hold to do the data analysis or the described field verification, and that is a genuine strength — this is not contracting, engineering-stamp, or operator-licensed work as scoped. Named caveats: (1) If field verification crosses into operating, repairing, or replacing meters/distribution assets, several states require certified water-distribution-system operators under their drinking-water primacy programs (state implementation of the federal Safe Drinking Water Act, 42 U.S.C. §300f et seq.); stay on the "test/confirm/diagnose" side or partner with the utility's own certified operators for any physical work. (2) Producing a formal "water audit" or recommending capital/engineering changes can edge toward the practice of professional engineering in states with strict PE-practice acts — staying in "data findings + verification + billing/rate-code correction" avoids this; "replace this main" does not. (3) A handful of states regulate "auditing"/"revenue recovery" contractors for government narrowly (more common in tax recovery). (4) Standard business registration, foreign-entity qualification, and vendor registration per buyer are required but routine. Net: no specialized credential collides with the founder profile, provided field scope is held tightly to diagnostics/verification and infrastructure work is left to licensed operators.

LIABILITY POSTURE
The headline exposure is professional-liability/economic-loss, not bodily injury: the entire pitch is "we will document recovered revenue," and if diagnostics are wrong — a meter flagged as under-registering that wasn't, a rate-code "correction" that overcharges residents, an over-stated recovery number the director presents to a board that then sets rates/budgets on it — the loss is financial and the claim is professional negligence / breach / misrepresentation. Insurable with Professional Liability (E&O), but only if claims, deliverables, and disclaimers are scoped carefully; a contingent-fee model sharpens this exposure by aligning pay with aggressive recovery numbers ("you inflated findings to grow your cut"). Second, re-billing residents creates third-party consumer exposure: a wrongly back-billed/shut-off resident can complain to AG/PUC and generate press; this largely belongs to the utility (it owns the billing relationship), but contract indemnity must put it there cleanly. Third, data liability: holding billing/payment data makes the founders a breach target with state-breach and contractual-indemnity exposure; Cyber insurance + a Data Processing Agreement handles the bulk. Fourth, field operations create modest premises/property and workers'-comp exposure. Pre-entity the founders carry all personally; an LLC/S-corp plus insurance (GL, Professional E&O, Cyber, workers' comp) and tightly drafted government contracts with mutual indemnity and a liability cap converts most to a manageable insured posture. The residual insurance does NOT cure: procurement legality (a contract void for failure to follow bidding law can leave the vendor unpaid for work performed — a real, uninsurable structural risk).

BIGGEST SINGLE RISK
The single issue most likely to seriously slow or distort this business is the collision of public-procurement law with the contingent-fee revenue model. The optional "share of first-year documented recovered revenue" is the most powerful sales lever in the one-pager — exactly what makes the decision "risk-free" for a broke district — yet it is the structure most likely to be unlawful for a public body to enter. Numerous states restrict or prohibit contingent-fee public contracts (the doctrine traces to anti-kickback, improper-incentive, and unconstitutional-gift-of-public-funds principles, and several states bar contingent-fee arrangements for governmental revenue/tax recovery by statute), and a municipal charter or state comptroller can void such a contract even where the underlying work was good. Compounding this, a $25K–$75K/year multi-year contract will exceed many local small-purchase thresholds and require competitive bidding or formal RFP — and a contract awarded in violation of bidding law is frequently void ab initio, exposing the vendor to performing work it cannot legally be paid for and giving any taxpayer or losing competitor standing to challenge it. Because both rules vary state-by-state and charter-by-charter, the founders cannot solve this once; they must clear it per jurisdiction. The mitigation is structural and known: lead with the flat annual subscription (clearly lawful as a professional-services purchase), treat the contingent fee as an exception offered only where counsel confirms it is permitted, and build a per-state procurement playbook (or sell through cooperative-purchasing vehicles / state rural-water association master agreements that pre-satisfy bidding requirements). Achievable, but until in place this is the risk that can leave the founders unpaid.
