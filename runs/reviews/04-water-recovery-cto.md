CTO REVIEW — Water Utility Revenue-Recovery Program

ONE-PARAGRAPH TECHNICAL READ
The technical heart of this business is not the dashboard — it is ingesting messy, heterogeneous billing/meter/production exports from dozens of incompatible utility billing systems (Tyler Munis/Incode, Caselle, BS&A, Springbrook, RVS, Edmunds, plus AMI/AMR vendors like Sensus, Badger, Neptune, Itron, Mueller and a long tail of homegrown Access/Excel setups) and running deterministic anomaly checks against them. The anomaly detection itself is genuinely tractable — rule-based and statistical (z-scores, consumption-trend breaks, zero-read flags, rate-code mismatches, mass-balance against production), not research-grade ML — and that is the good news. The bad news, which governs the whole build, is that there is no standard schema across these source systems, so a large fraction of every new customer's onboarding is bespoke data-mapping engineering and human reconciliation; this is a services-heavy data-integration business wearing a SaaS costume, scaling like a consulting practice with reusable parts, not like a product. For a two-founder bootstrap with one prototype-grade coder, that is buildable and defensibly profitable, but time-to-revenue and maintenance burden both live in the integration layer, not the analytics.

ARCHITECTURE SKETCH
- Frontend: a thin Next.js/React dashboard on Vercel — ranked findings table, dollars-recoverable per finding, account drilldown, board-ready PDF export, simple state-reporting view. Low-complexity; the visible 10%.
- Ingestion layer (where the real complexity lives): a per-source-system mapping framework. Inputs are CSV/Excel exports, occasional ODBC pull, fixed-width AMR/AMI meter files, PDF/printed registers. Needs a normalizer with named source adapters (one config per billing-system family), fuzzy column mapping, unit/period reconciliation, and a human-in-the-loop review for every new account onboarding.
- Backend/analytics: Python (pandas/Polars + DuckDB or Postgres) running deterministic anomaly checks — stopped/zero reads, declining-register trend, rate-code-vs-meter-size mismatch, off-roll accounts (in production data, absent from billing), and system-level mass-balance (water produced − water billed = NRW) with hotspot attribution. Background jobs via a simple queue (Celery/RQ or cron) for the per-cycle re-screen.
- Data store: Postgres (managed — Supabase/RDS) per-tenant isolated; raw source files in object storage (S3) with strict tenant prefixes. Modest volumes — a 5,000-account utility billing monthly is ~60k rows/year, trivially small.
- Integrations: mostly file-based, not API-based. A feature for bootstrapping (no live API contracts to break) but an operational liability (every export format drifts when the utility upgrades billing software).
- ML/AI: none required, and that is correct. Any "AI" should be confined to assisting column-mapping suggestions, not making billing determinations. Billing corrections that touch real customer money must be deterministic and auditable.
- Auth/hosting: managed auth (Clerk/Auth0 or Supabase Auth), per-tenant RBAC, standard managed cloud.

BUILD PLAN TO REVENUE-EARNING MVP
Critical reframing: the revenue-earning MVP is NOT a finished product — it is the founders manually running the analysis for the first 3-5 paying utilities using a half-built pipeline plus spreadsheet glue, then handing over a real findings report. Revenue precedes "done" because the deliverable is the diagnostic, not the app — the right bootstrap path, shortening time-to-cash.
1. Core anomaly library against ONE billing-system export format (4-6 dev-weeks): deterministic checks in Python/pandas against the first design-partner's actual export. The wedge; straightforward statistics.
2. Ingestion/normalization framework for the first 2-3 source systems (6-10 dev-weeks): the genuinely hard, repetitive part. Column mapping, unit/period reconciliation, meter-to-account joins, split/combined accounts, rate tiers. Every additional billing-system family beyond the first is another 1-3 weeks of adapter work essentially forever.
3. Findings/board report generator + minimal dashboard (3-5 dev-weeks): ranked findings, dollars-recoverable, PDF export. Punt to manual/spreadsheet for the first one or two.
4. Per-cycle re-screen automation + multi-tenant auth/storage (4-6 dev-weeks): turns the one-time audit into the recurring monitoring subscription that is the whole revenue thesis. Punt-able only briefly.
Realistic total: ~17-27 dev-weeks for one developer, but first paid diagnostic in ~6-8 weeks via chunks 1+3 manually for a single design partner. Given prototype-grade founder engineering (~5-6/10), calendar time is longer and a hire is likely needed before customer #10. CRITICAL ASSUMPTIONS
1. The mass-balance is computable: that the utility actually has usable production/pumping records (master-meter or SCADA totals) to set against billed volume. Many small systems have poor or no production metering — if absent, the headline NRW number can't be computed and the diagnostic is gutted. Verify: ask three target utilities for one year of monthly pumped-volume data before building anything.
2. Source-system heterogeneity is mappable, not infinite: that 70-80% of targets cluster onto a handful of billing systems (Tyler, Caselle, BS&A, Springbrook, Edmunds) so adapters amortize. If the long tail of homegrown Access/Excel/paper dominates, every customer is a one-off. Verify: survey 20 target utilities for what billing software they run.
3. Data is exportable at all: that directors can pull their own billing/meter history without paying their software vendor for a custom export. Verify: ask a design partner to produce a raw export this week.
4. Findings convert to actually-recovered dollars: that flagged anomalies survive field verification and the utility can legally/operationally re-bill or correct. Verify: one full audit-to-recovery cycle before signing performance-linked contracts.
5. Deterministic checks are accurate enough to trust with money: low enough false-positive rate that field verification isn't chasing noise. Verify: run the library where ground truth (known stopped meters) is partially known.

INFORMATION SECURITY POSTURE
Data is utility billing records: customer names, service addresses, account numbers, consumption history, sometimes payment/financial detail — PII at meaningful scale (an entire town's water customers), and address-level consumption has secondary sensitivity (occupancy patterns). It lives in multi-tenant managed cloud (Postgres + S3). Threat model at launch is mundane but real: credential compromise, tenant data bleed (one utility seeing another's data), exfiltration of raw exports in object storage. Required controls at launch, not later: encryption at rest and in transit (default on managed cloud), strict per-tenant isolation in the data model and storage prefixes (the single most important control — a cross-tenant leak is catastrophic for a vendor whose customers are public bodies), SSO/strong auth with RBAC, secrets in a manager, audit logging of who accessed which utility's data. Because customers are public entities, expect public-records, procurement-security questionnaires, and eventually state IT/cyber requirements rather than classic SOC2 — but SOC2 Type II will be asked for as contracts grow into mid-size systems, and the architecture should be SOC2-attainable from day one. A written breach-notification plan is needed early because state data-breach laws apply to residents' PII. Not a heavy security build, but "we'll fix it later" is unacceptable given the customer is government and the data is a whole community's records.

AVERAGE SCORE: 6.5 / 10

TOP 3 TECHNICAL STRENGTHS
- The hard part is data labor, not unsolved research: anomaly detection here is deterministic statistics (mass-balance, zero/stopped-read flags, rate-code mismatch), fully buildable by a small team in Python/pandas.
- Minimal live third-party dependency: file-based ingestion means no critical API, model, or marketplace can re-price or rate-limit the business out of existence — rare and valuable for a bootstrap.
- Revenue precedes a finished product: because the deliverable is a diagnostic report, the founders can earn in ~6-8 weeks by hand-running the pipeline for one design partner, before the SaaS is hardened.

TOP 3 TECHNICAL RISKS
- Source-system heterogeneity turns onboarding into bespoke integration: if target utilities don't cluster onto ~5 billing systems, every customer is a one-off and the business scales like consulting, not software.
- Missing/poor production-metering data at small utilities could make the headline non-revenue-water number uncomputable, gutting the core diagnostic for a meaningful share of the addressable market.
- Export-format drift and per-cycle reconciliation create a permanent two-founder oncall/ops burden that erodes the "low ongoing effort" thesis as customer count grows.

BIGGEST SINGLE RISK
The single risk most likely to break the economics is data-integration heterogeneity at the onboarding boundary. The product's whole promise — recurring, near-passive monitoring that pays for itself — depends on the marginal cost of adding a utility falling sharply after the first few, which only happens if customers cluster onto a small number of billing-system export formats that reusable adapters can absorb. Small and rural water systems are exactly the segment most likely to run idiosyncratic, ancient, or homegrown setups (Access databases, spreadsheets, paper registers, half-migrated AMR files), and each non-standard source costs days-to-weeks of human data-mapping plus ongoing reconciliation as that utility's exports drift over time. If the long tail dominates, the founders don't have a software business with a thin services wrapper — they have a consulting practice where the software is a productivity tool, and the per-customer integration labor caps how many utilities two founders (plus field techs) can hold without a real engineering team. That directly threatens the income-in-24-months math, because revenue stops scaling once the founders' integration bandwidth saturates. The cheap, decisive de-risking move is to survey 20-30 target utilities for (a) what billing software they run and (b) whether they have usable production-meter totals — before writing the second adapter.
