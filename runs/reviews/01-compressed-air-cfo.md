CFO REVIEW — Compressed Air Optimization Service

ONE-PARAGRAPH FINANCIAL READ

The recurring-contract structure and low startup capital are genuinely attractive: this is a real services subscription with a defensible "leaks regenerate" retention thesis, and you can launch for the cost of one or two acoustic imaging cameras (low five figures). The financial problem is not the per-plant economics — those are fine — it is the gap between a labor-bound field-service business and a $2M founder-income target in 24 months. At the stated ARPU ($8k–$30k/plant/year), you need on the order of 150–400 active contracts within two years, each requiring a physical on-site survey on a quarterly/semi-annual cadence, which converts the founders' time directly into a hiring-and-routing operation that is slow to stand up and whose margins compress the moment you staff it with W-2 technicians. The numbers pencil to a healthy lifestyle business at $400k–$900k of founder income; reaching the full $2M in 24 months requires heroic sales velocity and clean technician utilization that I would not assume.

THE MATH

Target: ~$2M combined founder income (pre-tax distributable) within 24 months.

Step 1 — Revenue needed. Founder income is what's left after COGS (technician labor, equipment, travel) and overhead (sales, software, admin). For a labor-heavy field-service business I'd model distributable founder margin at [ASSUMED] 25–40% of revenue once technicians are hired (lower than a pure-software SaaS because every renewal requires a paid site visit). To net $2M to the founders you therefore need roughly:
- At 40% founder margin: $2M / 0.40 = $5.0M revenue.
- At 25% founder margin: $2M / 0.25 = $8.0M revenue.
Call it $5M–$8M ARR by month 24.

Step 2 — Contracts needed. Using a blended ARPU. The range is $8k–$30k; a realistic blended average for SMB plants buying a 1–2x/year cadence is [ASSUMED] $12k–$15k/plant/year (most early customers will be smaller single-compressor shops anchoring near the bottom, not $30k multi-compressor sites).
- At $5M ÷ $13k ≈ 385 contracts.
- At $8M ÷ $13k ≈ 615 contracts.
Even if you bias the book toward larger $20k sites: $5M ÷ $20k = 250 contracts; $8M ÷ $20k = 400 contracts.
So the target requires ~250–600 active recurring contracts in 24 months. That is the crux number.

Step 3 — Can you survey them? A survey + report for a 50–500 HP plant is [ASSUMED] 0.5–2 technician-days each including travel and write-up; quarterly cadence quadruples that, semi-annual doubles it. Take 400 contracts at an average 2 visits/year × 1 tech-day = 800 tech-days/year of fieldwork, plus report writing. At [ASSUMED] ~200 productive field-days/tech/year, that's ~4–5 full-time technicians for fieldwork alone, plus founders or staff on sales, scheduling, and reporting. Standing up, training, and routing 4–5 reliable techs across dispersed regions inside 24 months while also closing 400 contracts is the operational bottleneck the revenue math hides.

Step 4 — Per-plant unit economics (the part that works). One $13k contract at [ASSUMED] gross margin: a survey costs maybe [ASSUMED] $1,500–$3,500 in tech labor + travel + equipment depreciation per visit, ~2 visits/year = $3k–$7k COGS, so gross margin ≈ 45–75% per contract before overhead. CAC via direct founder selling and utility-program/association channels is [ASSUMED] $1,500–$5,000 per logo. LTV at $13k/year × [ASSUMED] 3–5 year life × 55% GM ≈ $21k–$36k contribution → LTV/CAC ≈ 4–24x, payback well under 12 months. The single-customer economics are good; the business-level math is constrained by labor scaling, not by per-deal returns.

Bottom line: lifestyle outcome ($500k–$1M combined founder income on 80–150 contracts) is very plausible in 24 months. The full $2M requires 250–600 contracts and 4–5+ technicians — possible but not conservative.

2.  Working-capital profile:           8  — Service contracts billed annually or quarterly in advance per the one-pager; customer pays before the recurring surveys are delivered, so AR float is short and there is no inventory to carry.
3.  Cash flow self-funding:            7  — Advance-billed contracts fund the next survey cycle, but each new technician is a fixed cost added ahead of the contracts that fill their route, so aggressive scaling pulls cash forward modestly.
4.  Unit economics quality:            8  — At $13k ARPU, ~55% GM, $1.5k–$5k CAC and 3–5yr life, LTV/CAC pencils to ~4–24x with sub-12-month payback (shown in THE MATH Step 4).
5.  Gross margin:                      6  — Per-contract GM ~45–75% on founder-run surveys, but it compresses toward the low end once W-2 technicians + travel carry every recurring visit; this is labor-delivered, not software-delivered, so 80%+ is not reachable.
6.  Path to $2M in 24mo:               4  — Requires ~250–600 active contracts and 4–5 trained technicians inside 24 months (THE MATH Steps 2–3); reaches a strong $500k–$1M lifestyle income comfortably but $2M needs heroic sales velocity and utilization.
7.  Realistic obtainable market:       7  — DOE/Compressed Air Challenge field data put leaks at 20–30% of compressed-air energy, and U.S. has on the order of hundreds of thousands of SMB plants on compressed air; two founders capturing a few hundred is a rounding error, so saturation is not the constraint.
8.  Financial risk concentration:      8  — Revenue spread across hundreds of small plants at $8k–$30k each means no single customer exceeds ~1% of book at scale; channel risk (over-reliance on one utility rebate program) is the real concentration, not customer.
9.  Scaling economics:                 5  — Margins stay roughly flat-to-slightly-improving (route density and software leverage help), but the core delivery is headcount-linear — adding revenue means adding technicians, so there is no SaaS-style margin expansion.
10. Financeability / exit optionality: 7  — Recurring vendor-neutral contracts with documented kWh/$ savings and a renewal book are an acquirable asset for a larger energy-services or facilities firm (e.g., compressed-air dealers, ESCOs), more saleable than pure personal-services but valued as a labor-bound services roll-up, not a software multiple.

AVERAGE SCORE: 6.8 / 10

TOP 3 FINANCIAL STRENGTHS
- Genuinely low capital to first revenue: under $50k buys the imaging equipment and a dashboard, well inside the founders' self-fund capacity, with no inventory or facility.
- Advance-billed recurring contracts with a structural retention thesis (leaks physically regenerate in 12–18 months), giving short AR float and durable repeat revenue rather than one-time project fees.
- Strong, diversified per-plant unit economics — ~55% gross margin, sub-12-month CAC payback, LTV/CAC in the high-single-digits — with no single-customer concentration at scale.

TOP 3 FINANCIAL RISKS
- The $2M-in-24-months target is labor-gated: it implies 250–600 active contracts and 4–5+ field technicians, a hiring/routing build-out that throttles both revenue ramp and margin.
- Margin compression on scale-up: every recurring survey is a paid truck-roll, so blended gross margin drifts toward 45% as W-2 techs and travel replace founder sweat equity.
- ROI claim depends on customers actually fixing leaks AND a metered savings story holding up; if repairs lag or savings can't be verified, renewals and any shared-savings revenue erode, and the "documented savings" sales engine stalls.

BIGGEST SINGLE RISK

The business is fundamentally a labor-bound field-service operation wearing a recurring-revenue coat, and the single thing most likely to break the financial thesis is the collision between the $2M income target and technician-linear delivery. Every dollar of recurring revenue is tethered to a physical, scheduled on-site survey, so revenue cannot outrun the founders' ability to hire, train, and route reliable technicians across regionally dispersed plants — and that hiring happens ahead of the contracts that fill the routes, adding fixed cost before the offsetting revenue arrives. The founder profile explicitly flags sustained execution and high-volume closing as the team's weak spots (~6–7/10) and field operations as "not their happy place," which is exactly the muscle this model demands most heavily at the 250–600-contract scale required for $2M. The likely real outcome is a very good $500k–$1M combined-income lifestyle business reached in 24 months, with the full $2M slipping to year 3–4; that is a fine business, but it misses the stated 24-month target, and any plan that hits $2M on time should be treated with suspicion until the technician-utilization and sales-velocity assumptions are independently verified.

QUESTIONS THE FOUNDERS MUST ANSWER BEFORE I'M COMFORTABLE
- What is the true delivered cost and duration of one survey cycle (tech-hours, travel, report time) for a representative 50–500 HP plant, and what blended gross margin remains after you replace founder labor with paid technicians? This is the single number that decides whether founder margin is 25% or 40%.
- What blended ARPU and contract mix do you actually win in the field — how many of your first 50 contracts land near $8k vs. $20k–$30k — and what is the real sales cycle length and close rate per logo through direct selling vs. utility-program/association channels?
- What technician hiring, training, and utilization plan gets you to the 4–5 reliable field techs implied by 250–600 contracts within 24 months, and what is the realistic monthly contract-add rate your two-person sales motion can sustain?
- What share of customers verifiably fix the flagged leaks and re-survey-renew, and can the metered/verified savings actually be substantiated to support both renewals and any shared-savings billing? (Cheap experiment: run 8–12 paid pilot surveys, track time, cost, close rate, repair-completion, and verified savings before scaling headcount.)
