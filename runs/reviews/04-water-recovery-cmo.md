CMO REVIEW — Water Utility Revenue-Recovery Program

ONE-PARAGRAPH MARKET READ
This is a genuinely smart wedge into a sleepy, unglamorous, fragmented market, and the positioning writes itself in a way most B2B concepts never achieve: "we find money you're already losing and you pay us out of what we find." That self-funding pitch dissolves the single hardest objection in public-sector sales — budget — and there is no funded incumbent owning this niche for small systems. The real marketing problem is not differentiation or air; it is the buying motion. Public water utilities are slow, procurement-bound, board-governed buyers, and the math to ~$2M of founder income inside 24 months requires roughly 40-80 contracts in a market that signs at the speed of a town council meeting, sold by a two-person team whose own profile flags closing and sustained sales grind as the weak spot. The concept can absolutely be sold and positioned; whether it can be distributed fast enough at the price implied is the open question.

POSITIONING DRAFT
For the small-utility director who knows 20% of their water revenue is leaking but can't see where: "We find the water revenue you're already losing, document every dollar for your board, and keep it found — and the program pays for itself out of what we recover." Sharp, customer-true, one-sentence claim with real edges: a quantified pain (15-30% NRW), a concrete outcome (documented recovered dollars), a board-facing benefit (the director's actual political need), and a risk-reversal (self-funding). One of the cleaner positioning drafts I've seen — the rare case where I'd run the founder's own line almost verbatim.

THE WEDGE
The first 100 customers come through the National Rural Water Association (NRWA) and its 49 affiliated state rural-water associations, plus AWWA state sections — a small, listed, reachable, gossip-prone universe. Concrete tactics: (1) state rural-water-association annual conference booths ~$1,500-$5,000 each [ASSUMED; verify via prospectuses like Texas Rural Water Association]; an 8-10 state circuit ~$25k-$50k all-in. (2) NRWA "circuit rider" technical-assistance programs and member newsletters — co-marketed webinars/case studies, low-cost high-trust. (3) The killer tactic: 3-5 deeply documented pilot audits at/near cost, publish the recovered-dollar figure with the director's name ("Recovered $214k for the City of ___"), and let the association circuit and director-to-director word-of-mouth carry it. Three credible case studies beat any ad spend. The wedge is real, specific, founder-fit. The constraint is sales-cycle length per buyer.

COMPETITIVE PICTURE
- M.E. Simpson Co. — established water-loss audit and meter-testing player; best at one-time engineering field audits. Hands over a study; not a sticky recurring subscription — the air this concept exploits.
- Cavanaugh & Associates / AWWA M36 water-audit consultants — best at the formal M36 methodology often required for state reporting. Project-based, expensive, binder-and-leave; same gap.
- AMI/smart-meter vendors — Badger Meter (BEACON), Sensus (Xylem), Itron — hardware-led continuous metering with analytics on top. Their answer is "buy our meters," a capital project the small utility can't afford. This concept is the capital-light diagnostic on top of existing meters.
- Utility billing software (Tyler Technologies/Munis, CentralSquare) — system of record; holds the data but doesn't proactively hunt leakage as a service.
- True incumbent: doing nothing / the part-time circuit rider (free state-funded visit).
Where is the air? Nobody owns "recurring, capital-light, self-funding, data-plus-field revenue recovery for sub-10,000-person systems." Simpson and M36 firms do projects; meter vendors do hardware; software vendors do records. The recurring-monitoring + performance-linked + board-facing-dashboard combination is genuinely open — defensible air for a small team, for now.

AVERAGE SCORE: 7.0 / 10

TOP 3 MARKET STRENGTHS
- A self-funding, risk-reversed pitch that disarms the single biggest public-sector objection — budget — better than almost any B2B concept I review.
- A listed, finite, referral-dense buyer universe reachable through NRWA's 49 state associations and AWWA sections, with cheap conference and case-study tactics that fit a two-person team.
- Clean competitive air: M.E. Simpson, the AWWA M36 firms, and meter vendors like Badger Meter and Sensus all leave the recurring, capital-light, monitoring-subscription lane wide open.

TOP 3 MARKET RISKS
- The sales cycle: board-governed public utilities move at the speed of council agendas; a 6-12 month cycle per logo collides hard with a 24-month, ~40-80-contract income target.
- Thin early defensibility: the analytical anomaly-detection core is copyable within a year by M.E. Simpson or a meter vendor that decides recurring services are worth owning.
- Customer-truth gap: the concept may be over-indexed on the published NRW statistic and under-indexed on whether a cash-strapped director will sign a $25k-$75k annual contract versus taking the free state circuit-rider visit.

BIGGEST SINGLE RISK
The marketing risk most likely to kill this is sales-cycle velocity, not demand or positioning. The positioning is excellent and the air is real, but the customer is a public/quasi-public body that buys through boards, councils, and procurement — even a $25k decision can require a meeting, a vote, sometimes a competitive solicitation, and a budget cycle. A founder who is "chronically over-optimistic on growth speed" will model a 60-90 day close and discover a 6-12 month reality. At a blended ~$40k-$50k annual contract, reaching ~$2M of combined founder income in 24 months implies ~50-100 active contracts net of delivery cost — every one a slow, relationship-led, field-pilot-gated, board-approved sale executed by two people whose profile flags closing as the 6-7/10 soft spot. The wedge gets you in the room; the room just moves slowly. If the cycle runs long, the self-funding pitch still wins deals — it just wins them too slowly to clear the income bar on the stated timeline, a go-to-market pacing problem, not a product problem.

QUESTIONS THE FOUNDERS MUST ANSWER BEFORE I'M COMFORTABLE
- What is the real elapsed time from first conference contact to signed contract to first paid invoice, and how much is procurement/board approval you cannot compress? (Verify with 5-10 district managers and a state-association director.)
- Will a cash-strapped director actually pay $25k-$75k/year when free state-funded circuit-rider assistance and one-time grant-funded M36 audits exist? What does your recurring program give the board that the free help does not?
- Does the performance-linked "share of recovered revenue" survive public-sector procurement and political optics — can a public utility legally and palatably pay a contingency fee on ratepayer revenue, and does it require a competitive bid that resets your wedge?
- How many of the ~50,000 systems are actually addressable given the smallest can't support a $25k fee, and what is the true count in your viable band?
- Have you personally sat with at least five target directors and heard the pain in their own words, or is it inferred from the published statistic?
