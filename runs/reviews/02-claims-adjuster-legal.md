LEGAL / REGULATORY REVIEW — Independent Claims Adjuster Workspace

(Not formal legal advice. Experienced operator with regulatory pattern-recognition, flagging issues for retained counsel.)

ONE-PARAGRAPH RISK READ
This is a B2B SaaS documentation/workflow tool sold to licensed independent adjusters and small IA firms; the product explicitly does not adjust claims, set values, or act as an insurer, which keeps it out of the heavily-regulated core of the insurance business. The dominant exposures are ordinary SaaS exposures: a B2B subscription contract motion (clickwrap/MSA), data handling that includes third-party claimant personal information and photos of damaged property and named individuals (pulling in CCPA/CPRA service-provider obligations and likely state insurance-data-security regimes such as the NAIC Insurance Data Security Model Law and the GLBA Safeguards Rule), and a "court-ready" marketing claim that creates a meaningful product-liability/professional-reliance tail if a file the tool assembled is later challenged. None fatal, most contractual/structural; the biggest single landmine is data-security/breach exposure tied to insurance claim data plus the "court-ready" promise — both manageable if addressed before launch.

REGULATORY MAP
- State Departments of Insurance / NAIC — adjuster licensing is regulated, but the LICENSEE is the adjuster (the customer), NOT the software vendor. Vendor burden: effectively none for selling software, PROVIDED the product does not render claim decisions/valuations/"adjusting." Monitor: don't let auto-generated narratives or "missing-item" logic drift into setting scope/value (could be unlicensed adjusting in some states).
- NAIC Insurance Data Security Model Law (adopted in ~24+ states incl. NY 23 NYCRR 500, SC, OH, MI) — info-security-program and breach-notification duties on "licensees." Third-party service providers pulled in CONTRACTUALLY. Burden: moderate; vendor asked to sign data-security addenda and attest controls. Before scale.
- GLBA Safeguards Rule (16 CFR Part 314, FTC) — claim files contain nonpublic personal information; adjusters and their service providers handle GLBA-covered data. Vendor expected to maintain a written security program. Burden: moderate, overlaps NAIC. Before scale.
- California CCPA/CPRA (Cal. Civ. Code 1798.100 et seq., CPPA/CA AG) — vendor is a "service provider" processing CA claimants' personal information. Requires CPRA-mandated processing terms + reasonable security. Burden: low-to-moderate; standard DPA. Before launch.
- Other state privacy laws (Virginia VCDPA, Colorado CPA, Texas TDPSA) — same processor posture; largely one well-drafted DPA. Low incremental.
- FTC ROSCA / state auto-renewal laws (Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code 17600 et seq.; FTC click-to-cancel) — applies to the subscription; buyer is a business/sole proprietor, but disclosure + easy-cancel are cheap best practice. Low burden.
- FTC Act Section 5 + state UDAP — "court-ready" and "no-bounce-back" are advertising representations; if a "guaranteed" file is rejected/fails in litigation, the claim is the exposure surface. Manageable by softening + disclaiming. Before launch.
- CAN-SPAM / TCPA (47 U.S.C. 227) — content/community + outbound marketing; standard. Low burden.
- Federal CAT/NFIP — not directly applicable; touches adjusters' WORK, not the vendor's tool. Monitor.

LICENSING SUMMARY
The software vendor needs NO insurance license, adjuster license, or industry credential to sell this product in any state, because it is a documentation/workflow tool that does not adjust, value, or settle claims, does not act as insurer/producer, and does not advise insureds. This crucial structural fact must be protected: the moment the product is marketed as, or functions as, something that determines scope of loss, assigns damage values, or makes adjusting judgments, a state DOI could argue it enables unlicensed adjusting — but as scoped (capture, organization, assembly, checklist, status pipeline), that argument is weak. The only licenses the business needs are ordinary: entity registration, local business license, and sales-tax registration where SaaS is taxable. The per-state adjuster licensing/CE burden falls entirely on customers — and is actually a GTM asset (CE/licensing channels are a named acquisition path). Founders' lack of specialized legal credentials is not a problem; this needs competent counsel to paper data terms, not deep regulatory expertise to operate.

LIABILITY POSTURE
The entity (LLC/S-corp formed BEFORE first revenue — non-negotiable) carries three vectors. First, data-breach liability: the product stores claim photos, scope notes, and claimant PII in the cloud; a breach triggers 50-state notification, GLBA/NAIC scrutiny via the customer chain, and contractual indemnity demands from IA firms. Insurable with cyber/tech-E&O at modest premiums; contractually capped (limitation of liability, indemnity carve-outs). Second, professional-reliance/product liability: if an adjuster relies on the "missing-item" flag or "court-ready" assembly and a file is deficient, the adjuster may turn on the vendor — the tail the "court-ready" positioning creates; handled by Tech E&O plus a strong disclaimer that the adjuster remains solely responsible for completeness/accuracy and licensing compliance. Third, IP/infringement: low, assuming original code and no scraping of carrier portals. Pre-entity, founders carry all of this personally — so entity formation + baseline insurance are the first two checks. Contracts (clickwrap ToS for solos, short MSA/DPA for firms) absorb most of the rest.

BIGGEST SINGLE RISK
The single issue most likely to seriously slow or wound this business is data-security and breach exposure compounded by the "court-ready" promise. The product, by design, becomes the central repository for complete insurance claim files — claimant names, addresses, property details, photographs of homes and sometimes people, and the documentary record that later supports payment of a claim. That data is nonpublic personal information under the GLBA Safeguards Rule and personal information under CCPA/CPRA and every state breach-notification statute, and the customers who hold it are "licensees" subject to the NAIC Insurance Data Security Model Law (and, for any NY-touching adjuster, 23 NYCRR 500). Those licensees are required by statute and contract to push security obligations down to their service providers, meaning the vendor will increasingly be asked to maintain and attest to a written information-security program, sign data-security addenda, and indemnify for breaches. A single breach is therefore not just a cyber-insurance event; it cascades into 50-state notification, customer indemnity demands, and reputational collapse in a tight word-of-mouth community. Layer the "court-ready" claim: if the breached or deficient file was one the product told an adjuster was complete and court-ready, the vendor's own advertising becomes the plaintiff's exhibit, converting a security incident into a deceptive-practices and professional-reliance claim. None of this is fatal — it is the standard B2B-SaaS-handling-sensitive-data problem — but it must be engineered in from day one (security program, cyber + Tech E&O, DPA, disciplined marketing copy), not bolted on after the first firm-level customer or the first incident.
