COO REVIEW — Compressed Air Optimization Service

ONE-PARAGRAPH OPERATIONS READ

This is operationally one of the cleaner field-service concepts I have evaluated: a learnable, equipment-light survey delivered on-site, sold as a recurring contract, with a clear and standardizable unit of work. Two founders can absolutely run the first 15-30 contracts themselves and build a documented-savings reference base. The real operational question is not "can two people deliver a survey" — it plainly can be done — but "can the recurring-route model scale to the income target without becoming a labor business spread thin across a low-density regional geography," and "what happens with the repair coordination promise, which is where the messy, uncontrolled, multi-trade execution risk lives." The delivery itself is tractable; the throughput-to-target and the repair-verification scope are where I push hard.

THE TUESDAY-IN-MARCH WALKTHROUGH

It is Tuesday in March, roughly 12 months in. Assume the founders have ~20 contracted plants across a 150-mile radius of a metro base (say greater Philadelphia / NJ / eastern PA), a mix of $8k-$30k annual contracts, most on a quarterly or semi-annual cadence. Mike is on the road. He left home at 6:40am to beat traffic to an injection molder in Lehigh Valley for an 8:00am start. He badges in, does the safety briefing, and spends the morning walking the plant floor with an acoustic imaging camera (e.g., a Fluke ii900 / Sonotec / SONOCASE-class device), aiming it at fittings, quick-disconnects, drains, hose reels, FRL units, and overhead distribution piping. Each leak gets a numbered tag, a photo/thermal-acoustic capture, a location note, an estimated CFM band, and a dollar-per-year estimate based on the plant's electricity rate and compressor specifics. A 50-200 HP plant is roughly a 3-5 hour survey for one technician depending on size, access height (overhead piping needs a lift or a long-reach shot), and how many production lines are running. He finishes the floor walk around 12:30pm, eats in the truck, and drives 50 minutes to a second, smaller plant (a packaging shop, ~75 HP) for a 1:45pm survey that takes ~2.5 hours. He wraps the second site at 4:30pm and drives 75 minutes home, doing nothing billable in the truck.

Meanwhile Jon is at the home office. He is NOT surveying. He is (a) turning yesterday's two raw survey captures into written reports — pulling the tagged leaks into a template, computing total CFM lost and annual dollars, ranking the fix list, and generating the customer dashboard view; (b) chasing a utility rebate form for a customer in PA whose program requires pre/post documentation in a specific format; (c) fielding a call from a month-3 customer's maintenance lead who fixed 12 of the 18 tagged leaks and wants to know if the "verification re-survey" is included now or at the quarterly mark; and (d) doing outbound to fill Q3 — emailing a regional manufacturers' association and two electric-utility trade-ally program contacts. The thing that "breaks" on this Tuesday is the seam between surveying and reporting: each on-site survey generates 1.5-3 hours of back-office report production, and at 20 customers with quarterly cadence that report queue is already a near-full-time job for one founder, which is fine at 20 customers and becomes the bottleneck well before the income target. The second thing that quietly breaks is the repair-coordination promise — the molder asks Mike to "just get the overhead pipe section fixed," which is a contractor-scheduling, scope, and liability problem the founders did not actually want to own.

CAPACITY MATH (show the work)

Target: $2M+ distributable founder income annually within 24 months. For a labor-delivered field-service business with travel, the gap between revenue and founder income is wide. Let me work revenue first, then test the labor it implies.

Contract value: midpoint ~$15k/plant/year [ASSUMED midpoint of the stated $8k-$30k band; verify with 5 priced pilot quotes]. To hit even $3M revenue (a plausible floor to net ~$2M founder income after technician labor, vehicles, equipment, software, and the founders' own cost), that is:
- $3,000,000 / $15,000 = 200 active plant contracts.

Now the delivery labor for 200 contracts. Assume average cadence of 2 surveys/plant/year [ASSUMED blend of quarterly and semi-annual; verify in contracts]:
- 200 plants × 2 surveys = 400 surveys/year.
- Plus initial baseline survey for each new plant; in steady state treat baseline as one of the cadence visits, so ~400-450 survey-events/year.

A realistic technician day with regional travel: 1.5 surveys/day average (one large morning survey + one smaller afternoon survey is the good day from the walkthrough; many days are a single distant plant). At ~220 working days/year:
- 1.5 surveys/day × 220 days = 330 surveys/year per technician.
- 450 surveys / 330 = ~1.4 full-time field technicians just for surveying.

So delivery field labor is light — call it 2 technicians. That is genuinely good news on the labor-intensity axis. BUT the constraint that bites is report production and customer-ops, not floor labor:
- 450 surveys × ~2 hours report/dashboard/rebate work each = 900 hours/year of back-office production = ~0.5 FTE if templated well, more if rebate paperwork is bespoke per utility.
- Customer-ops (renewals, re-survey scheduling, savings reporting, the dashboard, churn-save calls) across 200 accounts at low-touch ~3 hours/account/year = 600 hours = ~0.3 FTE.

Headcount to run 200 contracts: ~2 field techs + founders covering sales, partnerships, reporting, and ops, plus likely 1 ops/admin coordinator. So roughly a 5-person company. That is operationally very achievable. The hard part is NOT staffing — it is reaching 200 signed recurring contracts in 24 months in a regionally dispersed market with a several-week, manager-budget sales cycle, which is a go-to-market throughput problem more than a delivery throughput problem. At 200 plants the geography also fractures: 200 plants will not sit inside one 150-mile radius at a price point that supports profitable routing, so the model implies multiple regional pods, each needing its own technician and route density — which is a scaling path, but it pushes the income target out past 24 months unless contract values run toward the high end.

Sensitivity: if average contract is $25k (high end) and the founders keep delivery lean, $3M revenue needs only 120 plants, which is far more tractable on density and on a 24-month timeline. The whole capacity case lives or dies on landing the higher contract values, which depends on selling larger multi-compressor plants and on the shared-savings upside actually being capturable.

SCORES (1–10)

1. Delivery clarity & repeatability: 8 — The unit of work is well-defined and standardizable: a 3-5 hour acoustic survey producing a tagged leak list with CFM/$ estimates, then a templated report; this is a learnable, documentable, route-able procedure, not bespoke craft (walkthrough shows 1.5 surveys/tech/day with a fixed capture-tag-report sequence).
2. Supply chain resilience: 8 — Inputs are minimal and multi-sourced: acoustic imaging cameras are available from several named vendors (Fluke ii900, Sonotec SONAPHONE, FLIR Si-series, CS Instruments), one-time ~$8k-$20k/unit capex per device with no MOQ and no recurring single-source dependency; the "supply chain" is essentially a camera, a laptop, and a truck.
3. Logistics tractability: 5 — On-site work in regionally dispersed industrial parks means real drive time and route density risk; the walkthrough's 50-75 minute inter-site drives are typical, and at 200 plants the geography fragments into multiple pods, which is workable but is the opposite of remote-friendly.
4. Customer-ops scalability: 6 — Low-to-moderate touch per account once a dashboard exists, but renewals, re-survey scheduling, per-utility rebate paperwork, and savings-verification disputes add unbudgeted hours; not self-serve, and the rebate documentation varies by utility, which resists full standardization.
5. Vendor/partner dependency risk: 8 — No bet-the-business platform dependency; the only meaningful partners are electric utilities' efficiency-rebate programs and manufacturers' associations, both helpful-but-optional channels, and the equipment vendors are interchangeable — losing any single one does not stop the business.
6. Hiring feasibility: 7 — The first hire is a survey technician who can be trained on a learnable, non-credentialed skill in weeks; the labor pool (mechanically-inclined field-service techs comfortable on plant floors) is broadly available regionally, though willingness to travel a route and reliability are the real screens [ASSUMED 4-8 week ramp; verify by training one non-founder tech and measuring survey quality variance].
7. Throughput capacity at target: 5 — Delivery labor to target is light (~2 techs for 200 contracts per the math), but reaching 200 recurring contracts in 24 months through a manager-budget, several-week sales cycle across a dispersed market is the genuine throughput wall; the income target is plausible only if contract values run to the high end (~120 plants at $25k) rather than the midpoint.
8. Quality control simplicity: 6 — Survey consistency is trainable, but the deliverable's credibility rests on CFM-loss and dollar-savings estimates that are inherently approximate; a customer who fixes leaks and does not see the promised metered savings is a callback/credibility failure, and the shared-savings option amplifies this measurement-dispute risk.
9. Geographic / seasonality risk: 7 — Demand is year-round and weather-neutral (indoor plant work; compressed air runs every shift regardless of season), with no holiday peak; the only geographic risk is needing enough industrial density per pod, not seasonality.
10. Tooling maturity available: 6 — CRM, scheduling, and billing are off-the-shelf (HubSpot, Jobber/ServiceTitan-class, QuickBooks), but the customer-facing leak-tracking dashboard and the CFM-to-dollars-to-rebate reporting layer must be built; it is prototype-grade buildable by the founders, not an off-the-shelf product, so some real software work is required before it scales cleanly.

AVERAGE SCORE: 6.6 / 10

TOP 3 OPERATIONAL STRENGTHS
- Light, multi-sourced, low-capex supply chain: the entire delivery apparatus is a ~$10k-$20k acoustic camera, a laptop, and a truck, with interchangeable vendors (Fluke, Sonotec, FLIR) and no MOQ or single-source lock-in.
- A genuinely standardizable, trainable unit of work: a 3-5 hour survey following a fixed capture-tag-estimate-report sequence, which lets the founders run early sites personally and then hand routine surveys to ~4-8-week-trained technicians.
- Year-round, weather-neutral, recession-resilient demand: compressed air runs every shift in every season, and the ROI/utility-rebate framing gives the buyer a budget justification independent of economic cycle.

TOP 3 OPERATIONAL RISKS
- Go-to-market throughput, not delivery throughput, is the wall: reaching ~120-200 recurring contracts in 24 months through a several-week, plant-manager-budget sales cycle across a dispersed geography is the real bottleneck, and it depends on a hard-closing sales motion the founder profile flags as "not their happy place."
- The repair-coordination / verification promise drags the business into uncontrolled multi-trade execution and savings-measurement disputes the founders did not want to own — this is where callbacks, liability, and credibility failures concentrate.
- Route density and geographic fragmentation: 200 plants will not sit in one radius at this price point, forcing multiple regional pods each needing its own technician and density before it is profitable, which pushes the timeline.

BIGGEST SINGLE RISK

The single operational issue most likely to kill this is not whether two people can deliver a survey — they clearly can — it is whether the business can manufacture recurring-contract volume fast enough to hit the income target before the model's geography and sales friction stall it. The delivery math is reassuringly light (about two technicians can survey 200 plants a year), which paradoxically means the binding constraint moves entirely to the top of the funnel: every dollar of recurring revenue requires a freshly closed plant-manager contract, and those buyers sit in unglamorous, regionally scattered facilities, carry maintenance budgets but a wary "prove it" posture, and move on a multi-week cycle. To reach ~$3M revenue at midpoint pricing the founders must sign ~200 such contracts, which both fragments their service geography into multiple pods (killing route economics) and demands a sustained, high-volume, ROI-justification sales grind — precisely the long, hard-closing motion that is not where this team's energy lives. If average contract value lands at the midpoint rather than the high end, I am not confident two founders close enough plants in 24 months without it becoming a full-time outbound-sales machine that crowds out everything else. The business is operationally easy to run and operationally hard to fill.

QUESTIONS THE FOUNDERS MUST ANSWER BEFORE I'M COMFORTABLE
- What is the actual average closed contract value and sales cycle length from your first 5-10 priced pilots? The entire 24-month feasibility flips on whether you are signing $15k or $25k contracts — at midpoint you need ~200 plants (geography fragments, timeline slips); at the high end you need ~120 (tractable). Verify cheaply by quoting 5 real plants now.
- Are you in the repair business or not? Is "coordination/verification of repairs" a thin referral-and-recheck layer, or do you own scheduling third-party trades and standing behind the fix? Define the exact scope boundary, because the open-ended version imports multi-trade liability and callback risk you cannot staff with a 5-person company.
- How will you defend the savings number when a customer fixes leaks and their utility bill does not visibly drop (compressor staging, production-volume changes, and weather all confound metered savings)? If you offer shared-savings, what is the agreed measurement protocol, and who arbitrates a dispute?
- What is the realistic route density per region — how many plants within a 90-minute radius support one technician's profitable weekly route — and how many such pods must exist to reach 120-200 contracts?
- Who trains and quality-checks technicians, and what is the measured survey-to-survey variance once a non-founder runs it?
