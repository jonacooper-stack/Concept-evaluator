# Clean-Room One-Pager — Water Utility Revenue-Recovery Program

## The customer
Small and mid-size public water utilities and rural water districts — the municipal and
quasi-municipal bodies that bill residents and businesses for water service. The U.S. has
roughly 50,000 community water systems; the vast majority are small (serving under ~10,000
people), run lean, and have no in-house data analyst. The buyer is the utility director,
district manager, public-works director, or town finance officer — someone accountable for
the system's budget and rate base, who answers to a board or council and is chronically short
of both staff and money.

## The problem
Small water utilities lose a large, recurring slice of revenue they are entitled to collect.
"Non-revenue water" — water produced but never billed — runs commonly in the 15–30% range for
small systems, driven by under-registering and stopped meters, meter-reading and data-entry
errors, accounts that fall off the billing roll, misconfigured rate codes, and leaks. Every
one of these is money the utility already spent to treat and pump but never recovered, and it
recurs every billing cycle until someone finds and fixes it. Small utilities rarely have the
staff, tools, or analytical skill to systematically find it; they know the number is bad but
not where it hides. Replacing every meter is capital they don't have; what they lack is the
diagnostic and the ongoing discipline.

## The product / service
A recurring revenue-recovery and meter-data-integrity program:
- A diagnostic audit that ingests the utility's billing data, meter-read history, and
  production/pumping records and surfaces the specific revenue leaks — stopped/under-registering
  meters, misread accounts, billing exceptions, unbilled or mis-rated accounts, and apparent-
  loss hotspots — ranked by dollars recoverable.
- Field verification on the highest-value findings (testing suspect meters, confirming
  problem accounts) — the hands-on component the founders and trained local techs perform.
- A prioritized recovery plan and help executing it (corrected billing, meter-change
  priorities, rate-code fixes), with the recovered dollars documented for the board.
- An ongoing monitoring subscription: each billing cycle the data is re-screened so new
  anomalies are caught early and the recovered baseline is held, with a simple dashboard the
  utility shows its board and uses for state reporting.

## The revenue model
An annual recurring contract per utility, roughly $25,000–$75,000 per year depending on system
size, covering the ongoing monitoring, periodic re-audit, and field verification. The pitch is
self-funding: the program is sized to recover several times its fee in money the utility was
already losing, so it pays for itself out of found revenue rather than out of a strained
budget. A performance-linked component (a share of first-year documented recovered revenue) can
be offered to make the buying decision risk-free for a cash-strapped district. Contracts are
multi-year and sticky because the monitoring must continue for the recovery to hold.

## Positioning
"We find the water revenue you're already losing — and keep it found." Positioned against the
status quo of doing nothing (the leak just continues), against expensive one-time engineering
"water-loss audits" that hand over a binder and leave, and against full meter-replacement
capital projects the small utility cannot afford. The wedge is the recurring, data-plus-field,
self-funding program that converts a chronic invisible loss into documented recovered revenue
the director can show the board every year.

## How it's delivered and grown
The analytical core is standard data work (ingesting billing/meter/production data, running a
library of anomaly checks, producing ranked findings and a dashboard) that the founders can
build and run; the field-verification component is a learnable, non-licensed hands-on task.
Early customers are reached through state rural-water associations and AWWA sections, state
revolving-fund and primacy-agency relationships, public-works and municipal-finance
conferences, and direct outreach to district managers — a small, listed, reachable universe of
buyers who talk to each other. The founders run utility sales/partnerships, the data/dashboard
layer, and a regional field-verification routine staffed by trained techs as contracts grow.
