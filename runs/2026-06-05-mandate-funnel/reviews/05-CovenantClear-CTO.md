# CTO REVIEW — CovenantClear (Managed Loan-Covenant Compliance for Lower-Middle-Market Borrowers)

ONE-PARAGRAPH TECHNICAL READ
At its technical core a structured-data CRUD app plus a calculation engine plus a scheduler — none hard — wrapped around two genuinely tricky pieces: (1) LLM-assisted extraction of covenant definitions from idiosyncratic, lawyer-drafted credit agreements, and (2) reliable ingestion of financial data out of QuickBooks/NetSuite/Sage. The good news for a two-founder bootstrap is that the build is deliberately human-in-the-loop: a finance specialist reviews every certificate before it ships, so the software does not have to be autonomously correct — it has to be a good assistant. That single design choice (humans on the critical path) turns what could be a bet-the-company AI accuracy problem into a tractable, shippable v1 in roughly 8-12 dev-weeks. The real technical risk is not feasibility; it is that the "calculation engine" is deceptively bespoke per loan (every credit agreement defines EBITDA, fixed-charge coverage, and the borrowing base differently), pushing this toward configurable-per-customer modeling rather than a clean uniform schema — a maintenance and scaling tax, not a build-killer.

ARCHITECTURE SKETCH
- Frontend: a thin Next.js/React app on Vercel — borrower dashboard (covenant register, cushion-to-breach gauges, upcoming deadlines), internal specialist workbench (review/approve queue, certificate generator). No mobile needed; desktop CFO/controller software.
- Backend: a single Postgres-backed service (Node/TypeScript or Python/FastAPI) on a managed host. The data model is the product: Borrower → Facility → Covenant (definition, threshold, test frequency, calc formula) → ReportingObligation (deadline, deliverable type) → CalcRun → Certificate.
- Calculation engine: a per-covenant formula evaluator. The honest version is a configurable expression layer (named financial inputs mapped to a formula like FCCR = (EBITDA − unfinanced capex − cash taxes − distributions) / (interest + scheduled principal)), where the formula and component definitions are set per facility during onboarding. The hidden complexity.
- Integrations: accounting-system ingestion — QuickBooks Online API (OAuth, well-documented), NetSuite (SuiteTalk/REST, heavier), Sage (fragmented). Realistically v1 should lean on CSV/trial-balance export upload, not live integrations, to avoid drowning in connector maintenance.
- LLM/AI: one constrained job — abstract the credit agreement into the structured covenant register. Frontier model with a strict JSON schema, retrieval over the document, mandatory human verification. AI never touches the final certificate math unsupervised.
- Async/scheduling: a job queue (cron + worker, or Temporal/Inngest) drives the quarterly close-to-certificate cycle, deadline reminders, breach-projection alerts. Low volume, low complexity.
- Auth/hosting: managed auth (Clerk/Auth0/WorkOS), role separation (borrower vs internal specialist), encrypted Postgres, secrets in a vault. The real complexity concentrates in the covenant data model and the per-loan calc configuration — everything else is boring proven plumbing.

BUILD PLAN TO REVENUE-EARNING MVP
~8-12 dev-weeks of one developer to a version that can earn revenue, because the human-in-the-loop design lets the first paying customers be onboarded semi-manually while software backfills.
1. Covenant data model + specialist workbench (3-4 weeks): the schema, a UI to enter covenants/definitions by hand, deadline tracker, calc-run record. This alone, plus a human doing the math in a spreadsheet, is a sellable managed service. Ship first.
2. Calculation engine (2-3 weeks): configurable per-covenant formula evaluator with common covenants (leverage, FCCR, min EBITDA, TNW) and the borrowing-base template. Cushion-to-breach display and a simple projection.
3. Certificate generation (1-2 weeks): lender-ready PDF/Excel output matching the agreement's exhibit format, with an approval gate before send.
4. Financial-data ingestion (1-2 weeks for CSV/trial-balance upload; QBO live integration a v2 punt): map the borrower's GL/trial balance to named calc inputs.
5. LLM-assisted agreement abstraction (1-2 weeks): schema-constrained extraction with a human-verification screen. An accelerant for onboarding, not a v1 dependency.
Punt to v2: live accounting integrations beyond QBO, multi-lender portals, automated breach-remediation modeling, any self-serve. The path to first dollar is short because the service can run on spreadsheets-plus-database while the engine matures.

CRITICAL ASSUMPTIONS
1. "Covenant calculations are configurable, not uniform." [ASSUMED — true, with effort.] Every credit agreement defines EBITDA add-backs, FCCR components, and borrowing-base eligibility differently. If the team assumed one calc fits all, the engine will be wrong constantly. Verify: collect 5-10 real lower-middle-market credit agreements and diff their covenant definitions before building the schema.
2. "An LLM can reliably extract covenant terms with human verification." [ASSUMED 70-90% pre-review.] Plausible (semi-structured legal docs with standard sections) but varies by drafting quality. Verify: run 10 real agreements through a frontier model against a strict schema and measure human-correction rate.
3. "Borrowers can/will hand over GL data on a quarterly cycle." [ASSUMED.] If borrowers are slow/messy (likely, given they lack finance staff), the bottleneck is data collection, not software. Verify: pilot with 3 borrowers and time data wrangling.
4. "A human specialist reviewing every certificate is economically compatible with the price." A service-margin assumption that gates the architecture: the workbench must make a specialist fast (minutes, not hours). Verify by timing a real review on the prototype.
5. "Live accounting integrations are not required for v1." [ASSUMED true.] If buyers demand live sync, the connector-maintenance burden arrives early. Check: ask 10 prospects whether quarterly export upload is acceptable.

INFORMATION SECURITY POSTURE
Holds extremely sensitive data: full financial statements, GL/trial balances, complete credit agreements for private companies — material non-public financial information. Threat model: data theft (a competitor/fraudster/market actor would value this) and integrity (a wrong certificate delivered to a lender is reputational and liability event). Launch controls (not later): encryption at rest (Postgres-level + field-level for the most sensitive financials) and in transit (TLS everywhere); managed auth with strict role separation between borrowers (who see only their own data — multi-tenant isolation is non-negotiable and the single most important control) and internal specialists; secrets in a managed vault; full audit logging of who viewed/edited/sent every certificate (also the team's own liability evidence); least-privilege access for reviewers. PII is modest (company financial data, not consumer PII), so the regime is contractual confidentiality and financial-data sensitivity rather than HIPAA/PCI. SOC 2 Type II will be asked for — by sophisticated CFOs and possibly indirectly by lenders — and is very attainable given the founders' stated SOC 2 program experience; budget for it year one as a sales unlock. Incident readiness: a breach disclosure plan and a "wrong certificate" remediation runbook before the first customer, because the failure modes are reputationally fatal. The architecture is small enough that a clean, audit-ready security posture is achievable from day one.

AVERAGE SCORE: 7.3 / 10

TOP 3 TECHNICAL STRENGTHS
- Human-in-the-loop by design removes the AI-accuracy bet from the critical path — the LLM is an onboarding accelerant, never an unsupervised decision-maker, so the riskiest tech is also the most optional.
- Genuinely short path to revenue: a specialist-plus-Postgres service is sellable inside ~8-12 dev-weeks, and the hard parts (calc engine, integrations) can mature behind paying customers.
- Small, low-volume, owned-data architecture (Postgres + job queue + managed auth) that scales 100x without a rewrite and is cheap to run for two founders.

TOP 3 TECHNICAL RISKS
- The "calculation engine" is bespoke per credit agreement; covenant definitions vary enough that the calc layer trends toward per-customer configuration, creating a persistent modeling/QA tax rather than a uniform product.
- Accounting-data ingestion is the operational bottleneck: borrowers without finance staff deliver messy, late GL data, and live QBO/NetSuite/Sage connectors are a maintenance sinkhole if pulled in too early.
- Certificate-correctness liability: a wrong ratio delivered to a lender can trigger the exact default the product promises to prevent, so the human-review gate and audit trail are load-bearing controls, not nice-to-haves.

BIGGEST SINGLE RISK
Correctness liability on the calculation, amplified by per-loan variance. The product's entire promise is "never trip a covenant," which means a delivered certificate that miscalculates a leverage ratio or borrowing base — because the engine applied a generic EBITDA definition instead of this agreement's specific add-back schedule, or mishandled an eligibility carve-out — can produce or mask the very technical default the service exists to prevent. Because every credit agreement defines its terms differently, the calc engine cannot be uniformly tested once and trusted forever; each onboarded loan is effectively a new, lawyer-drafted spec that must be modeled and validated. This is simultaneously a technical risk (the configurable formula layer and its test coverage), an operational risk (the human reviewer must catch what the engine gets wrong, fast enough to be margin-positive), and a business/liability risk (an error sent to a lender is reputationally and possibly legally costly). The mitigation — mandatory specialist review, full audit logging, conservative "flag, don't auto-send" defaults — is sound and built into the concept, but it means the business's quality ceiling is set by reviewer rigor, and the architecture must make that review fast and traceable from day one.

QUESTIONS THE FOUNDERS MUST ANSWER
- Have you diffed 5-10 real lower-middle-market credit agreements to confirm how much covenant-definition variance the calc engine must absorb, and does that argue for a configurable formula layer (more build) or a fixed set of covenant types (simpler but less accurate)?
- What is the measured LLM extraction accuracy on real agreements against a strict schema, and does AI-assisted abstraction actually save specialist time vs manual abstraction — accelerant or theater?
- Is quarterly trial-balance/CSV upload acceptable to your first 10 prospects, letting you defer live QBO/NetSuite/Sage integrations to v2, or do buyers expect live sync on day one?
- Given you hold full company financials and credit agreements, what is your multi-tenant isolation strategy and your "wrong certificate delivered" remediation runbook, and what is your timeline to SOC 2 Type II as a sales unlock?
- How fast can a specialist review and approve one certificate on the workbench, since that throughput — not infrastructure — is the real scaling constraint and margin determinant?

RECOMMENDATION: GO
The technology is feasible, well-scoped, and not hiding research-grade bombs. The architecture is boring-proven (Next.js + Postgres + job queue + managed auth + Stripe), the genuinely custom wedge is a contained ~5-6 dev-weeks of covenant-model-plus-calc-engine work, and the riskiest component (LLM agreement abstraction) is deliberately kept off the critical path by mandatory human verification — exactly the right posture for a bootstrap. Time to revenue-earning MVP is short (~8-12 dev-weeks) because the service can run on a specialist-plus-database while the engine matures behind paying customers. The two things to watch are not blockers but must be respected in the build: the calc engine should be designed as a configurable per-loan formula layer from the start (not retrofitted), and live accounting integrations should be explicitly deferred to v2 in favor of export upload. Security must be designed in from day one given the sensitivity of the data — multi-tenant isolation, field-level encryption, full audit logging — and SOC 2 Type II planned as a year-one sales unlock, realistic given the founders' documented compliance-program background. From a pure CTO lens, this clears the bar: GO.
