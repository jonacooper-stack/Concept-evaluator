# LEGAL / REGULATORY REVIEW — CovenantClear (Managed Loan-Covenant Compliance for Lower-Middle-Market Borrowers)

Not formal legal advice. Operator pattern-recognition flagging issues for retained counsel.

ONE-PARAGRAPH RISK READ
One of the cleaner regulatory postures in this category: a B2B managed service that prepares financial calculations and a draft compliance certificate for a borrower's management to sign and submit, while explicitly disclaiming any audit, attest, tax, or legal advice. The dominant risk is not a licensing regime or a regulator — it is professional/negligence liability. The service produces numbers that, if wrong, can cause a borrower to either trip a covenant it could have avoided or to certify compliance it did not actually meet, and that downstream harm (a called loan, default interest, a damaging waiver) is exactly the kind of severe, foreseeable economic loss that drives malpractice-style claims. The good news: no specialized license required, the data is ordinary business-financial PII rather than HIPAA/biometric, and the customers are entities (not consumers), so the consumer-protection thicket largely does not apply. The whole posture rises or falls on contractual liability limitation, scope discipline (preparer not certifier), and E&O insurance.

REGULATORY MAP
- No federal financial-services license regime applies to the core service. The business is not a lender, broker, money transmitter, investment adviser, or bank service company; it is a preparer of financial calculations for a borrower. No FinCEN, OCC, state DFI, or SEC registration triggered. The most important fact: the service sits outside the regulated-financial-institution perimeter because it touches neither deposits, credit decisions, securities, nor money movement.
- AICPA / state accountancy boards — attest vs non-attest line. The live regulatory line, not a license. Preparing calculations and a certificate for management's review/signature is non-attest advisory work that does not require a CPA license in most states. The moment the service "certifies," issues an opinion, performs an "examination/review/agreed-upon procedures" engagement, or signs the certificate itself, it crosses into attest territory (state boards, AICPA SSAEs / AT-C sections). The one-pager already draws this line correctly. Risk is scope creep and marketing language, not a filing. Low IF the line holds.
- Unauthorized practice of law (UPL) — state bar rules. "Parses the loan agreement to build a structured register of every covenant, definition, threshold" edges toward interpreting a legal contract. Extracting defined terms and deadlines is generally permissible; advising on the legal meaning of an ambiguous covenant definition, or counseling on waiver strategy/lender negotiation, can be construed as practicing law. Low-to-moderate; managed by keeping output to calculation/operational deliverables and routing genuine interpretive ambiguity back to the borrower's own counsel.
- FTC Act §5 / state UDAP — advertising claims. "Never trip a covenant — keep your financing safe" is an outcome guarantee the service cannot deliver (a borrower can still breach due to bad operating performance). Deception/unfairness exposure under FTC Act §5 (15 U.S.C. §45) and state mini-FTC acts. Low and entirely fixable with disclaimed, softened copy. Must-fix before launch, but a copy edit.
- Data privacy — minimal special-category exposure. The service ingests borrower financial statements and loan agreements: business-confidential, not HIPAA PHI, biometric, or children's data. To the extent financials contain owner/employee personal info, generic state regimes (CCPA/CPRA for B2B contact data) and contractual confidentiality apply, but no GLBA Safeguards Rule trigger (the service is not a "financial institution" serving consumers; clients are commercial entities). Low — SOC-2-grade hygiene + NDA, not a privacy-law program.
- Contract / commercial law — ordinary B2B services agreement. No regulator. Low.

LICENSING SUMMARY
No occupational or industry license is required to operate this business as scoped. The founders do not need to be CPAs, attorneys, or licensed financial professionals to prepare covenant calculations and draft certificates for a client's management to review and sign — provided the service stays on the non-attest, non-legal side of the line. There is no multi-state licensing cliff: a borrower in any state can engage a preparer regardless of where the preparer sits, because the deliverable is prepared for the client's own use, not certified to a third party under a regulated engagement standard. The only "credential" question is competence-based, not license-based: the finance specialist reviewing each certificate should be genuinely qualified (a CPA or experienced controller-level reviewer) for liability and credibility reasons, but that is a hiring/quality decision, not a statutory requirement. Standard entity formation (LLC/C-corp), a local business license, and registered-agent/foreign-qualification housekeeping are the entire footprint. If the founders ever decide to certify covenants or offer agreed-upon-procedures reports, that requires a CPA firm and state accountancy-board registration — a deliberate future choice, not a launch requirement.

LIABILITY POSTURE
The heart of the file. The entity's exposure is professional negligence / E&O: if the calculation engine or the reviewing specialist gets a leverage ratio, EBITDA add-back, or borrowing-base figure wrong, the borrower may (a) certify compliance it didn't have, or (b) needlessly trip or fail to anticipate a breach — and the consequential damages (default-rate interest, a called loan, a punitive waiver/amendment, in the worst case business failure) are large relative to a quarterly subscription fee. That asymmetry — small fee, potentially seven-figure downstream harm — is the defining liability risk, managed three ways: (1) contractually, with a strong limitation-of-liability clause capping damages at fees paid (or a small multiple), an express disclaimer of consequential/indirect damages, and clear allocation that management retains responsibility for review, sign-off, and submission (the one-pager already builds this in — the single best risk decision in the design); (2) by E&O / professional-liability insurance, readily available at modest premiums for a finance-advisory shop — far cheaper than for an attest firm; and (3) by process — documented review, version control on covenant models, a sign-off trail. Personal exposure: form the entity before the first engagement, keep finances separate, carry the E&O policy. Residual hard-to-cap risk: a LOL clause may be narrowed for gross negligence or where the client is unsophisticated — but the lower-middle-market CFO/controller buyer is a commercial, reasonably sophisticated counterparty, strengthening enforceability. There is no third-party (lender) liability as long as the service never certifies to the lender; the borrower's management is the certifying party and the service is a behind-the-scenes preparer — preserving that wall is the most important operational liability control.

TOP 3 LEGAL/REGULATORY STRENGTHS
- The design draws the single most important line correctly: the service prepares for management's review/signature and never certifies to the lender, keeping it on the non-attest side of state accountancy boards and AICPA SSAE attest standards — no CPA license, no firm registration, no regulated engagement.
- Clean data and consumer-protection profile: business-financial data only (no HIPAA/biometric/children's data, no GLBA Safeguards trigger), entity buyers (no FTC ROSCA / 50-state auto-renewal thicket, no TCPA consumer exposure).
- No licensing cliff and no field-ops exposure: a single home-state footprint can serve borrowers nationwide with no per-state occupational license, and an all-remote finance team avoids multi-state wage-and-hour, workers'-comp, and background-check liability.

TOP 3 LEGAL/REGULATORY RISKS
- Professional negligence / E&O: a wrong ratio or borrowing-base figure can cause a called loan, default-rate interest, or a punitive waiver — small subscription fee against potentially seven-figure consequential harm.
- Scope creep across two lines: drifting from "prepare calculations" into "certify" (state accountancy-board attest territory) or into interpreting ambiguous covenant definitions and advising on waiver strategy (state UPL statutes).
- Deceptive-advertising exposure from the "never trip a covenant" guarantee under FTC Act §5 and state UDAP, since the service cannot guarantee an outcome it doesn't control.

BIGGEST SINGLE RISK
Professional-negligence/E&O liability driven by a structural fee-to-harm asymmetry: the borrower pays a modest quarterly subscription, but a single calculation error can directly cause a tripped covenant, default-rate interest, a frozen line, a forced waiver/amendment, or — in the tail — the loss of the company's financing and survival. When the entire value proposition is "we make sure the covenant numbers are right," an error in those numbers maps directly to catastrophic client harm — precisely the fact pattern that generates malpractice-style claims and the temptation for a damaged client to argue the service "effectively certified" the figures. Manageable but not eliminable: cap contractually (LOL to fees paid or a modest multiple, consequential-damages waiver, explicit allocation of certification/sign-off to management — which the design already does), insure via E&O (modest premium for non-attest finance advisory), and control procedurally (documented review by a qualified specialist, version-controlled models, audit trail). The two failure modes that unravel these protections: (a) gross-negligence/recklessness findings, which courts often refuse to let LOL clauses cap, and (b) marketing or conduct that blurs the preparer/certifier wall so a court treats the service as having assumed certifying responsibility. Hold the wall and carry the insurance, and this is a normal, insurable advisory-business risk rather than a fatal one.

QUESTIONS FOR REAL COUNSEL BEFORE LAUNCH
- Engagement-letter drafting: will a LOL clause capping damages at fees paid (plus a consequential-damages waiver) be enforceable against lower-middle-market commercial clients in our operating states, and how to draft it to survive a gross-negligence challenge?
- Attest line: confirm with a state-accountancy specialist exactly which activities (parsing the agreement, building the register, computing ratios, drafting the certificate for signature) remain non-attest and what specific words/acts tip us into a regulated attest or "agreed-upon procedures" engagement requiring CPA-firm registration.
- UPL line: how to structure deliverables so extracting and tracking covenant definitions/deadlines is operational/financial work and not UPL, and where exactly must we route ambiguous covenant-interpretation or waiver-strategy questions back to the client's own counsel?
- Marketing review: can counsel scrub the "never trip a covenant / keep your financing safe" positioning for FTC §5 and state UDAP exposure and approve compliant, disclaimed claim language?
- Insurance scope: what E&O / professional-liability policy form, limit, and definitions (does "professional services" cover covenant-calculation preparation?) do we need, and are cyber/privacy endorsements warranted given we hold clients' confidential financials and loan agreements?
- Entity and contracting hygiene: confirm formation, veil-protection practices, IP-assignment from contractors building the calc engine, and a standard NDA/confidentiality framework appropriate to holding clients' loan agreements.

RECOMMENDATION: GO
From a legal/regulatory standpoint this is a favorable, launchable concept. It sits outside the licensed-financial-institution perimeter, requires no occupational license, carries a clean data and consumer-protection profile, and has no field-ops or multi-state licensing cliff — the design already makes the single most important risk decision correctly by keeping the service a behind-the-scenes preparer and leaving certification/signature with the borrower's management. The one real risk, professional-negligence/E&O exposure from the fee-to-harm asymmetry, is a normal and insurable advisory-business risk, not a structural defect, provided the founders (1) hold the preparer/certifier wall and the non-attest/UPL lines as a matter of discipline and documented process, (2) launch on a counsel-drafted engagement letter with a robust LOL and management-responsibility clause, (3) carry E&O insurance from day one, and (4) soften the "never trip a covenant" guarantee to a defensible claim. None of these is structural; all are standard pre-launch hygiene, which is why this earns a GO rather than a REFINE.
