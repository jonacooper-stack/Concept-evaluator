# LEGAL / REGULATORY REVIEW — SOC 1 Readiness Managed Service

Not formal legal advice. Operator pattern-recognition flagging issues for retained counsel.

ONE-PARAGRAPH RISK READ
One of the cleaner regulatory postures in this category: a B2B managed-readiness service that explicitly does NOT perform the attestation, does NOT issue the SOC 1, and does NOT touch a license-gated activity. The single most important structural choice — "the service is explicitly NOT the auditor and does not perform the attestation, preserving the CPA firm's independence" — is exactly the right line, and if held in practice keeps the business clear of the AICPA/state-board independence machinery that governs the CPA firm. Two real, manageable exposures: (a) AICPA independence rules (ET 1.295, nonattest-services / management-responsibilities) that can disqualify the CPA firm if the readiness provider crosses into doing management's work or entangles with the audit firm via referral/fee-sharing; and (b) professional-liability exposure if a client's SOC 1 is qualified or late and the client blames the readiness vendor for a lost or delayed enterprise contract. Neither is fatal; both insurable and contract-manageable.

REGULATORY MAP
- AICPA Code of Professional Conduct — Independence (ET 1.200 / 1.295, Nonattest Services and Management Responsibilities). Governs the CPA firm, not directly the vendor — but the load-bearing constraint. The SOC 1 attestation (SSAE 18 / AT-C 320) requires the CPA to be independent. The vendor must NOT assume management responsibilities AND must not be so entangled with the audit firm that it impairs independence. The client must designate a competent individual to own the controls; the vendor advises and assembles. Moderate, structural, addressed in engagement design and contract — not a license.
- SSAE 18 / AT-C 320 (examination of controls at a service organization relevant to user entities' ICFR). The attestation standard the CPA applies; defines "management's description of the system" and the management assertion. The vendor helps prepare; management adopts and asserts. This is the product's subject matter, not an obligation on the vendor.
- No federal industry licensing applies to the vendor. This is not "public accounting." State accountancy acts (CA Board of Accountancy, NY State Board for Public Accountancy) regulate attest/audit practice and the "CPA" designation. Readiness/evidence-management consulting is NOT the practice of public accountancy and requires no CPA license, provided the vendor does not perform attest work or hold out as performing an audit. The line is "don't issue or sign the opinion, don't call it an audit." Low burden.
- FTC Act §5 / advertising-claims law. "Pass your SOC 1 every year and never lose a deal over it" is an outcome guarantee the vendor cannot control (CPA issues the opinion; client operates the controls). Deceptive-advertising risk. Low and fully self-controllable via claim discipline.
- Data privacy — limited. Evidence is largely the service org's own operational/financial data, B2B, not consumer PII. Can incidentally include employee access lists, sample transaction records. No HIPAA/biometric/children's/GLBA by default. Low-to-moderate, handled by DPA/MSA + SOC 2-grade internal security (ironically the vendor should itself be SOC 2 clean).
- State auto-renewal / subscription law (CA ARL, FTC click-to-cancel trajectory). B2B contracted revenue, not consumer auto-renew, so the consumer ARL regimes largely don't reach it. Minimal.
- UPL/"unauthorized practice" analogs. No "unauthorized practice of accountancy" claim against readiness consulting; risk only materializes if the vendor signs/issues/holds out as issuing the report. Low if the line is held.

LICENSING SUMMARY
No occupational or industry license is required for the readiness vendor as scoped. The business is B2B advisory/managed-services consulting plus software; it requires only ordinary business registration. Critically, no CPA license is needed and none should be obtained or implied, because holding a CPA out while running the client's readiness program is exactly what could entangle the engagement with attest-independence rules. The competent-person requirement under ET 1.295 falls on the CLIENT (management designates someone to own the controls), not the vendor. The one genuine credential question is commercial, not legal: customers and CPA firms will expect the vendor's controls specialists to have real SOC 1 / ICFR / SSAE 18 competence (former auditors, CISA, CIA, controls-audit backgrounds). That is a hiring/talent matter, bridgeable via a named practitioner hire and audit-firm partnerships.

LIABILITY POSTURE
Core liability is professional/E&O: a client's SOC 1 comes back qualified/late/with exceptions, the client loses or delays an enterprise contract, and argues the vendor failed to get them ready. Because the vendor explicitly does not issue the opinion and the CPA's independent judgment intervenes, direct causation is attenuated — but the "never lose a deal" positioning hands a plaintiff a ready-made reliance/breach narrative, so marketing must be disciplined and the MSA must carry strong limitation-of-liability (cap at fees paid), disclaimers of guaranteed outcomes, and an explicit statement that the CPA firm independently determines the result and the client owns its controls. Secondary exposure: data/security — the vendor aggregates sensitive control evidence and financial-system artifacts; a breach would be embarrassing and contract-breaching but insurable via cyber liability and contained by a DPA. Third, subtle: independence taint — if the vendor's involvement is ever characterized as having impaired the CPA firm's independence, the report itself could be challenged — a reputational catastrophe even if not direct legal liability. All three addressed by: a tight MSA with caps and outcome disclaimers, professional/E&O insurance (modest premium, no attest exposure), cyber-liability, an entity formed before first contract, and a documented engagement model keeping management responsibilities with the client. Residual founder personal exposure post-entity is low.

AVERAGE SCORE: 7.7 / 10

TOP 3 LEGAL/REGULATORY STRENGTHS
- Structurally outside the licensed/attest perimeter: never issues the SOC 1, so no CPA license, no state board oversight, no "unauthorized practice of accountancy" exposure — the heaviest machinery governs the CPA firm, not the vendor.
- Pure B2B revenue to finance/compliance buyers eliminates the consumer-protection minefield (auto-renewal statutes, TCPA, cooling-off) and the data-privacy heavyweights (HIPAA, GLBA, biometric, COPPA).
- A single national attestation standard (SSAE 18 / AT-C 320) means no 50-state regulatory multiplication, so remote delivery scales without a licensing cliff at each state line.

TOP 3 LEGAL/REGULATORY RISKS
- AICPA independence entanglement (ET 1.295): if the vendor crosses into making management's decisions, or ever combines/refers/fee-shares with the attesting CPA firm, it can impair that firm's independence and taint the very report it was hired to enable.
- The "never lose a deal" positioning is an outcome guarantee the vendor cannot control, exposing it to FTC §5 deceptive-advertising scrutiny and a ready-made reliance/breach theory.
- Professional/E&O liability tied to a qualified/late report causing lost enterprise revenue, plus a data-security breach of aggregated sensitive control evidence — both insurable but real.

BIGGEST SINGLE RISK
Independence entanglement under the AICPA Code (ET 1.295, management-responsibilities and nonattest-services). The entire commercial value of a SOC 1 depends on the CPA firm being independent; if the vendor's involvement is ever characterized as having assumed management's responsibilities — making the control decisions, owning the description, effectively running the client's accountability rather than advising it — or if the vendor develops a fee-sharing/co-branded relationship with the issuing audit firm, a sophisticated user-entity auditor or a state board could question the report's validity. That doesn't just create vendor liability; it can void the deliverable the client paid for and torch the vendor's reputation across a referral-driven market. The defense is entirely structural and within the founders' control: keep "management responsibilities" contractually and operationally with the client (a designated competent individual must own the controls and adopt the description), keep the CPA firm at genuine arm's length (no fee-sharing, no joint entity, no implication the vendor steers the opinion), and never let marketing or the engagement blur "we get you ready" vs "we get you the report." Hold that line and the risk stays managed; blur it and it becomes existential.

QUESTIONS FOR REAL COUNSEL BEFORE LAUNCH
1. Under AICPA ET 1.295 and AT-C 320, exactly how must the engagement and MSA be structured so the vendor's readiness work is a permissible nonattest service that does NOT impair the CPA firm's independence — what must remain a "management responsibility," and what language documents the client's designation of a competent individual?
2. Can the vendor accept referral fees from, refer clients to, or co-market with specific CPA audit firms without creating an independence problem — and what arrangement (none, neutral referral list, fee-sharing) is safe vs disqualifying?
3. How should the "never lose a deal / pass every year" positioning be worded to avoid an FTC §5 deceptive-claim problem and a breach-of-warranty/reliance theory, and what disclaimers and liability caps belong in the MSA?
4. The correct, non-infringing way to use "SOC 1" / "SOC" (AICPA-associated marks) without implying AICPA endorsement or that the vendor issues SOC reports?
5. What does the DPA need to say given evidence packages may incidentally contain personal data and sensitive financial-system artifacts, and what cyber/E&O limits are appropriate?
6. Does any state define "readiness" or "control assessment" work narrowly enough that it could be construed as the practice of public accountancy, and how is that line avoided in writing?

RECOMMENDATION: GO
This concept sits on the favorable side of nearly every dimension. It uses a hard, recurring, contractually mandated buy (a real standards-forced painkiller) while keeping the business outside the licensed, attest-regulated perimeter — the AICPA independence machinery governs the CPA firm, not the vendor, and the founders stay clear by design. No occupational license, no 50-state licensing cliff, no consumer-protection or heavy-PII regime, no field operations. The two genuine exposures — independence entanglement and outcome-guarantee/E&O liability — are both controllable through engagement structure, disciplined marketing, a templated MSA with caps and outcome disclaimers, and standard E&O + cyber insurance behind a properly formed entity. None requires a credential the founders lack; it requires a competent controls specialist hire (talent) and a few hours of specialized counsel to lock the engagement template. From a legal/regulatory standpoint a clean, fundable, low-landmine business. Proceed, with the independence line and the advertising claims treated as launch-blocking items to settle with counsel before the first signed contract.
