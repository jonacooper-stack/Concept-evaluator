LEGAL / REGULATORY REVIEW — FranAudit

Not legal advice. I am not a licensed attorney and this is not formal legal advice. I am an experienced operator with regulatory pattern-recognition flagging issues the founders must take to real franchise counsel before acting.

ONE-PARAGRAPH RISK READ
FranAudit sits directly on top of one of the more dangerous fault lines in compliance services: the FDD is a legal disclosure document governed by the FTC Franchise Rule (16 CFR Part 436) and a patchwork of state franchise statutes, and preparing/amending it and counseling a franchisor on what to disclose is squarely the practice of law. The proposed mitigation — a partnered/named franchise attorney "reviews and signs" the disclosure while non-attorney founders do "assembly, filing, tracking, project management" — is a real and commonly-used structure, but the unauthorized-practice-of-law (UPL) line is where the whole business lives or dies, and it is thinner and more fact-specific than the one-pager implies. The deepest concern is not whether the model can be made compliant (it can, in principle) but that the legally defensible version collapses the founders' margin and differentiation: the more the attorney must actually do (not rubber-stamp) to stay on the right side of UPL, the closer FranAudit gets to being a law firm's back office rather than a software-leveraged independent service. Data and consumer-protection exposure are modest; the existential risk is UPL plus the malpractice-adjacent liability of getting a disclosure wrong.

REGULATORY MAP
- FTC Franchise Rule — 16 CFR Part 436 (FTC). Governs the FDD: 23 required items, annual update within 120 days of FYE, mandatory amendment for material changes, audited financials, prescribed delivery timing. No federal filing/pre-approval, but the FTC enforces against deficient disclosure. Determining what must be disclosed, characterizing litigation, framing Item 19 FPR, and judging "materiality" are legal judgments. High burden, inherently legal not clerical.
- State franchise registration/disclosure statutes (state securities/franchise regulators). ~13 registration states — CA (DFPI, California Franchise Investment Law, Corp. Code §31000 et seq.), NY (Dept. of Law/AG, NY Franchise Act, GBL Art. 33), IL (AG, Illinois Franchise Disclosure Act), MD, VA, WA, MN, WI, ND, SD, HI, IN, MI, RI. Plus "filing/notice" states. Each requires registration/renewal before lawfully offering/selling, charges fees, and many issue examiner comment letters demanding revisions. Responding substantively to a comment letter is legal work. High, multi-jurisdictional, recurring — the must-have pull, but exactly where UPL bites.
- State UPL statutes (state bars / supreme courts / AG consumer-protection). Every state prohibits non-lawyers practicing law and a non-lawyer entity sharing legal fees with or controlling a lawyer's delivery of legal services to third parties (corporate-practice-of-law / ABA Model Rule 5.4 as adopted). The central regime — determines whether FranAudit can lawfully sell what it proposes and whether the "partnered attorney" arrangement is itself an improper fee-split or unauthorized law-firm-by-another-name. Structural burden.
- Securities-law analogy. In several registration states the franchise regulator is the securities regulator (CA DFPI), and registration is treated with securities-disclosure seriousness; misstatements carry rescission/liability for the franchisor, amplifying the malpractice-adjacent liability of any error FranAudit's process introduces.
- Data privacy — CCPA/CPRA + other state laws. Business financial statements, litigation history, officer info — largely business/entity data + some officer PII. B2B, low-sensitivity. CCPA/CPRA applies at threshold; no HIPAA/biometric/children's data. Low burden.
- FTC Act §5 / state UDAP — advertising claims. "Renewed and filed in every state, on time, every year" is an outcome promise where missed deadlines have legal consequences; overclaiming reliability/legal protection is deceptive-practices exposure. Moderate, manageable with disciplined copy.
- Regulatory trajectory. FTC has been actively scrutinizing franchising (2024 policy statement/staff guidance on franchisor conduct/undisclosed fees). The core disclosure regime (Part 436) is stable/longstanding; attention to disclosure is tightening, increasing demand but also the consequences of error. State UPL enforcement is stable but unforgiving.

LICENSING SUMMARY
FranAudit as an entity needs no special license to exist or sell software/PM services — a standard business license + entity formation suffice. The decisive credential issue is the opposite of a license-to-obtain: it is a license-the-founders-cannot-substitute-for. The substantive deliverables — drafting/amending FDD items, advising on materiality, characterizing litigation and FPRs, negotiating comment-letter responses with state examiners — require a licensed attorney, and in most states require the legal service be delivered through a lawyer or a law firm, not through a non-lawyer-owned company that merely contracts a lawyer. The founders are not attorneys. The "partnered franchise attorney" must hold an active bar license, and — critically — the engagement and attorney-client relationship likely must run from the attorney to the franchisor directly, not from FranAudit to the franchisor with the attorney as a subcontractor, to avoid both UPL and the prohibition on fee-splitting with non-lawyers (Model Rule 5.4). That structural requirement is not paperwork; it constrains who owns the client relationship, who can bill, and how revenue flows — exactly the friction that erodes the productized fixed-fee thesis. No exam/CE burden on the founders; the burden is that the legal core can never be theirs.

LIABILITY POSTURE
Three layers. (1) UPL liability: if the structure constitutes UPL, exposure includes state-bar/AG injunctions, disgorgement, unenforceable contracts, and in some states criminal misdemeanor liability — plus reputational destruction in a referral-driven market. Not insurable in any meaningful way. (2) E&O/malpractice-adjacent liability: if a missed deadline, misclassified material change, or defective Item 19 causes a franchisor to make unlawful sales, the franchisor faces rescission claims + regulatory action and looks to FranAudit. The supervising attorney carries malpractice insurance for the legal work; FranAudit needs its own tech/professional E&O, but where legal error and process error blur, coverage disputes follow and the company is the deep-pocket-of-convenience. (3) Personal exposure pre-entity: ordinary, solved by proper formation + E&O + disciplined contracting. The first two layers dominate. Contracts can allocate some risk (LoL, indemnification from the franchisor for data accuracy, clear delineation that legal judgment is the attorney's), but cannot cure UPL and cannot fully transfer the consequence of a blown statutory deadline the client paid you to catch.

BIGGEST SINGLE RISK
UPL is the issue most likely to kill or fundamentally reshape this business, and it does so worse than a simple yes/no. Every state prohibits non-lawyers practicing law and prohibits a non-lawyer-owned entity from sharing legal fees with or controlling a lawyer's delivery of legal services to third parties (Model Rule 5.4 as adopted; enforced by state bars, supreme courts, AG consumer-protection units). Preparing/amending an FDD, judging a "material change" under Part 436, framing an Item 19 FPR, and negotiating language with a CA DFPI/NY/IL examiner are all legal judgments. The proposed cure only works if the attorney genuinely makes the legal judgments, the attorney-client relationship runs attorney-to-franchisor (not FranAudit-to-franchisor with the lawyer as a vendor), and fees are not split in a prohibited way. The trap: the compliant version and the profitable version pull in opposite directions — the more "assembly" the non-attorneys do, the more it looks like the company is practicing law through a figurehead lawyer; the more the lawyer actually does, the less productized/margin-rich FranAudit is and the more the lawyer owns the client and economics. A single UPL finding in one registration state — or one disgruntled franchisor who got a defective FDD and complains to a state bar — can produce injunctions, disgorgement, unenforceable customer contracts, and reputational collapse in a tight referral-driven market, none of it insurable.

QUESTIONS FOR REAL COUNSEL BEFORE LAUNCH
1. Under UPL and Rule 5.4 in the registration states FranAudit will serve (CA, NY, IL, MD, WA, MN, WI), can a non-attorney-owned company lawfully (a) assemble/populate FDD items, (b) manage state filings, (c) coordinate comment-letter responses, where a separately-engaged attorney makes all substantive disclosure judgments — and which tasks must the attorney perform personally?
2. How must the engagement be structured to avoid improper fee-splitting: must the attorney-client relationship run directly attorney-to-franchisor, must the legal fee be billed/held separately from the software/PM fee, and is a flat-fee bundled subscription including legal work permissible in each target state?
3. What is the line between "legal work" (attorney-only) and "ministerial/administrative work" (founders) for FDD prep and filings in each registration state — a written task-allocation matrix to build product/pricing around?
4. Does responding substantively to a state examiner's comment letter constitute the practice of law and/or representation before a state agency that only an admitted attorney may perform?
5. What liability allocation is enforceable: can FranAudit cap/disclaim liability for a missed statutory deadline its product was sold to catch, and how should franchisor-data-accuracy indemnification and the supervising attorney's malpractice coverage interlock?
6. What E&O/tech-professional-liability coverage is available adjacent to legal services, and does any carrier exclude UPL-related claims (they typically do)?
7. Do any state franchise-examiner rules require filings be submitted by, or certified by, the franchisor's counsel of record, forcing the attorney to the front of the relationship?

RECOMMENDATION: REFINE
The demand is real and statutorily forced, but the business as drafted is built on the most dangerous possible substrate for non-attorney founders — a legal disclosure document and multi-state legal-filing regime where the core deliverable is the practice of law. From a pure risk posture (average 4.8) this is not a confident GO. The path to acceptability is structural, not cosmetic. The strongest refinement is to flip the relationship so the partnered franchise attorney (or a captive law-firm entity under proper ethical structure) is the named provider of the legal work with a direct attorney-client relationship to the franchisor, and FranAudit sells the software, deadline calendar, document-assembly tooling, filing logistics, and project management to the law firm and/or to franchisors as an administrative layer — i.e., productize the operations around the legal work, not the legal work itself. A second, cleaner refinement: narrow scope to the indisputably non-legal slices (compliance calendar, deadline tracking, audited-financials coordination, filing-status/fee tracking) sold as software, leaving all FDD drafting and examiner negotiation to the customer's own counsel. Either path materially lowers UPL and liability exposure but also shrinks the wallet and differentiation, so the founders must confirm with franchise counsel that a structure exists that is both legal in the registration states and still leaves enough margin and client ownership to hit the income ladder. Until that structural answer is in hand, this is a REFINE, not a GO.
