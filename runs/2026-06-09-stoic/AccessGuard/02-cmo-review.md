CMO REVIEW — AccessGuard

ONE-PARAGRAPH MARKET READ
This is one of the cleaner painkiller theses I have seen in a while: the buy is forced by a legal demand letter, the customer is enumerable from public court dockets, and the recurring logic (sites drift, so attestation must renew) is genuinely true rather than manufactured. The marketing problem is almost ideal for these founders — a distrust-laden category (overlays burned everyone) where a sharp, evidence-based, "we keep the receipts" position cuts through immediately. The two real questions are competitive air (Level Access, Deque, and AudioEye are not sleepy — AudioEye is public and aggressively litigation-focused) and whether the acute "just got sued" trigger produces enough durable, renewing volume versus a one-time fix-and-leave dynamic. On balance it sells, it positions, and it distributes, but the differentiation against well-funded specialists is thinner than the one-pager implies.

POSITIONING DRAFT
"You just got an ADA demand letter. We make your website provably accessible, keep a dated audit trail your lawyer can hand a judge, and watch it every week so it never drifts back — so you stop writing $15K settlement checks." That is a sharp, ownable, customer-language sentence. It has edges: it names the trigger (demand letter), the deliverable (provable + dated audit trail), the recurring mechanism (watch it weekly), and the financial frame (vs. settlements). It explicitly is NOT "we help businesses be accessible," which is the mush version everyone else sells. The one risk in the positioning is the implicit legal promise ("a case the day a letter arrives") — marketing must not over-claim litigation outcomes, which Legal will flag.

THE WEDGE
The first 100 customers come from scraped Title III filings and demand-letter patterns. This is the strongest part of the concept and it is real: federal ADA Title III website filings run in the multiple thousands per year (Seyfarth Shaw's ADA Title III tracker reported roughly 4,000+ federal filings annually in recent years, heavily concentrated in NY, FL, CA) [partially ASSUMED on exact current count — verify against the latest Seyfarth Shaw and UsableNet annual reports, free to download]. PACER dockets are public; named-defendant lists are enumerable for near-zero cost. The tactic: a researcher/scraper builds a weekly list of newly-named defendants, and outbound (email + LinkedIn + a physical letter) hits them inside the 1-to-3-week window when pain is acute and budget is unlocked. CAC on outbound to a freshly-sued company with a $30K/yr offer cheaper than the next settlement should be low — [ASSUMED CAC $1,500-$4,000 per closed deal at a 3-8% cold-outbound-to-meeting rate; verify with a 200-letter test campaign costing ~$2,000]. Secondary wedge: defense-side ADA law firm referrals — a handful of firms see most cases and want a vetted remediation partner. Tertiary: SEO on "ADA website lawsuit / demand letter / accessiBe alternative." The wedge is specific, low-cost, and founder-fit.

COMPETITIVE PICTURE
- Level Access (merged with eSSENTIAL Accessibility) — enterprise specialist; deep manual audit + platform + legal expertise; PE-backed, expensive, slow; ignores the $5M-$100M just-sued buyer.
- Deque Systems — engineering/standards authority (axe is the de facto testing engine); sells tools and services, not a turnkey managed defense service for mid-market.
- AudioEye (NASDAQ: AYE) — public, ~$30M+ revenue, explicitly litigation-and-mid-market positioned, offers monitoring + remediation + a managed component, moving away from pure overlay. The single most dangerous competitor — closest to AccessGuard's exact positioning and funded/marketing-aggressive.
- accessiBe / UserWay — overlay vendors, reputation-damaged among plaintiffs and dinged by the FTC (accessiBe paid a $1M FTC settlement in 2025 over deceptive accessibility claims). Burned-customer pool = a gift to AccessGuard's message.
- Boutique audit consultancies + general web agencies — fragmented, one-time-audit, no recurring attestation product, poorly marketed. The sleepy underbelly to out-position.

Where is the air? The specific intersection of (a) productized recurring managed service, (b) aimed at the just-sued $5M-$100M mid-market buyer, (c) NOT an overlay, (d) sold on defensible documentation. The genuine threat is AudioEye occupying this exact lane with more money — the air exists, but it is shared, not empty. This is NOT the wide-open sleepy field the archetype describes.

AVERAGE SCORE: 7.7 / 10

TOP 3 MARKET STRENGTHS
- An enumerable, public-record buyer list (PACER Title III dockets) hitting customers at the exact moment of acute, legally-forced, budgeted pain — a near-perfect wedge.
- Sharp, ownable positioning in a distrust-laden category, with a compounding "credible alternative to the FTC-fined overlays" story that lowers CAC over time.
- A GTM motion (trigger-outbound + founder-led close + defense-attorney referral) that maps precisely to the founders' edge with no field ops and no paid-auction dependence.

TOP 3 MARKET RISKS
- AudioEye (NASDAQ: AYE) is a funded, public, litigation-focused incumbent occupying nearly the identical positioning — the air is contested, not open.
- The acute "just got sued" trigger may produce one-time fix-and-leave behavior; if clients churn after the threat passes, the recurring thesis weakens and CAC must be re-amortized over a shorter life.
- The differentiation (recurring + documentation) is execution-based and copyable inside 90 days by any focused competitor, so the lead is a head start, not a structural moat.

BIGGEST SINGLE RISK
The retention/durability of the recurring revenue under an acute-trigger acquisition model. The wedge is excellent precisely because the demand letter forces an immediate buy — but a forced buyer is also one whose felt urgency decays once the threat is resolved and the remediation sprint is done. The whole financial case rests on "sites drift, so attestation renews," which is true technically, but the customer's perceived need to keep paying $2,500/mo drops sharply the day after their lawyer settles. If a meaningful share downgrade or cancel at renewal, the model degrades from durable recurring revenue toward a churny project business, CAC must be recovered over fewer months, and the income ladder gets much harder. The marketing answer — designed in from day one — is to reframe the product around continuous legal exposure (every CMS update reopens liability) and make the dated audit trail a living asset whose value is destroyed by a coverage gap. Whether that reframe holds at renewal is the question on which this business lives or dies.

QUESTIONS THE FOUNDERS MUST ANSWER BEFORE I'M COMFORTABLE
- What is the realistic month-13 net revenue retention, and the actual cancellation rate at first annual renewal once the triggering lawsuit is resolved? (Cohort test: sign 10 just-sued clients, track renewal once their case settles.)
- How do you out-position AudioEye specifically — what does a freshly-sued retailer who Googles their problem see, and why pick AccessGuard over a public company with a sales team?
- What is the true yield of the defense-attorney referral channel — will ADA-defense firms actually refer, or view it as a conflict/revenue stream they keep? (Verify with 5 candid conversations.)
- Can the outbound clear legal and tone review without appearing ambulance-chasing, and what is the measured reply-and-meeting rate on a 200-contact test?
- How large and reachable is the "watched a peer get hit" preventive segment, given weaker urgency?

RECOMMENDATION: GO
This clears my bar on the dimensions that matter most: market pull is high and legally forced, the customer is enumerable from public dockets, positioning is sharp, and GTM fits the founders' edge with no field ops. The two things keeping it from a higher mark are real and must be managed: AudioEye is a funded competitor in nearly the same lane (contested, not sleepy, air), and the recurring thesis under an acute-trigger model is unproven. Neither is fatal. Advance it with two non-negotiable validation steps before meaningful spend: (1) a renewal/retention cohort test, and (2) a 200-contact outbound test plus 5 defense-attorney conversations. If retention holds at renewal, this is a strong, durable concept; if not, it is a good project business wearing a subscription costume.
