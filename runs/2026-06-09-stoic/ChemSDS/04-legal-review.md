LEGAL / REGULATORY REVIEW — ChemSDS

Not legal advice. I am not a licensed attorney; this is an experienced operator's regulatory pattern-recognition meant to flag what the founders must brief real counsel on, not to resolve those questions.

ONE-PARAGRAPH RISK READ
The business operates in a lightly-licensed but liability-heavy zone: there is no license required to author Safety Data Sheets or classify hazards, so the licensing posture is genuinely clean, but ChemSDS is selling the correctness of a federally-mandated hazard document under OSHA's Hazard Communication Standard (29 CFR 1910.1200), and the concept's own note concedes the core risk — an under-classified hazard on a SDS or label that a downstream worker, transporter, or emergency responder relies on. That is professional-liability exposure of the "errors & omissions plus possible bodily-injury" kind, and it does not vanish into a terms-of-service limitation. The data-privacy and consumer-protection surface is small and ordinary (B2B SaaS), but the substantive E&O exposure and a "who actually signs the classification" question are the things that decide whether this is insurable and structurable rather than a latent landmine.

REGULATORY MAP
- OSHA Hazard Communication Standard — 29 CFR 1910.1200 (HazCom 2012, final rule May 20, 2024 aligning to GHS Revision 7). Regulator: federal OSHA + ~22 OSHA-approved State Plan states (Cal/OSHA, WA, OR, MI) that may adopt equivalent-or-stricter rules/deadlines. Scope: manufacturers/importers classify hazards, author the 16-section SDS, label per GHS; employers maintain access. The deliverable's accuracy IS the regulated artifact. Staggered dates (substances ~Jan 19, 2026; mixtures ~July 19, 2027) are the demand engine.
- DOT Hazardous Materials Regulations — 49 CFR Parts 171–180 (PHMSA). Adjacent: transport classification, UN numbers, placarding differ from OSHA/GHS workplace classification. If ChemSDS touches SDS Section 14 it brushes PHMSA — scope-creep and liability vector.
- EPA — EPCRA Tier II / SARA Title III §311–312 (40 CFR Part 370) and TSCA (15 USC 2601 et seq.). The concept offers "help with EPCRA Tier II / SARA 312." Tier II goes to SERCs/LEPCs/fire departments on a March 1 cycle. Helping file a government report is higher-liability than authoring a document.
- FTC / state auto-renewal (ROSCA; CA ARL; click-to-cancel). Applies to the subscription but B2B → low burden.
- State data-privacy (CCPA/CPRA + CO/VA/CT/TX). Minimal trigger: business-contact data + portal access logs, no special categories.
- GHS is a UN framework, not a US regulator — criteria are codified and public (UN "Purple Book"), favorable: deterministic-ish published criteria, not proprietary black-box law.

LICENSING SUMMARY
No occupational or business license is required to author SDS, classify hazards under GHS, or generate GHS labels in the US. OSHA does not license SDS authors; it imposes obligations on manufacturers/importers/employers, who may delegate authoring freely. No "certified SDS author" credential is mandated. Genuinely clean: only ordinary entity formation + a generic local business license. The nuance is professional, not licensure: a PE or board-certified toxicologist/CIH sign-off is not legally required, but a named credentialed reviewer is the practical standard of care that makes the service defensible and insurable — the moat the concept names as bridgeable via a hired/partnered chemist-toxicologist.

LIABILITY POSTURE
The crux. ChemSDS authors a federally-mandated safety document and a hazard classification that downstream workers, handlers, transporters, and emergency responders rely on. If a classification understates a real hazard — wrong signal word, missing pictogram, omitted health hazard, wrong PPE — and someone is injured, ChemSDS is squarely in the chain: negligent-misrepresentation / professional-negligence / failure-to-warn, potentially with bodily-injury damages dwarfing the contract value. A SaaS limitation-of-liability + "customer remains responsible for compliance" clause helps (and the manufacturer does retain primary regulatory duty to OSHA), but contracts do not extinguish a tort claim by an injured third party who never signed ChemSDS's terms. Insurance is the load-bearing answer: Technology/Miscellaneous Professional Liability/E&O, and given the bodily-injury vector, likely products-liability/contingent-bodily-injury coverage — premiums materially higher than plain B2B SaaS, with underwriters asking exactly who signs the classifications and on what evidence. Contracts must do real work: strong LOL caps, customer warranties on accuracy/completeness of supplied ingredient/SDS data (garbage-in is a major defense), clear allocation that the customer retains the regulatory obligation, mutual indemnity. Founders carry personal exposure only until a properly capitalized, insured LLC/corp with clean formalities exists — standard, but must be done before the first SDS ships.

- Contracting and insurability friction: heavier-than-SaaS negotiated terms and materially higher E&O/products-liability premiums.
- Scope-creep into higher-liability regulatory filings (EPCRA Tier II / SARA 312 to SERC/LEPC, and DOT/PHMSA 49 CFR transport classification in SDS Section 14), each a distinct error-and-penalty vector.

BIGGEST SINGLE RISK
The defining hazard is professional/products liability from an incorrect hazard classification that understates a real danger. ChemSDS is authoring the legally-operative safety document that workers, transporters, and emergency responders rely on under 29 CFR 1910.1200, and it layers an expert sign-off precisely because the classification is a consequential professional judgment. If a SDS or label omits a health hazard, gives the wrong signal word/pictogram, or misstates PPE, and a worker is harmed, ChemSDS sits in the failure-to-warn chain alongside the manufacturer. An LOL clause and "customer retains regulatory responsibility" language protect against the contracting customer but do nothing against an injured third party's tort claim, and bodily-injury damages can be orders of magnitude larger than the ~$21.6K/yr [ASSUMED] contract value. The model's viability rests on (a) obtainable, affordably-priced E&O plus contingent-bodily-injury/products coverage, and (b) a documented, defensible classification methodology with a named credentialed reviewer (CIH/toxicologist) and strong customer data-accuracy warranties — without those, a single understated SDS can produce a claim exceeding years of revenue.

QUESTIONS FOR REAL COUNSEL BEFORE LAUNCH
1. Realistic exposure under negligent-misrepresentation / professional-negligence / failure-to-warn when ChemSDS authors a SDS/classification a downstream third party (not our customer) relies on and is injured — and how much can an LOL clause actually constrain that?
2. What insurance program is required and obtainable — Tech/Misc Professional Liability (E&O), contingent bodily injury, products liability — and what underwriting conditions (reviewer credentials, methodology documentation) at what premium vs ~$21.6K/yr ARPU? (Cheap experiment: two specialty-broker quotes against a one-page service description before building.)
3. How should the chemist/toxicologist reviewer be engaged (employee vs 1099 vs partner firm), and how to structure IP assignment, standard-of-care representations, and any PE/CIH stamp so the sign-off is legally meaningful and insurable?
4. What customer-contract architecture — LOL caps, customer warranties on supplied-data accuracy, allocation that the customer retains primary OSHA obligation, mutual indemnity — and is clickwrap sufficient or must these be signed?
5. Incremental liability/agency questions from offering EPCRA Tier II / SARA 312 filing support and populating SDS Section 14 transport info under DOT/PHMSA — carve out, disclaim, or partner at launch?
6. How do OSHA State Plan states (Cal/OSHA, WA, OR, MI) deviate from federal HazCom in substance and deadline, and does serving them multiply exposure/disclosures?
7. Is the underlying hazard/tox data properly licensed for commercial redistribution in customer-facing SDS, and are there trademark conflicts on the name in the EHS/SDS category?

RECOMMENDATION: REFINE
The regulatory framing is attractive — a federally-mandated, deadline-driven, recurring, budgeted buy with no licensing gate and a clean privacy/consumer surface. What pulls it out of a clean GO is liability dimension 8: ChemSDS is on the hook for the correctness of a safety document with a third-party bodily-injury vector that contracts cannot fully contain, so the model lives or dies on insurability and a defensible methodology. The refinements are concrete and within reach: (1) confirm affordable E&O + contingent-bodily-injury/products coverage before build (a two-quote experiment); (2) lock in a named credentialed reviewer (CIH/toxicologist) with documented methodology; (3) require enforceable customer warranties on input-data accuracy; (4) at launch, scope tightly to SDS authoring/labeling/portal and carve out or partner the higher-liability EPCRA Tier II filings and DOT/PHMSA transport classification until the core is proven and insured. With those four moves, the residual risk is a manageable, insurable professional-services exposure rather than a latent landmine.
