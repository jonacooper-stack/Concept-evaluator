LEGAL / REGULATORY REVIEW — Managed License & Permit Renewal Compliance for Multi-Location Food-Service Operators

(Not legal advice; experienced-operator pattern-recognition to brief real counsel.)

ONE-PARAGRAPH RISK READ
A low-direct-regulatory-burden B2B SaaS-plus-managed-service play, and that is its legal beauty: the founders are not in a regulated industry; their customers are. The business runs a tracking/filing spine for someone else's compliance obligations, keeping the founders out of liquor, food-safety, and fire-code liability themselves — provided they are scrupulous about disclaiming guarantees and structuring the relationship as an administrative agent, not a licensed professional or guarantor of outcomes. The two real landmines: (1) acting as a "third-party filer/agent" before government authorities across hundreds of jurisdictions, some of which require the actual license-holder, a registered agent, or a licensed professional to file (notably liquor licenses), and (2) liability creep — if a location gets shut down for a lapsed permit "the service was supposed to handle," the customer will look to the contract, so indemnity, limitation-of-liability, and scope-of-service language is the whole ballgame. None of this is fatal; it is contract-drafting and a careful service-scope definition — the cheapest kind of legal risk to manage.

REGULATORY MAP
- FTC Act §5 / state UDAP (advertising & service claims) — how the service markets ("we keep you compliant," "never miss a renewal"); low-to-moderate, controllable by disciplined copy and avoiding outcome guarantees.
- State liquor/ABC third-party filing rules (CA ABC, TX TABC, NY SLA) — who may submit/renew a liquor license; many ABCs require the licensee or an attorney/licensed agent, restrict POA filings; the part of the renewal stack the service can least uniformly file on the customer's behalf — moderate, jurisdiction-specific.
- FTC ROSCA / state auto-renewal laws (CA 17600 et seq., NY) — the service's OWN recurring subscription billing; low if clear-disclosure + easy-cancel built in.
- Data privacy — CCPA/CPRA + state laws; owner/officer names, addresses, food-handler staff names/cert numbers, payment data; low-to-moderate, standard privacy program, no special categories.
- PCI-DSS (contractual) — passing through government fees + subscriptions; outsource card handling to Stripe → SAQ-A; low.
- UPL (state bars) — preparing/"advising" on liquor/permit applications can edge toward UPL if the service interprets law or represents the client; low-to-moderate; stay on the administrative/form-prep side, partner with local counsel for liquor.
- TCPA/CAN-SPAM — renewal-reminder texts/emails + outbound marketing; low with consent + opt-out.
- Money-transmission / pass-through fee handling — if the service collects government fees and remits, structure as agent-of-payor pass-through (generally NOT money transmission), never holding funds as a transmitter; low if fees paid by the customer directly or handled as a true agent — worth a counsel check before scaling fee collection.

LICENSING SUMMARY
The business itself needs essentially nothing exotic: a standard entity (LLC), a local business license, and that is the floor. The founders do NOT need food-safety credentials, liquor licenses, or contractor licenses — they hold none of the regulated permits; the restaurant does. The one nuance is "third-party agent/filer" status: some jurisdictions let a third party submit a renewal on POA/authorized-agent basis (easy), some require the license-holder's own login/signature (the service can prep-and-hand-off but not submit), and liquor authorities are strictest, frequently requiring the licensee or a licensed attorney. For liquor specifically, the service should either (a) limit itself to preparation, document-chasing, deadline management and hand final submission to the client/their attorney, or (b) partner with local liquor-license attorneys per state. No single national license; the model works because it sells coordination, not a credential. Licensing burden on the founders is genuinely minimal — one of the cleanest dimensions in the concept.

LIABILITY POSTURE
The central exposure is performance liability: the customer buys "you make sure we don't lapse," and a lapse can close a location, forfeit a liquor license, or trigger fines. If the service misses or fat-fingers a renewal, the customer's consequential damages (lost revenue from a shuttered location, reinstatement costs) dwarf the subscription fee. Contained — not eliminated — by (1) a tightly drafted MSA with a limitation-of-liability cap (e.g., trailing-12-months fees), exclusion of consequential/lost-profit damages, and an explicit statement that the customer remains the responsible license-holder and must confirm submissions; (2) E&O / professional liability + general liability + cyber/tech E&O, insurable at modest premiums; (3) clear scope language that the service handles the renewal/documentation spine and the operator owns physical/inspection compliance. Personal exposure is low once the LLC is formed and they avoid personal guarantees and gross-negligence conduct. Data liability is modest (business + light staff PII, no PHI/biometric), covered by a standard cyber policy. The residual, irreducible risk is reputational/contractual fallout from a single high-profile missed renewal that shuts a client location — which is why the LOL cap, the "customer-confirms" workflow step, and E&O are non-negotiable.

TOP 3 LEGAL/REGULATORY STRENGTHS
- The founders carry no industry-specific regulatory burden or credential requirement — they coordinate other parties' compliance, so the heavy regimes (food safety, liquor, fire code) sit on the customer.
- Clean IP and data posture: original software, public-portal/public-data dependency with no revocable restricted API, only business/light-employee PII.
- Durable, stable regulatory trajectory: permit-renewal mandates not loosening, portal digitization making the model more feasible over time.

TOP 3 LEGAL/REGULATORY RISKS
- Performance/consequential-liability creep: a single missed renewal can shut a client location and produce damages vastly exceeding the subscription, making the LOL cap, consequential-damages exclusion, and "customer-confirms" workflow load-bearing.
- Third-party-filer and liquor-license constraints: many ABCs (CA ABC, TABC, NY SLA) and some jurisdictions bar non-licensee/non-attorney third parties from submitting renewals, fragmenting what the service can actually file vs only prepare, with UPL exposure if it drifts into legal advice.
- Marketing-claim/UDAP risk: "we keep you compliant" / "never miss a renewal" invites FTC §5 / state-AG UDAP scrutiny and arms plaintiff customers after any lapse; claims must be hedged.

BIGGEST SINGLE RISK
The gap between what customers will believe they bought and what the service can legally and operationally guarantee, crystallized at the moment a location gets shut down for a lapsed permit. The customer bought "you make sure we never lapse"; the service, to survive, must contractually be "we track, prepare, prompt, and file where permitted, but you remain the responsible license-holder and must confirm." If that gap is not closed in writing before the first customer signs — via a negotiated MSA with a LOL cap (trailing fees), exclusion of consequential/lost-profit damages, explicit allocation of the license-holder's own duty, and a workflow that forces the client to acknowledge submissions — then a single shuttered location produces a claim for lost revenue and reinstatement costs an order of magnitude larger than the account's lifetime value, plus a UDAP/breach narrative built on the company's own marketing copy. Not fatal and entirely a function of contract drafting and scope discipline, but it must be solved at day one, not at scale — which is why a competent commercial attorney is a launch prerequisite, not a luxury.

QUESTIONS FOR REAL COUNSEL BEFORE LAUNCH
- In our launch states, which permit/license renewals may a non-attorney third-party agent legally submit (POA/authorized agent), and which — especially liquor — require the licensee or a licensed attorney, so we can scope "we file" vs "we prepare and you file"?
- Draft an MSA with a LOL cap, exclusion of consequential/lost-profit damages, explicit "customer remains the responsible license-holder," and an indemnity structure — is clickwrap defensible for smaller accounts, or do all accounts need a signed MSA given shut-a-location stakes?
- Does preparing/interpreting/advising on liquor/permit applications create UPL exposure in our target states, and where do we partner with/refer to local licensed attorneys?
- If we collect government fees and remit them, does any state money-transmitter regime apply, or is this clean agent-of-payor pass-through, and how should funds flow to stay out of money-transmission?
- What "compliance"/"never missing a renewal" claims are safe under FTC §5 and state UDAP, and what hedging must accompany them?
- Does our own recurring subscription billing comply with FTC ROSCA and CA/NY auto-renewal laws?

RECOMMENDATION: GO
From a purely legal/regulatory standpoint an attractive, low-burden concept: the founders need no credential, sit outside the regulated industries their customers occupy, have clean IP and modest data exposure, and face a stable-to-favorable regulatory trajectory. The risk is concentrated, knowable, and contract-shaped rather than diffuse or structural — it lives almost entirely in (a) defining service scope around third-party-filer and liquor-license limits and (b) capping performance/consequential liability in a negotiated MSA backed by E&O. To tighten before scale: launch in a small number of states first to learn each ABC's/jurisdiction's third-party-filer rules before promising nationwide filing; scope liquor renewals as "prepare/coordinate" with attorney partners rather than "file"; and discipline all marketing away from absolute compliance guarantees. With those controls, the legal posture supports a GO.
