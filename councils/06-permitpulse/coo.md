COO REVIEW — Managed License & Permit Renewal Compliance for Multi-Location Food-Service Operators

ONE-PARAGRAPH OPERATIONS READ
Genuinely remote, software-leveraged managed service — no trucks, installs, or on-site inspections — squarely the operational shape the founders want. The unit of work is a recurring renewal-filing cycle plus exception handling, and most volume (tracking, reminders, deadline math, document chasing) is automatable. The deep risk is not labor density or geography; it is the fragmentation and non-uniformity of the work itself: every jurisdiction has its own portal, agent-of-record rules, document set, and renewal mechanics, so "specialist labor" hides a long tail of bespoke manual research that resists standardization and quietly inflates per-customer touch time. Two founders plus a small ops team can plausibly run this at the target income, but only if they ruthlessly geo-cluster early customers into a handful of jurisdictions rather than chasing 50-location groups spread across 30 states.

THE TUESDAY-IN-MARCH WALKTHROUGH
Month 12: [ASSUMED] ~45 operator accounts averaging ~8 locations = ~360 locations under management at ~$70/location/month. Two founders + one full-time permit-ops specialist (hired ~month 6–7).
Tuesday, early March. Overnight digest: 14 renewals due in the next 30 days, 6 documents outstanding (a notarized owner affidavit for a Texas liquor renewal, two updated food-manager certs in Ohio, a COI for a Chicago sidewalk-café permit), 2 portals flagged a fee-schedule change the rule-lookup hasn't reconciled.
The specialist works the renewal queue. Of 14, ~9 are "clean" — a jurisdiction allowing third-party agent filing, a known form, a payment to push — each 15–35 min including portal login, confirming data, submitting, recording the confirmation. The other 5 are messy: one county switched portals and the operator's login no longer works (25-min call to the GM who doesn't know it either, then to the county clerk on lunch). One liquor renewal in a control state requires a wet-signature notarized form that legally cannot be filed by a third party — so the specialist preps the packet, FedExes it to the location with a checklist, and the deliverable becomes "we did everything except the signature you must physically provide." That nuance — we are NOT agent of record everywhere — is the operational crux of a Tuesday.
Founder A handles two onboardings: a new 11-location group. Building their permit inventory means, per location, identifying every required permit across health/fire/liquor/sign/business/food-handler, confirming status/renewal date with each authority, and collecting documents from a slow GM — research-heavy and front-loaded ([ASSUMED] 3–6 hrs/location at onboarding), so an 11-location group is ~40–60 hours over 2–3 weeks. Founder B is on sales calls and a quality escape: a Florida food-handler cert lapsed because the operator never told us the employee was rehired under a new entity — staff-cert tracking is only as good as the operator's HR feed. Where it breaks: the messy-tail renewals and a missed cert — nothing physical, just jurisdiction variance and dependency on operators handing us accurate data and credentials.

CAPACITY MATH
Ladder: ~$300K/founder mo12 = ~$600K combined. To net ~$600K, [ASSUMED] ~$800K–$1.0M ARR (one specialist ~$60–80K loaded + tooling ~$20–40K eats the gap).
Revenue: at $70/location/month = $840/location/yr. $900K ARR ÷ $840 = ~1,070 locations. At ~8/account = ~134 accounts — closing ~11 accounts/month, a meaningful mo12 ask but not implausible for a marketing-led motion.
Throughput (the real test): per location/year, [ASSUMED] ~8 filings × ~30 min human-touch each after automation = ~4 hrs/location/yr. 1,070 × 4 = ~4,280 hrs/yr recurring. Plus onboarding: ~half the book onboarded in year 1 × ~4 hrs/location = ~2,140 hrs. Total ~6,420 hrs ÷ ~1,700 productive hrs/FTE = ~3.8 delivery FTEs. So at mo12 the realistic team is two founders (split with sales/ops) + ~2–3 specialists, not one. The model holds — not a 50-person op — but it's more labor-intensive per dollar than SaaS, and per-location touch time is the single most important number. 30 min/filing → ~3–4 FTEs; 60 min/filing → FTE count doubles and the math tightens toward higher pricing or aggressive automation. Government-fee pass-through adds an AP/trust-money burden needing clean process (possibly a trust account).

AVERAGE SCORE: 6.2 / 10

TOP 3 OPERATIONAL STRENGTHS
- Fully remote, zero field ops — delivery spine is filings and correspondence from a laptop.
- Year-round, staggered, weather-neutral demand — smooth operating load and revenue, no seasonal crunch.
- Affordable, available first hire from a non-scarce compliance-admin/paralegal-adjacent pool.

TOP 3 OPERATIONAL RISKS
- Jurisdictional variance defeats standardization — every city/county/state has its own portal, agent-of-record rules, and document set; the long tail stays bespoke and inflates per-customer touch time.
- Asymmetric quality stakes with customer-dependent data — one missed renewal can shut a location, yet accuracy depends on operators supplying correct credentials, staff changes, and documents we don't control.
- Labor scales near-linearly with locations — ~4 hrs/location/yr recurring + front-loaded onboarding = ~3.8 FTEs at ~$900K ARR; moderate, not SaaS-like, leverage.

BIGGEST SINGLE RISK
The collision of asymmetric liability with uncontrolled input data across non-uniform jurisdictions. The value prop is "we make sure you never miss a renewal that could close your location" — so the failure mode is catastrophic and visible, not soft. But every renewal's accuracy depends on data and access the operator must hand over and keep current: portal credentials that change, staff rehired under new entities whose certs we never hear about, ownership/insurance changes that alter a liquor renewal, document collection from slow GMs. In jurisdictions where we are not the legal agent-of-record (common for liquor and many health permits), we can do 95% of the work and still the operator must physically sign or appear, so a lapse can occur on the 5% we cannot control while the customer believes "the service handles it." That gap between perceived full coverage and actual partial coverage is where churn, disputes, and even liability claims live — and it is an operational/process problem (crisp scope contracts, hard data-feed SLAs, fail-safe escalation, explicit "your action required" handoffs) before it is a software problem. If the founders can't operationalize that boundary tightly, two people + a couple specialists will spend their time firefighting blame for misses structurally outside their control.

QUESTIONS THE FOUNDERS MUST ANSWER BEFORE I'M COMFORTABLE
- Real measured human-touch time per filing, split clean vs messy, across at least 3 jurisdiction types? (Time-log the first 200 filings — 20 vs 60 min decides the whole FTE count.)
- In how many target jurisdictions can you legally act as third-party agent-of-record vs where the operator must sign/appear personally — and how will the contract/workflow draw that line so a miss on the operator's required action is not your liability or churn?
- How do you keep inputs current (credentials, staff-cert changes, ownership/insurance) given you don't control the operator's HR/admin — what is the hard data-feed/SLA mechanism, and what happens when an operator doesn't respond before a deadline?

RECOMMENDATION: REFINE
The operational shape is fundamentally right — remote, recurring, no field ops, year-round, affordable staffing — a serious look. But three simplifications before GO. First, geo-concentrate: launch in a tightly chosen handful of jurisdictions (one or two states' restaurant-dense metros) so the rules-engine build and specialist knowledge compound; chasing geographically dispersed 50-location groups day one would shatter the labor model. Second, contractually and operationally hard-wire the agent-of-record boundary and a data-feed SLA so catastrophic-miss liability sits where the founders can control it. Third, drive automation at the messy tail, not just clean filings — the FTE math only stays lean if jurisdictional research and document-chasing get systematized, since that long tail left manual turns a 3-FTE business into a 6-FTE one.
