CTO REVIEW — Managed Supplier-Compliance Document & Attestation Service for SMB Suppliers to Large Buyers

ONE-PARAGRAPH TECHNICAL READ
Mostly boring, very buildable: the software core is a tracking/workflow database (buyer relationships, document inventory, expiration dates, renewal reminders) wrapped in a portal, plus AI-assisted questionnaire pre-fill and document generation, plus a thin managed-service layer where a human does buyer-portal submissions by hand. None of the hard parts are research problems — the genuine friction is integration brittleness with dozens of heterogeneous buyer vendor portals (most have no API, many are third-party networks like Avetta/ISN/Ariba), so "submission" is human-in-loop RPA-or-manual rather than clean API plumbing. The real risk is not "can we build it" but that the architecture quietly bakes in a manual-ops core — every buyer portal is a snowflake, so labor doesn't compress the way a clean SaaS would, and that tension between the productized-software pitch and the artisanal-portal reality is the thing to watch. For two founders, the revenue-earning MVP is small and fast (a tracking workspace plus a human doing the work), so build risk is low; the scaling and margin risk is where the bodies are buried.

ARCHITECTURE SKETCH
- Frontend: standard multi-tenant web app — Next.js/React on Vercel; supplier-facing dashboard (buyer list, doc status, expiry calendar, alerts) + an internal ops console for the specialist. No novel UI.
- Backend/data: Postgres (Supabase/RDS) as system of record — tenants, buyers, requirement templates, documents, attestations, expiration dates, submission log. The moat-able asset: a structured library of "what each buyer requires." Object storage (S3) for the COIs/certs/PDFs.
- Async/background: a scheduler (cron via Inngest/Temporal/Postgres queue) for expiration tracking, renewal reminders, chase-detection. The genuinely valuable engine — deadline management at scale; boring to build (~1 week), high leverage.
- Integrations (the hard part): buyer vendor portals — Ariba/Coupa, Avetta, ISN, RapidRatings, buyer-proprietary portals, plus COI sources from supplier brokers. Few expose supplier-side APIs. So "submission" = a human in the ops console uploading to the portal, optionally assisted by browser automation (Playwright RPA) that breaks whenever a portal changes. Email ingestion (parse buyer chaser emails into tasks) via a mailbox + LLM classifier.
- ML/AI: LLM for (a) parsing inbound buyer requirement emails/PDFs into structured tasks, (b) pre-filling repetitive questionnaires from a supplier profile, (c) OCR/extraction from COIs to verify limits/endorsements. All assistive with human review — not autonomous, which is correct.
- Auth/security: Clerk/Auth0/Supabase Auth, RBAC (supplier users vs internal ops), row-level tenant isolation, audit log. Document store holds insurance certs, W-9s (EIN/TIN), attestations — moderately sensitive PII/business-confidential, not PHI/PCI.
- Hosting: Vercel + Supabase/AWS. Capital-light, scales to thousands of tenants before rearchitecture.
Where complexity hides: NOT in the stack — in the long tail of buyer-portal idiosyncrasies and the requirement-template library. Every new buyer is bespoke discovery work that lives in humans' heads until encoded.

BUILD PLAN TO REVENUE-EARNING MVP
~6–9 weeks of one developer to a version that can onboard paying suppliers. The managed service can begin earning before the software is polished — a founder runs the first 5–10 suppliers off a shared spreadsheet + the real buyer portals while the app is built (the correct bootstrap move; de-risks demand before code).
1. Core tracking workspace (~3 wks): tenant model, buyer/requirement/document schema, expiration dates, status dashboard, reminder scheduler. This alone + a human is sellable.
2. Document store + audit record (~1 wk): S3-backed uploads, versioning, "audit-ready" export per buyer. High perceived value, low effort.
3. Internal ops console + submission log (~1–2 wks): task queue, per-portal checklists, evidence of submission. Makes the managed service scalable-ish.
4. AI questionnaire pre-fill + email/PDF parsing (~2 wks): LLM over a supplier profile to draft answers and turn inbound emails into tasks. Assistive only.
5. (Punt to v2): browser-automation/RPA for portal submission, broker integrations, self-serve onboarding, buyer-specific template marketplace. Do NOT build RPA early — a maintenance tar pit; humans do submissions until volume justifies fragile automation.

CRITICAL ASSUMPTIONS
1. Buyer portals tolerate a third party submitting on the supplier's behalf. Many vendor portals/TPRM networks have ToS restricting access to the vendor; some networks (Avetta/ISN) charge the supplier directly and may view a managed intermediary as friction/a competitor. Verify: read 5 representative portal/network ToS; ask 5 target suppliers to confirm a third party can act on their login or be added as a delegate.
2. Manual per-supplier labor compresses enough to hold margin at $200–$1,000/mo. If portals stay artisanal, a specialist handles only ~30–60 suppliers, capping per-head economics. Verify: time-box a founder doing the full cycle for 3 real suppliers; measure hours/supplier/month.
3. LLM questionnaire pre-fill is reliable enough to save time without creating attestation liability. These are legally meaningful attestations (UFLPA, anti-corruption) — a wrong AI answer is the supplier's signature on a false statement. Verify: run the LLM on 20 real questionnaire items; measure human-edit rate.
4. The requirement set is common enough to templatize. If every buyer's stack is unique, the "library" never amortizes. Verify: collect actual requirement lists from 10 buyers across 3 verticals; measure overlap.
5. Suppliers will hand a vendor their certs, EINs, and the keys to their buyer relationships — a trust/security bar. Verify: gauge willingness to grant portal delegate access in early sales calls.

INFORMATION SECURITY POSTURE
Data: COIs, W-9s (EIN/TIN — business PII), code-of-conduct + UFLPA/anti-corruption attestations, food-safety/quality certs, data-security questionnaire answers (which describe the supplier's security posture — sensitive), and buyer-portal credentials or delegated access (the highest-value secret). No PHI, no payment-card data, minimal consumer PII — so the regime is "business-confidential + credential custody," not HIPAA/PCI. Threat model: the crown jewels are stored buyer-portal credentials; a breach there is catastrophic (manipulate a supplier's standing with its largest customers, or harvest credentials across many buyers). Controls at launch: TLS + at rest (KMS), a real secrets vault for any stored portal credentials (prefer delegated access / SSO over storing passwords; if passwords must be stored, a dedicated secrets manager, not the app DB), strict tenant row-level isolation, RBAC separating supplier and internal-ops roles, full audit logging of who submitted what to which buyer (also the product's selling point), MFA on internal accounts, a documented IR runbook. At scale: SOC 2 Type II becomes a sales requirement quickly (ironically you sell compliance) — the founders' real SOC 2 Type II experience makes it bridgeable in-house, ~4–6 month parallel effort, not a blocker. The single most important design decision: avoid storing reusable portal passwords; architect for delegate/SSO access so a breach can't replay credentials.

AVERAGE SCORE: 6.9 / 10

TOP 3 TECHNICAL STRENGTHS
- Entirely boring, proven, buy-not-build stack — the deadline/expiration scheduler + tracking DB are the whole technical core and take ~6 dev-weeks; no research risk.
- Revenue can precede the software — a founder can run early suppliers manually and validate demand before writing the app, the ideal bootstrap sequencing.
- An accumulating, structured requirement-template library (what each buyer mandates) is a real, ownable data asset that compounds and is hard for a sleepy incumbent to replicate.

TOP 3 TECHNICAL RISKS
- The submission layer is irreducibly manual/heterogeneous — dozens of snowflake buyer portals with no supplier API means labor scales with headcount, undercutting the "productized software" margin story.
- Custody of buyer-portal credentials is a high-value breach target; storing reusable passwords would be a latent catastrophe across many suppliers and buyers.
- LLM pre-fill of legally-meaningful attestations (UFLPA/anti-corruption) carries liability if wrong, and may save less human time than assumed, weakening the AI-leverage claim.

BIGGEST SINGLE RISK
That the architecture quietly encodes a manual-ops core dressed up as software. The buyer-side compliance world is a fragmented mass of proprietary vendor portals and third-party networks (Avetta, ISN, Ariba/Coupa, buyer-built systems), almost none of which expose a supplier-side API for a third party to submit documents and answer questionnaires programmatically. So the actual unit of work — uploading the right COI with the right endorsements, completing this quarter's questionnaire on this buyer's portal before the automated hold trips — is performed by a human navigating a one-off UI, and RPA built to automate it is a brittle, ToS-exposed maintenance tar pit that breaks every time a portal redesigns. The result is that the "software-leveraged managed service" behaves more like a staffed back-office whose throughput scales with people, not code, which both caps margin at $200–$1,000/month pricing and pulls the business toward the manual-operations model the founders explicitly want to avoid. The technology is genuinely easy; the trap is mistaking a tracking dashboard for the elimination of the labor, when the labor is the product.

QUESTIONS THE FOUNDERS MUST ANSWER BEFORE I'M COMFORTABLE
- For your 10 most common buyer portals/networks, do their ToS permit a third party to act on the supplier's behalf, and can you get delegated/SSO access so you never store a reusable password — or are you forced into credential custody?
- After timing a founder doing the full monthly cycle for 3 real suppliers, how many suppliers can one specialist actually carry, and does that headcount math support gross margin at $200–$1,000/month?
- When you run your LLM pre-fill against 20 real questionnaire items including UFLPA/anti-corruption attestations, what is the human-edit rate, and who bears liability when the supplier signs an AI-drafted attestation that turns out false?

RECOMMENDATION: REFINE
The build itself is a clear GO — proven stack, ~6–9 dev-weeks to a revenue-earning MVP, founders have the SOC 2 experience the security posture demands. What needs refining is not the technology but the operating model the technology sits on. Before committing, run two cheap experiments: (1) the ToS/delegate-access audit across the top 10 buyer portals to confirm you can operate without storing credentials and without violating network terms, and (2) the timed manual cycle on 3 real suppliers to measure true labor per account. If portals permit delegated access and a specialist can carry 40+ suppliers, this is a strong, low-tech-risk business; if the work stays artisanal and credential-bound, the architecture should pivot toward a software-first, supplier-self-serve tool (you provide tracking, reminders, pre-fill, and the audit record; the supplier still clicks submit) to keep it out of the manual-ops zone the founders are trying to escape. Scope cut for v1: do not build RPA; humans do submissions; build only the tracking workspace, document store, ops console, and LLM assist.
