LEGAL / REGULATORY REVIEW — Managed Supplier-Compliance Document & Attestation Service for SMB Suppliers to Large Buyers

(Not legal advice; experienced-operator pattern-recognition to brief real counsel.)

ONE-PARAGRAPH RISK READ
One of the cleaner regulatory postures I see: a B2B managed service that helps suppliers organize, renew, and submit documents to their own buyers. The business itself does not operate in a licensed industry, does not give legal or insurance advice, holds little sensitive personal data, and signs ordinary commercial service agreements. The real exposure is not industry regulation — it is professional-services liability arising from the company acting as an agent that attests, submits, and certifies on the supplier's behalf: if the company completes a forced-labor (UFLPA) attestation, a food-safety questionnaire, or a COI request inaccurately and the supplier is deactivated or the buyer relies on a false attestation, the supplier will look for someone to blame. That exposure is real but squarely manageable with disciplined contracting (supplier-signs-the-attestation, E&O carve-outs, customer-as-source-of-truth), E&O insurance, and a firm rule against touching the unauthorized practice of insurance or law.

REGULATORY MAP
- No industry-licensing regime governs the core service. "Helping a business submit its own compliance documents to its customers" is not a regulated activity under any federal or state licensing statute I can name. The dominant fact of the review.
- Unauthorized practice of insurance (state insurance codes / DOIs). Relaying COI requests to the supplier's own broker and tracking expirations is fine. Advising on coverage levels, recommending endorsements, or representing that a policy meets a buyer's requirement drifts toward acting as an unlicensed producer/advisor — low if the line is held (broker owns coverage decisions), moderate landmine if blurred.
- Unauthorized practice of law (state UPL / bar). Code-of-conduct, anti-corruption, and especially UFLPA forced-labor attestations are legal representations. Filling them out for a client, or interpreting what a buyer's clause "requires," edges toward UPL if framed as legal judgment — low if administrative and the supplier signs every attestation; landmine if the company opines on legal sufficiency.
- UFLPA / forced-labor and supply-chain attestation accuracy (19 U.S.C. § 1307; CBP; Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act). The company does not import and is not the CBP-regulated party — the supplier and buyer are. But drafting/submitting a false forced-labor attestation = participating in a material misrepresentation up the chain. Contractual/liability, not licensing.
- FTC Act § 5 (deceptive practices). "Guaranteed approved-vendor status" / "we keep you compliant" must be carefully bounded — outcomes depend on the buyer. Low, marketing-discipline.
- Data privacy — CCPA/CPRA + state laws + contractual data-handling. Data held is overwhelmingly business documents (COIs, W-9s, certifications). W-9s contain a TIN/EIN and sometimes an SSN for sole proprietors — the one pocket of sensitive PII. Low-to-moderate; standard SaaS hygiene + a DPA. No HIPAA/biometric/children's.
- TCPA / CAN-SPAM for outbound marketing + renewal reminders. Low.
- FTC Click-to-Cancel / state ARLs — B2B softens it, but easy-cancel + clear renewal terms are cheap. Low.
- Contractual flow-down from buyers. Acting inside the buyer's portal on the supplier's credentials may inherit portal terms of use. Low-to-moderate; monitor.

LICENSING SUMMARY
No specialized occupational or industry license is required. The company needs only ordinary business registration (entity, state business license, sales-tax registration where SaaS is taxable). The critical point is negative: the company must NOT need an insurance producer license or to practice law, and it stays out of both by holding two bright lines — (1) the supplier's own licensed broker makes all coverage decisions and issues COIs; the company only requests, tracks, and uploads; and (2) the supplier personally reviews and signs every legal attestation (code of conduct, anti-corruption, UFLPA); the company prepares and submits administrative paperwork but never certifies a legal fact on its own authority. If those lines hold, this is a no-license business. Food-safety certs (GFSI/SQF/BRC) are held by the supplier and issued by accredited bodies — the company never issues or audits them, only tracks/uploads, so no accreditation required. Founders lack no credential this business needs.

LIABILITY POSTURE
The entity's central exposure is professional E&O: a missed deadline, lapsed COI, wrong coverage limit, or misfiled questionnaire that results in the supplier being placed on hold, having POs blocked, payments withheld, or deactivated — which directly and measurably cuts off the supplier's revenue, making damages easy to quantify and attractive to litigate. Handled by (a) E&O / professional liability insurance (readily available, modestly priced for a document-management managed service) + GL + cyber; (b) a service agreement that caps liability at fees paid, disclaims responsibility for buyer decisions, makes the supplier the source of truth and the signatory on all attestations, and requires the supplier to maintain its own insurance and certifications; and (c) operational discipline (the supplier's broker owns COI accuracy; the supplier signs attestations). The secondary exposure is participating in a false attestation up the chain — if the company drafts a UFLPA or anti-corruption attestation that proves false, the buyer could claim reliance; the contractual cure (supplier signs and warrants the underlying facts) plus a strict "we transcribe what you tell us, we do not verify your supply chain" posture confines it. Pre-entity, founders carry personal exposure on early contracts; forming an LLC/Corp and getting E&O before the first paid engagement closes most of it. Data exposure is low (mostly business documents, W-9 TIN/SSN the one item warranting encryption/access control).

- UPL / unauthorized-practice-of-insurance drift: completing legal attestations (UFLPA, anti-corruption) or advising on insurance coverage levels can cross into practicing law or insurance without a license if the bright lines blur.
- Third-party portal terms of use: operating inside buyer/TPRM portals (Avetta, ISN, Coupa) on the supplier's credentials may violate those platforms' automation or access terms — a contractual/access risk outside the founders' control.

BIGGEST SINGLE RISK
Professional-liability exposure crystallizing from a single document failure. The value prop is "we make sure your compliance never lapses," and the buyer-side consequence of a lapse — POs blocked, payments withheld, vendor deactivation — translates into immediate, measurable, easily-pleaded lost revenue for the supplier. When (not if) a deadline is missed, a COI renews at the wrong limit, or a questionnaire is filed late and a supplier gets put on hold, that supplier will have a damages number and a clear "you were paid to prevent exactly this" narrative. This is insurable with E&O and contractually capped at fees paid, but the contract must be airtight: the supplier must be the signatory and warrantor on every attestation, the supplier's own broker must own all coverage decisions, buyer decisions must be expressly disclaimed, and marketing must never promise "guaranteed" outcomes (FTC § 5). Get the liability allocation, the attestation-signature workflow, and the E&O policy in place before the first paid engagement, and this drops from potentially business-threatening to a manageable cost of doing business; ignore it and a single mid-size supplier's deactivation claim could exceed a year of that account's revenue.

QUESTIONS FOR REAL COUNSEL BEFORE LAUNCH
1. Where exactly is the line on unauthorized practice of insurance — can we request/track/upload COIs and relay buyer requirements without a producer license, so long as the supplier's broker makes all coverage decisions, and how must we word client communications?
2. Where is the line on UPL for preparing/submitting UFLPA, anti-corruption, and code-of-conduct attestations — confirm an administrative "we prepare, you review and sign" workflow keeps us out, and draft the signature flow so the supplier is always the certifying party.
3. What liability cap, disclaimer, and indemnity structure caps a vendor-deactivation/blocked-PO loss at fees paid and puts responsibility for the truth of attestations and adequacy of insurance on the supplier?
4. Do the buyer and TPRM portals (Avetta, ISN, Coupa, SAP Ariba, major retailer vendor portals) permit a paid agent to log in and act on a supplier's behalf, and does any automation/scraping violate their ToS or the CFAA — which require an explicit authorized-agent designation?
5. Exposure if a supplier's attestation we submitted later proves false (e.g., a forced-labor representation under UFLPA) and the buyer claims reliance — does "supplier warrants the underlying facts" adequately insulate us?
6. Appropriate E&O/cyber/GL limits, and DPA terms for W-9 TIN/SSN and buyer documents under CCPA/CPRA + state privacy laws?

RECOMMENDATION: GO
Legally attractive: no licensing barrier, no regulated-industry burden on the company itself, low data sensitivity, a clean remote-W-2 employment posture, and a regulatory trajectory that drives demand. The genuine exposures — professional E&O and the UPL/unauthorized-insurance bright lines — are well-understood, insurable, and contractually manageable, and none requires a credential the founders lack. Required pre-launch work is concrete and finite: form the entity, bind E&O/cyber/GL before the first paid engagement, retain counsel to draft the service agreement with a strict liability cap and a supplier-signs-every-attestation workflow, hold the broker-owns-insurance and supplier-owns-legal-facts lines operationally, vet the major buyer/TPRM portals for authorized-agent terms, and discipline marketing claims against FTC § 5. With those in place this is a low-to-moderate regulatory-risk business — a clear GO from this council seat.
