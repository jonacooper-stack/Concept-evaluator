COO REVIEW — Managed CLIA Compliance & Quality-Systems Program for Small Clinical Laboratories

ONE-PARAGRAPH OPERATIONS READ
Structurally close to the archetype: a fully-remote, recurring, software-leveraged managed compliance service against a forced regulatory burden, served today by hourly, sleepy MT(ASCP)/retired-lab-director consultants — no trucks, no installs, no field crew. The unit of work is documentation and quality-program management (QMS, PT calendar, competency records, CAPA, mock inspections, biennial-survey prep), all deliverable over screen-share and shared workspaces. The real tension is not logistics; it is delivery-staffing dependency: the load-bearing input is a credentialed lab-quality specialist who can map CAP/COLA checklists, judge competency-assessment adequacy, and stand behind inspection-readiness — scarce, expensive, and the actual throughput bottleneck. The software thins the work but does not remove the licensed-judgment layer. Two founders can operate the documentation spine and the close; whether they can operate the QUALITY of the compliance product without one or two specialists carrying every account through inspection season is the operational question.

THE TUESDAY-IN-MARCH WALKTHROUGH
Tuesday in March, ~12-month mark. ~28 labs under recurring subscription. Two founders + one contracted lab-quality specialist (the named MT(ASCP) advisor, ~25–30 hrs/wk); a part-time second specialist being onboarded because inspection season is starting to bunch.
Founder A (GTM/close): morning on outbound and demos — two discovery calls with specialty/molecular labs that found the firm through a "10 most common CAP deficiencies" content piece, plus a proposal follow-up. Steady-state acquisition, squarely in the founders' wheelhouse.
Founder B (product/ops): triaging the week's deadline alerts. Three things a Tuesday in March actually generates: (1) a lab's PT shipment came back with an unacceptable score on one analyte — time-sensitive, an unsuccessful PT event can trigger a corrective-action obligation and, if recurrent, sanctions; (2) two competency-assessment cycles due this month with incomplete records (missing the direct-observation element of the six CLIA-required procedures); (3) one client has a biennial inspection window opening in ~6 weeks and the mock-inspection isn't scheduled.
The PT failure is where the model is tested. Founder B flags it and assembles paperwork, but the root-cause analysis and the defensibility of the corrective action (clerical transcription error? instrument calibration drift? reagent lot? competency gap?) is clinical/technical judgment → the specialist, ~2–3 hours with the lab's general supervisor reconstructing what happened and drafting a CAPA that survives accreditor scrutiny. The highest-value, least-automatable hour in the business — NOT founder labor. By midday the specialist preps the 6-weeks-out mock inspection (~half-to-full day of focused work per lab), and several land in the same window because biennial cycles cluster — the seasonal pinch. Support tickets: ~4–6 across 28 labs; accreditor-correspondence tickets route to the specialist, not the founders. Nothing required leaving a desk or a truck. What "breaks" on a heavy Tuesday is specialist capacity colliding with clustered inspection windows, plus the founders' ceiling on technically judging a PT failure they aren't credentialed to adjudicate.

Delivery labor, annual specialist-hours/lab [ASSUMED, verify with advisor]: routine QMS/PT/competency judgment ~24 hrs/yr; mock inspection + survey prep ~8–12 hrs but every other year → ~5 hrs/yr amortized; PT-failure CAPAs + accreditor correspondence ~6–10 hrs/yr → total ~35–40 hrs/lab/yr credentialed-specialist load; founders absorb the rest.

Practically ~1 FTE + a fractional second covers ~30–40 labs. Senior MT(ASCP)/lab-quality pro ~$90K–$140K loaded [ASSUMED]; one FTE + half ~$150K–$200K/yr ≈ ~15–20% of revenue at 30+ labs — healthy, leaves the ladder intact. Binding constraint is NOT founder throughput and NOT logistics; it's (a) hiring 1–2 credentialed specialists in a shortage market and (b) smoothing the inspection-season cluster. Both solvable with cohort-pacing + a contract-specialist bench.

AVERAGE SCORE: 6.7 / 10

TOP 3 OPERATIONAL STRENGTHS
- Cleanest possible logistics: fully remote, zero installs/trucks/site visits; the unit of work is a reviewed document + a recorded PT/competency entry (dim 3 = 9).
- Externally-fixed, regulation-defined scope (42 CFR 493 + published CAP/COLA checklists) makes the service unusually standardizable vs bespoke consulting; capacity math fits a 2-founder + ~1.5-specialist team at the mo24 base.
- Durable, low-risk supply/vendor posture: commodity cloud, no deplatforming risk, a national multi-sourced pool of certified lab-quality professionals.

TOP 3 OPERATIONAL RISKS
- Credentialed-specialist dependency in a shortage market: delivery quality + throughput hinge on hiring/retaining MT(ASCP)-grade specialists scarce by the same forces that create demand (dim 6 = 5).
- Seasonal inspection clustering: biennial windows + fixed PT dates bunch by cohort, creating labor peaks a single specialist can't absorb — forcing a bench and cohort-pacing discipline.
- Quality-failure blast radius: a client's failed inspection or mishandled PT-failure CAPA is reputationally severe and partly turns on technical judgment the founders don't hold — brand risk concentrates in the specialist layer.

BIGGEST SINGLE RISK
The credentialed-judgment bottleneck colliding with consequence severity. Unlike the CMMC archetype (where readiness is largely document assembly and policy mapping versatile founders can self-perform with an advisor on call), the CLIA product has an irreducible layer of clinical/technical lab judgment (adjudicating a PT failure's root cause, certifying competency-assessment adequacy across the six CLIA elements, defending a CAPA to an accreditor) that the founders are not credentialed to deliver and that AI cannot responsibly automate. The named specialist is not a nice-to-have advisor — they are a load-bearing delivery FTE, and the business cannot scale a single lab beyond the specialist's available, correctly-timed hours. When that specialist is underwater during a clustered inspection spring, the founders cannot simply absorb the overflow the way they could with policy documents — and the cost of a slip is a client's failed survey, which can mean sanctions up to certificate revocation and a brand-destroying reference. The model works only if the founders treat specialist-bench depth and cohort-pacing as a first-class operational discipline from day one.

QUESTIONS THE FOUNDERS MUST ANSWER BEFORE I'M COMFORTABLE
- Real measured specialist-hours-per-lab-per-year, split routine vs PT-failure events vs the survey-year inspection-prep spike? (Have the named advisor log hours against 3–5 real accounts for a quarter before pricing tiers.)
- How do you smooth biennial-inspection clustering — pace sales to spread survey cohorts, keep a contract-specialist bench for spring peaks, or both? Peak-week specialist load at 30 labs if a quarter's cohort all comes due at once?
- Where exactly is the line of liability/responsibility when a client fails an inspection or PT event despite the service? Is scope advisory (the lab's designated director/supervisor signs and owns it) or implicitly underwriting outcomes — and how does that line survive a client trying to blame you for a revocation?

RECOMMENDATION: REFINE
Operationally a strong, remote-first, capital-light, recurring model with the cleanest possible logistics and a capacity profile that clears the ladder (~30–40 labs ≈ ~1.5 specialists, ~15–20% of revenue in delivery labor). Not a field-ops business; doesn't break on throughput or supply chain. REFINE rather than GO purely because delivery quality and scalability rest on a scarce, load-bearing credentialed specialist whose hours cluster seasonally and whose mistakes carry existential client consequences — a heavier real-people dependency than the founder-led, software-thin archetype. Before GO: (1) productize scope so founders self-perform 70%+ of routine work and the specialist is reserved for genuine credentialed judgment; (2) build cohort-pacing and a contract-specialist bench into the GTM plan so inspection-season peaks are designed away; (3) draw a hard, contractual scope-of-responsibility line keeping statutory ownership with the lab's own designated personnel.
