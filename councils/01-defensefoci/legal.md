LEGAL / REGULATORY REVIEW — Managed FOCI Mitigation & NISPOM Compliance for Small Cleared Defense Contractors

(Not legal advice; experienced-operator pattern-recognition to brief real counsel.)

ONE-PARAGRAPH RISK READ
This concept sits inside the most heavily regulated corner of the economy: the National Industrial Security Program, administered by DCSA under 32 CFR Part 117 and EO 12829/13691, governing access to classified national security information. Good news: the customer carries the regulatory burden — the founders sell a managed service to help cleared contractors meet obligations DCSA imposes on them, not directly holding classified information or an FCL at launch. Bad news: several functions are statutorily reserved to cleared U.S. persons (FSO, government-approved outside directors, proxy holders), the work product is classified-program adjacent, and the delivery model depends on a named cleared practitioner whose availability/clearance/willingness is a single point of regulatory failure. Not fatal but unusually structural: you cannot paper over a gap with a disclaimer, and the vendor itself may at scale be drawn into the NISP as a cleared entity.

REGULATORY MAP
- DCSA / NISP — 32 CFR Part 117 (codified NISPOM): FCLs, PCLs, FOCI instruments (Board Resolution, SCA, SSA, Proxy, Voting Trust), self-inspections, Insider Threat Program, recurring security reviews. Burden on customer; vendor's burden is accuracy/competence.
- EO 12829, 13526 (classification), 13587/13691: underlying authority; stable, bipartisan; background context.
- National Interest Determinations / DCSA FOCI Operations: determines instrument and approved cleared directors/proxy holders. The substantive expertise sold; errors → lost clearance.
- ITAR (22 CFR 120–130, DDTC) and EAR (15 CFR, BIS): adjacent/overlapping; the TCP often must address export control. Selling TCP generation without ITAR/EAR competence is a malpractice landmine — in-scope expertise.
- CFIUS (31 CFR 800/802, Treasury): many FOCI customers also face CFIUS; advice near that boundary can require counsel.
- DD Form 254 / DFARS 252.204-7XXX: prime flow-down; deliverables must satisfy what the prime demands of the sub.
- False Claims Act (31 U.S.C. 3729), Procurement Integrity Act, TINA: if vendor-prepared documents/certifications submitted to government are false, exposure can reach the preparer. Liability, not licensing.
- Classified vs controlled information handling: if any engagement touches classified material, the vendor entity needs an FCL and cleared personnel. The model stays on the unclassified-administration side — that line is the whole ballgame; draw it precisely with counsel.
- State data-privacy (CCPA/CPRA): minimal — B2B, sensitive data is employee security records (not consumer PII); some records may be CUI requiring NIST SP 800-171 handling.

LICENSING SUMMARY
No conventional occupational license to run a NISP-compliance consultancy. But something stricter: statutorily reserved roles requiring a cleared U.S. person. The FSO must be a cleared employee of the cleared contractor; outside directors, proxy holders, and GSC members under SSA/Proxy/Voting Trust must be U.S. citizens approved by DCSA and individually cleared. Founders cannot perform these without personal clearances, which are sponsored only through a cleared contractor against a real classified-work requirement — you cannot buy a clearance. The concept correctly routes these to a named cleared partner/advisor. The single licensing-equivalent dependency is the cleared practitioner. At scale the vendor entity will likely need its own FCL and cleared staff to handle the work durably — a slow (6–18 month), DCSA-sponsored process requiring a legitimate classified contract and U.S.-ownership posture. Net: no ordinary license, but a hard, non-purchasable credential gate around the highest-value work.

LIABILITY POSTURE
Dominant exposure is professional liability / E&O: the vendor advises on and prepares the instruments that keep a customer's clearance alive. A deficient self-inspection, wrong TCP, missed deadline, or mis-structured instrument → clearance suspension/revocation, an existential multi-million-dollar event — precisely the large, causally-attributable damage that generates lawsuits; E&O premiums for "your error cost us our defense business" are non-trivial. Contracts need strong limitation-of-liability + "customer remains responsible for its own compliance and certifications," but those are tested when government certifications are involved (you cannot disclaim a False Claims Act problem if you knowingly prepared a false certification). Second exposure: the information-handling line — inadvertently receiving/storing classified or ITAR-controlled information without authorization is a federal violation, not a civil dispute. Third: founders' personal exposure pre-entity-formation — form a properly structured U.S.-owned C-corp/LLC and ensure the entity's own ownership has no FOCI (a FOCI-mitigation vendor with foreign owners is a credibility/access problem). Insurance handles E&O/GL; tight contracts handle scope/responsibility; nothing fully handles the criminal-statute layer except discipline about never touching unauthorized classified/ITAR material.

AVERAGE SCORE: 5.6 / 10

TOP 3 LEGAL/REGULATORY STRENGTHS
- Federally uniform regime — one rule (32 CFR Part 117), one regulator (DCSA), no 50-state licensing patchwork.
- Pure B2B, no consumer-protection surface — no ROSCA/TCPA/cooling-off landmines.
- Burden falls on the customer, not the vendor — founders aren't holding the FCL obligation directly at launch.

TOP 3 LEGAL/REGULATORY RISKS
- Cleared-practitioner single point of failure — statutorily reserved roles require cleared U.S. persons; the model hinges on a named advisor whose clearance/availability is non-purchasable and non-substitutable on short notice.
- High-consequence professional liability with a federal-statute tail — an error that costs a customer its clearance is existential/litigable; FCA/ITAR tail uncovered by E&O.
- The classified/CUI information-handling line — receiving/storing classified or ITAR material without authorization forces a vendor FCL and cleared staff; a slow, scaling-constraining process.

BIGGEST SINGLE RISK
The non-purchasable cleared-personnel gate combined with the high-consequence liability of being wrong. The domain reserves its most valuable functions — FSO, government-approved outside directors, proxy holders, GSC members under SSA/Proxy/Voting Trust per 32 CFR 117.11 — to cleared U.S. persons individually approved by DCSA, and a clearance cannot be bought; it must be sponsored through a cleared contractor against a genuine classified-work requirement. The founders cannot perform the highest-margin, highest-trust parts and are structurally dependent on a single named advisor. If that advisor becomes unavailable, loses/doesn't renew their clearance, or declines to put their name on a deliverable, the service cannot be rendered — and the customers are precisely those for whom a lapse is existential. Layered on: the vendor advises on the instruments that keep the clearance alive, so an error is severe and directly attributable, and a false government certification pierces ordinary contractual disclaimers (FCA). A credential the founders cannot acquire, concentrated in one person, attached to catastrophic-when-wrong consequences — a permanent operating constraint, not a one-time paperwork fix.

QUESTIONS FOR REAL COUNSEL BEFORE LAUNCH
1. Where exactly is the classified/CUI line — can the full advertised scope (TCP, ECP, self-inspection prep, GSC packages, review prep) be performed WITHOUT the vendor receiving/storing classified or ITAR/EAR-controlled information, and therefore without a vendor FCL? Get a written map.
2. What cleared-advisor structure does DCSA accept — can a named practitioner serve/advise statutory roles without creating an unauthorized-disclosure/conflict problem; what contractual + clearance-sponsorship arrangement makes it durable; how is exclusivity/availability secured?
3. What is the FCA/government-certification exposure of a document preparer (31 U.S.C. 3729, Procurement Integrity Act), and what contract language actually protects the founders (and its limits)?
4. What E&O covers "advice that cost a client its clearance," and what does it exclude (confirm FCA/ITAR uninsurable) — design operating discipline around it.
5. Does the vendor entity need a clean U.S.-ownership posture (avoid its own FOCI) for credibility and eventual FCL eligibility?
6. Are ITAR/EAR competence/registration obligations triggered by generating Technology Control Plans?

RECOMMENDATION: REFINE
The regulatory environment is both strength and weakness: federally uniform, zero consumer-protection surface, burden on the customer — but the model depends on non-purchasable cleared personnel and carries a high-consequence liability profile with an uninsurable federal-statute tail. Not fatal, and the founders correctly anticipated the central gate. To GO: (1) draw and document the classified/CUI line with counsel and scope the offering to stay on the uncleared-administration side so the vendor never needs its own FCL to deliver the core product; (2) harden the cleared-advisor dependency into a durable, exclusive, well-contracted relationship (ideally a bench of more than one); (3) engineer the liability posture deliberately — vendor as preparer/support with customer retaining responsibility for all government certifications, E&O with eyes open to FCA/ITAR exclusion, discipline that never touches classified/export-controlled material. With those, the posture is manageable-and-expensive rather than structurally fragile.
