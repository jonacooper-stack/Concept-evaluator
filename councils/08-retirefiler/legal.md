LEGAL / REGULATORY REVIEW — Managed ERISA Compliance & Filing Oversight for Small Retirement-Plan Sponsors

(Not legal advice; experienced-operator pattern-recognition to brief retained ERISA counsel.)

ONE-PARAGRAPH RISK READ
This concept lives squarely inside one of the most liability-dense federal statutes in existence — ERISA (29 U.S.C. ch. 18) — which is exactly what makes the underlying customer demand real and budgeted, and exactly what makes the service provider's own posture delicate. Good news: the business sells oversight and administrative support to the plan sponsor, NOT acting as the named fiduciary, the plan's auditor, the enrolled actuary, or an investment advisor, so it can plausibly position itself outside the most catastrophic liability tiers (3(16)/3(21)/3(38) fiduciary status, RIA registration, IQPA audit). Hard news: a service whose entire value proposition is "we make sure your fiduciary filing obligations are met" acquires a de facto duty of care to a client personally on the hook for DOL/IRS penalties — so the single largest exposure is professional-liability/E&O when the founders' service misses or mis-handles a deadline and the sponsor eats a penalty and sues. This is a B2B, single-state-of-incorporation, low-PII, contractually-governable business with manageable formal licensing — but it requires unusually careful scope-of-engagement contracting and a clear, defensible line that the firm is NOT the ERISA fiduciary, the plan administrator of record, the actuary, the auditor, or an investment advisor.

REGULATORY MAP
- ERISA (29 U.S.C. §§1001 et seq.), enforced by DOL EBSA — the governing regime for the customer's obligations: Form 5500, SAR + participant-notice distribution, fidelity-bond maintenance (§412: generally 10% of plan assets, min $1,000/max $500,000, $1M with employer securities), plan-document currency, fiduciary duties (§404). The sponsor bears these. The firm's burden is operational competence, not a license — but it must be scrupulously clear it is not assuming §3(16) "plan administrator" status, which would convert administrative support into direct ERISA fiduciary liability. The crux of the posture.
- IRS — IRC §401(a) and related (5500 is jointly DOL/IRS/PBGC; nondiscrimination testing under §§401(k)/(m), 410(b), 415; SECURE 2.0 amendment deadlines). The firm coordinates but does not certify testing; the TPA/actuary owns the math.
- DOL Delinquent Filer Voluntary Compliance Program (DFVCP) — the remediation line. Preparing/submitting DFVCP filings is administrative, but sits close enough to "interpreting penalty exposure and eligibility" that founders should confirm it doesn't constitute UPL; TPAs/consultants do this routinely without being lawyers — low-but-nonzero risk.
- Investment Advisers Act of 1940 / state RIA regimes — a bright-line AVOID. The moment the service touches fund selection, fee-reasonableness opinions tied to investment menus, or anything resembling investment advice, it risks needing RIA registration. The concept correctly disclaims this; must stay disclaimed in contract and marketing.
- AICPA / DOL audit rules (IQPA audit for large plans, generally 100+ eligible participants) — the firm assembles the audit-ready file, does not perform the audit; no CPA license implicated as long as it never opines on financial statements.
- Unauthorized Practice of Law (state bars) — advising a sponsor on its statutory fiduciary obligations and penalty exposure flirts with UPL. Defensible framing: compliance administration and process oversight, not legal opinions; refer genuine legal interpretation (plan-document drafting, fiduciary-breach questions) to ERISA counsel. TPAs operate in this gray zone today — precedent exists, but it must be deliberately managed.
- State data privacy (CCPA/CPRA + patchwork) — employer plan data + some participant PII (names, comp data, possibly SSNs for notice distribution/testing). Moderate, ordinary B2B-processor PII — not HIPAA, not biometric — but SSNs/comp data elevate breach stakes. GLBA generally NOT triggered (the firm is not a financial institution); confirm.
- FTC Act / advertising substantiation — "we make sure nothing falls through the cracks" / "avoid DOL penalties" must be substantiated and hedged; over-promising on penalty avoidance is both a marketing-law and a liability problem.

LICENSING SUMMARY
There is NO specific occupational or state license required to provide ERISA compliance administration and oversight. Unlike RIA work (Series 65 / state or SEC registration), CPA work, or law, ERISA third-party administration is NOT a licensed profession — TPAs operate nationwide without a TPA license. Genuinely favorable and a real strength: the named specialist's competence is a quality/liability issue, not a statutory licensing gate. Credentials that matter for credibility/competence (not legal permission) include ASPPA designations (QKA, QPA, CPC), an ERPA (Enrolled Retirement Plan Agent) to represent clients before the IRS on plan matters, or an enrolled-actuary relationship for testing/actuarial items — all bridgeable via the named specialist and partnerships. The firm needs only ordinary business registration, an EIN, standard entity formation. Hard lines to respect: do NOT cross into (a) investment advice without RIA registration, (b) plan-document legal drafting/opinions without counsel, or (c) named §3(16) plan-administrator status without the corresponding fiduciary insurance and intent. Stay on the administration/oversight side and the licensing picture is clean.

LIABILITY POSTURE
The core exposure is professional liability (E&O) for a missed or mishandled deadline. The client is a sponsor personally liable to the DOL/IRS for penalties; if the firm's oversight fails to catch a late 5500 or an undistributed notice and the sponsor is penalized, the sponsor's natural move is to recover from the firm paid to prevent exactly that. Insurable — ERISA-administration/TPA E&O policies exist — but premiums and underwriting scrutiny rise with the assurance language used. Two contractual levers determine manageable-vs-fatal: (1) scope-of-engagement clarity — the engagement letter must define precisely what the firm does (track, verify, prepare/distribute notices, chase, document) and does NOT do (act as fiduciary, file as plan administrator of record, give legal/investment advice, guarantee outcomes the sponsor or TPA controls); (2) limitation-of-liability + disclaimer of fiduciary status — caps tied to fees paid, carve-outs for sponsor/TPA failures to provide data, explicit statement that the sponsor remains the named plan administrator and fiduciary. ERISA §410(a) voids agreements purporting to relieve a fiduciary of fiduciary responsibility — precisely why the firm must NOT be a fiduciary; as a non-fiduciary service vendor, ordinary commercial limitation-of-liability clauses apply. Pre-entity personal exposure is closed by an LLC/S-corp + E&O + a tight engagement letter. Data liability (SSNs, comp data) is secondary but real: a breach triggers state breach-notification + reputational harm; standard cyber/data coverage + SOC-2-style controls handle it.

BIGGEST SINGLE RISK
The business's value proposition and its largest liability are the same thing. The firm is paid to be the safety net for obligations whose failure costs the sponsor real money (DOL/IRS late-filing penalties — "tens of thousands per plan-year") and exposes the sponsor personally as the named plan administrator and ERISA fiduciary. So every dollar of perceived value is also a dollar of perceived blame when something slips — and in a split-responsibility world where the TPA, recordkeeper, advisor, and sponsor all touch the process, causation will be contested. If a 5500 is filed late and the firm was retained as the "compliance oversight layer," the sponsor's counsel will argue the firm's whole job was to catch exactly that, regardless of whether the TPA actually controlled the filing. Survivable only with (a) airtight engagement-letter scoping distinguishing "verify and chase" from "guarantee and perform," (b) an explicit, repeated disclaimer of fiduciary and plan-administrator status (so §410(a)'s anti-waiver rule never applies and ordinary commercial limitation-of-liability caps hold), and (c) ERISA-administration E&O priced for the assurance language used. Get the contract architecture wrong — promise outcomes the firm doesn't control, or let marketing imply a guarantee — and a single penalized client can generate a claim that exceeds a year of that client's fees and threatens the firm's insurability.

QUESTIONS FOR REAL COUNSEL BEFORE LAUNCH
1. Fiduciary-status line: how to structure so the firm is definitively NOT a named §3(16) plan administrator or a §3(21) functional fiduciary, given we prepare/distribute participant notices and chase filings? What acts tip into fiduciary status, and can we contractually and operationally stay clear?
2. Limitation-of-liability enforceability: as a non-fiduciary service vendor, are fee-based caps, sponsor/TPA-failure carve-outs, and disclaimers enforceable, and does §410(a) reach us in any scenario? Draft a master engagement letter accordingly.
3. UPL and DFVCP remediation: does preparing/submitting DFVCP filings and advising on penalty exposure constitute UPL in our state(s), and where must we refer to ERISA counsel? Do we need an ERPA on staff to represent clients before the IRS?
4. Investment-advice boundary: what activities (fee benchmarking, menu commentary, provider comparisons) trigger Investment Advisers Act / state RIA registration, and how do we keep service + marketing clear of that line?
5. Marketing substantiation: which penalty-avoidance / "nothing falls through the cracks" claims can we make without an FTC substantiation problem, and how must they be hedged?
6. Data + breach posture: given SSNs and comp data, what state breach-notification + CCPA/CPRA obligations apply, do we owe a processor/DPA to each client, and is GLBA truly inapplicable?
7. E&O underwriting: what ERISA-administration/TPA E&O policy, at what limits, will an underwriter write given our assurance-style positioning, and what contract language do they require?

RECOMMENDATION: REFINE
The regulatory posture is workable and the demand-side driver (a federally mandated, penalty-backed, recurring, already-budgeted obligation on an under-served legacy segment) is exactly the forced, non-vitamin pull the objectives prize — and critically, the vendor faces no occupational-license gate and benefits from ERISA's federal uniformity, so this is not disqualified on legal grounds. REFINE rather than GO only because the liability architecture is load-bearing and must be settled before the first client: the firm must (1) lock a non-fiduciary, non-plan-administrator, non-investment-advice scope in both contract and marketing; (2) stand up an ERISA-administration E&O policy + a templated engagement letter with fee-based caps and TPA/sponsor-failure carve-outs; (3) discipline marketing away from guarantee language toward "oversight, tracking, and documentation." Make those three structural moves — none of which changes the customer, the revenue mechanism, or the delivery model — and the legal/regulatory risk drops from "manageable but sharp" to "ordinary professional-services." The single thing that would push it to NO-GO: if the founders intend to market outcome guarantees or to assume §3(16) responsibility to differentiate — that would invite uninsurable fiduciary exposure and should be explicitly off the table.
