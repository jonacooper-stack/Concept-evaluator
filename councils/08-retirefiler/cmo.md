CMO REVIEW — Managed ERISA Compliance & Filing Oversight for Small Retirement-Plan Sponsors

ONE-PARAGRAPH MARKET READ
A genuinely forced, penalty-backed, recurring problem — a Form 5500 deadline is not a vitamin, and a plan sponsor personally liable for fiduciary breaches is the rare buyer already afraid before you call. The marketing trouble is that this concept is positioned as an oversight/assurance layer sitting on top of parties (the TPA and recordkeeper) who are already contractually paid to do the filing, which means the founders must first convince a sponsor that the people they already pay are letting things slip — a harder, more abstract sell than "we do your 5500." The wedge is real but narrow, the buyer is hard to reach at low cost (small-business owners and HR/finance leads don't search for "ERISA compliance oversight"), and the most credible distribution path — advisor and TPA referrals — is structurally conflicted because you are positioning yourself as the watchdog over the very TPA you'd need to refer you. Sellable, yes; cheaply and at the velocity the income ladder requires, that's the open question.

POSITIONING DRAFT
For a customer hearing it: "You're personally on the hook for your company's 401(k) compliance — and your TPA and advisor each assume the other one's handling it. We're the independent layer that makes sure every 5500, notice, and deadline actually gets done, with an audit-ready file proving it." A defensible sentence with edges. The risk is the soft middle: "compliance oversight" and "assurance layer" are abstractions a busy owner doesn't feel until something has gone wrong. The sharper, more sellable version leads with the fear and the artifact: "Never miss a 401(k) filing deadline again — and have the paper trail to prove you didn't if the DOL asks." The fear-of-penalty framing is concrete; the "oversight layer" framing is consultant-speak that mushes in a cold inbox.

THE WEDGE
The first 100 do NOT come from SEO at meaningful volume — "ERISA compliance" and "Form 5500 help" are low-volume, high-competition terms already farmed by TPAs, ERISA law firms, and content mills, and the searcher is often a TPA professional, not a sponsor. The realistic wedge is twofold. (1) A timing-based outbound play against the DOL's public 5500 filing data: every filed Form 5500 is public via the DOL EFAST2 dataset, including filer, plan size, assets, and whether it was filed late or carried compliance flags. Build a list of small plans on independent recordkeeper/TPA arrangements (not on Guideline/Vestwell), filter for late filings or missing schedules, and run founder-led outbound — "we noticed your 2023 5500 was filed after the deadline; here's what that exposes you to." Specific, data-driven, founder-fit, and the data is free; CAC = founder time + maybe $1–3K/mo for a list-building stack [ASSUMED]. (2) An advisor/wealth-management referral channel: independent retirement-plan advisors (who explicitly disclaim filing duties) have a liability gap they'd love to outsource and touch the exact buyer — the conflict is the catch. Net: the wedge is more concrete than most concepts, but it is outbound-and-referral, not an inbound-content flywheel, which caps how cheap and self-propelling acquisition gets.

COMPETITIVE PICTURE
Guideline, Human Interest, Vestwell, Betterment at Work — modern bundled providers, well-funded, strong-tech, explicitly out of scope (they bundle the 5500/notices for plans on their own platform). The concept wisely declines to fight them — the right call. In the target segment, competitors are: (a) the TPAs themselves — FuturePlan by Ascensus, July Business Services, Nova 401(k) Associates, and a long fragmented tail — who already file the 5500 and run testing and will say "we already do this." (b) ERISA compliance software / 3(16) fiduciary administrators — the sharpest threat the one-pager under-weights: Pentegra, Newfront/3(16) providers, and a growing set of 3(16) administrative-fiduciary services already sell "we take the plan-administrator burden off you" — exactly this value prop, sometimes with the added strength of legally assuming the fiduciary role (which this concept explicitly does NOT). (c) hourly ERISA consultants/attorneys — sleepy, unmarketed. The genuine air is the independent, productized, sponsor-side, plan-stays-where-it-is position: 3(16) providers usually want to be administrator of record and often bundle with a recordkeeper move; the TPAs are conflicted; the consultants don't market. A productized "we oversee whoever you already use" is a real, if narrow, slot. But the concept's chosen non-fiduciary stance is a marketing weakness vs 3(16) providers — "we assume the legal liability" beats "we make sure someone else does it" in a fear-driven sale.

AVERAGE SCORE: 5.7 / 10

TOP 3 MARKET STRENGTHS
- The pain is forced, recurring, penalty-backed, already budgeted — a Form 5500 deadline and personal fiduciary liability are painkillers, and the buyer arrives pre-scared.
- A genuinely concrete, free, founder-fit wedge: the DOL EFAST2 public 5500 dataset lets the founders target non-bundled small plans with late/flagged filings and lead outbound with the sponsor's specific documented exposure.
- The "independent, sponsor-side, plan-stays-where-it-is, productized" slot is real and under-marketed in the legacy TPA/recordkeeper segment.

TOP 3 MARKET RISKS
- The "oversight of parties already paid to do it" framing forces a two-step persuasion (convince the sponsor their TPA is failing, then sell the fix) — abstract and harder than "we do your filing."
- The strongest adjacent competitor — 3(16) administrative-fiduciary providers like Pentegra — sells the same assurance with a stronger claim (they legally assume the plan-administrator liability), while this concept explicitly declines to — a marketing disadvantage in a fear-driven sale.
- The most efficient referral channel (TPAs/advisors) is structurally conflicted with a watchdog positioning, leaving outbound grind as the primary motion, capping acquisition velocity and threatening the income-ladder timeline.

BIGGEST SINGLE RISK
Buyer self-perception denial: the small-plan sponsor who needs this most is precisely the one who believes "my TPA and my advisor have this handled" — because that's what they're paying them for. The entire pitch requires first dislodging a comfortable (often false) belief before any selling can begin — the slow, expensive kind of sale where the customer must be educated into recognizing a problem they assumed was covered. The objectives doc is explicit: if the customer "has to be educated into believing they have the problem, it is the wrong problem." This concept is on the knife's edge of that line: the consequence (penalty, personal liability) is real and budgeted, but the gap (your paid providers are letting it slip) is exactly what the sponsor doesn't believe until after it's gone wrong — and most won't pay to prevent a problem they don't think they have. The EFAST2 late-filing data wedge is the one mechanism that punctures this denial (you show them they already missed a deadline), and it's the only thing pulling this concept out of vitamin territory — which means the whole business leans hard on that one channel working at scale and at acceptable CAC.

QUESTIONS THE FOUNDERS MUST ANSWER BEFORE I'M COMFORTABLE
- Have you interviewed 15–20 small-plan sponsors on legacy TPA/recordkeeper arrangements, and did any say — unprompted — that they worry their TPA is dropping obligations and would pay an independent party to watch? Or are you inferring the pain from the regulation?
- When you run the EFAST2 late-filer outbound test (50–100 contacts), what are reply/meeting/close rates, and does CAC sit comfortably under a 12-month payback at $150–$600/mo? The make-or-break number.
- How do you defend against 3(16) administrative-fiduciary providers (Pentegra, Newfront) who can say "we assume the legal liability and you don't" — what's your honest answer to "why not just hire a 3(16) instead of a watcher who takes on no liability?"
- Can the TPA/advisor referral channel ever work given your watchdog positioning, or does it collapse the moment a TPA realizes your service exists to catch their misses?

RECOMMENDATION: REFINE
The bones are good — forced, recurring, penalty-backed demand in a fragmented, under-marketed segment, with a rare free-data outbound wedge fitting the founders' regulated-sales and compliance-operator strengths. But three things must change before GO. First, commit to the sharp fear-led positioning ("never miss a 401(k) filing deadline, with the paper trail to prove it") and retire the abstract "oversight layer / assurance" language. Second, resolve the 3(16) competitive flank — either move toward offering (or partnering for) actual 3(16) administrative-fiduciary liability assumption (a STRONGER sell than pure oversight), or articulate a crisp reason a sponsor should prefer independent watching over delegated liability. Third, non-negotiably, run the EFAST2 late-filer outbound test and report real reply-to-close numbers before scaling; that one channel does almost all the work of converting this from a vitamin into a painkiller, and the business doesn't earn a GO until that number is real rather than assumed.
