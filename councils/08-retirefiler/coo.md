COO REVIEW — Managed ERISA Compliance & Filing Oversight for Small Retirement-Plan Sponsors

ONE-PARAGRAPH OPERATIONS READ
One of the cleaner managed-compliance shapes: fully remote, no trucks, no installs, no physical supply chain, and a delivery unit that is fundamentally a recurring calendar-tracking-and-verification loop plus exception handling. Two founders plus a named ERISA specialist can plausibly run it because the high-volume work (calendar mapping, notice generation, deadline chasing, status dashboards) is software-leverageable, and the genuinely expert work (interpreting a missed amendment, structuring a DFVCP filing, reading a TPA's testing output) lands on a low number of exceptions per plan per year. The real tension is not labor volume — it is the seasonality spike around the Form 5500 deadline and the fact that the "verification" promise depends on third parties (TPA/recordkeeper) you do not control, so your unit of work is partly chasing other people's work product. Doable, but it puts a human-judgment specialist in the critical path more than the pitch admits.

THE TUESDAY-IN-MARCH WALKTHROUGH
Month 12, ~120 paying plans [ASSUMED mid-tier mix]. Tuesday in early March 2027 — operationally meaningful: for calendar-year plans the 5500 deadline is July 31 (extendable to Oct 15), and ADP/ACP corrective distributions for failed tests are due March 15. So March is one of two genuinely busy windows.
The day opens with software, not a person: the workspace pushed each plan's calendar items into status buckets overnight — green (verified done), yellow (due in 30–60 days, owner notified), red (at risk — TPA non-responsive, or a document the sponsor still owes). The ops-lead founder opens the master dashboard: ~14 red/yellow items across 120 plans need human action today. Most plans are green, zero touch — the whole point of the model.
The named ERISA specialist takes the substantive items. Two plans failed ADP testing per the TPA's preliminary results; the March 15 corrective-distribution clock is live — the specialist reviews the TPA's numbers, confirms refund amounts and participant communications, emails sponsor + TPA to confirm distribution is executed before the 15th, documents in the fiduciary file. Three plans have a SAR distribution flagged; the platform generates participant-ready PDFs, out they go (electronic where permissible, print-and-mail vendor where not). One plan's fidelity-bond renewal lapsed 11 days ago — the specialist contacts the broker, gets it reinstated, logs the gap.
The marketing-lead founder grows the business: a webinar follow-up to TPAs/advisors (referral channel), two founder-led sales calls off a "is your 5500 actually getting filed?" content piece, and onboarding kickoff for a new plan — the heaviest single unit of work (pull the plan document, prior 5500s, party contacts, build the calendar, establish who-owns-what; multi-hour over a couple of weeks, hence the $1K–$4K setup). Where it breaks: a TPA goes dark on a plan whose 5500 schedules may not even be in progress, deadline math tightening. The value prop is "we verify it actually got done" — but the founders cannot file the 5500 themselves for a plan the TPA administers; they can only escalate, document, and if it slips, run DFVCP. The failure mode is partly outside their control, and the customer's emotional expectation ("you've got this") can exceed what the service can mechanically guarantee. That gap is the operational tightrope.

TOP 3 OPERATIONAL RISKS
- The "we verify it got done" promise depends on third parties (TPA/recordkeeper) the founders don't control and cannot substitute for — delivery risk partly lives outside the operation.
- Single-point staffing dependency on one named ERISA specialist; the pool is real but thin, and the service's credibility rests on that expertise being present and correct.
- Hard seasonality (March 15 testing, July 31/Oct 15 5500) creates 2–3× peak-load weeks that become the binding constraint at scale and demand pre-built surge capacity.

BIGGEST SINGLE RISK
The gap between the promise ("we make sure every obligation is actually met") and the mechanical reality that the founders cannot perform the core regulated work themselves — the TPA files the 5500, the actuary/auditor does their piece, and the service is an oversight-and-chase layer on top of parties it does not control. On a good Tuesday this is fine. But the failure mode is asymmetric and high-consequence: if a TPA goes dark and a 5500 slips, the sponsor will feel the service failed even though it did everything it could short of filing — and the downside (penalties, fiduciary exposure) is exactly the catastrophic outcome the customer bought you to prevent. Not a "two people can't do the volume" risk (capacity is comfortable); a "two people are accountable for an outcome they can only influence, not guarantee" risk. Manageable with crisp scope language, documented escalation trails, and a willingness to step into remediation (DFVCP) — but the specialist's judgment and the contractual definition of the promise are in the critical path on every edge case, and getting that boundary wrong turns a happy customer into a liability claim.

QUESTIONS THE FOUNDERS MUST ANSWER BEFORE I'M COMFORTABLE
- Exactly where does your responsibility end? When a TPA misses a filing despite your chasing, are you contractually an oversight/notification service (flagged, documented, escalated) or on the hook for the outcome? The whole liability posture and the customer's expectation hinge on this line.
- Who is the named ERISA specialist, are they secured, and what is your bench? At mo24 you need ~2 specialists through the Oct 15 crunch — where does the second come from, and what happens if your one specialist leaves in September?

RECOMMENDATION: GO
A strong fit for a two-founder remote team: no field ops, no physical supply chain, a finite repeatable delivery unit, and labor that scales sub-linearly because software does the volume work — the capacity math (~150 plans mo12 on 2 founders + 1 specialist + 1 associate; ~300–400 mo24 on a sub-10-person team) clears the ladder without heroics. The two things keeping it from a clean 8+ are (1) the dependency on uncontrolled third parties to actually complete regulated work you can only verify and chase, and (2) the thin single-specialist staffing pool combined with hard 5500/testing seasonality. Neither is disqualifying and both are addressable: pin the scope boundary in the contract so you are an accountable oversight layer rather than a guarantor of others' filings, secure and bench the ERISA specialist role early, and validate the per-plan and peak-season hours on a live cohort before scaling the book.
