CTO REVIEW — Managed ERISA Compliance & Filing Oversight for Small Retirement-Plan Sponsors

ONE-PARAGRAPH TECHNICAL READ
A textbook bootstrap-friendly build: fundamentally a structured-data compliance calendar plus a document-generation and notification engine layered over a managed-service workflow — none of which requires unsolved technology. The software wedge is genuinely thin (a per-plan obligation tracker, deadline scheduler, notice-template generator, audit-ready document vault), and every component maps to off-the-shelf primitives (Postgres, a job scheduler, a templating engine, a transactional email/mail provider, Stripe). The real complexity is NOT in the code; it is in the domain rules-engine — encoding the correct ERISA/SECURE 2.0 obligation matrix per plan type and keeping it current as the law changes — and in the data-ingestion friction of pulling plan facts out of TPAs/recordkeepers who have no API. The security surface is meaningful but not exotic: you are custodian of employer plan data and participant PII, a fiduciary-file system of record, so encryption, access control, and audit logging must be designed in from day one. For two founders with a compliance-operator background and a named ERISA specialist, this is buildable and operable; the technology is the easy part and the domain-rules correctness is the part most likely to bite.

ARCHITECTURE SKETCH
- Frontend: a single Next.js/React app on Vercel — an internal specialist/ops console (the workhorse) + a lighter sponsor-facing dashboard (compliance calendar, status-per-obligation, downloadable fiduciary file). Most value accrues to the internal console first.
- Backend: a boring monolith (Node/TS or Python/Django) with Postgres as the single source of truth. Core entities: Plan, Sponsor, Obligation (instances from a rules template), Notice, Document, Task, Party (TPA/recordkeeper/advisor).
- The wedge — an obligation rules-engine: a versioned config/ruleset that, given plan attributes (plan type, plan-year-end, safe-harbor vs not, auto-enroll, participant count, assets), expands into the dated obligation calendar (5500 + schedule determination, SAR, 404(a)(5), QDIA, safe-harbor, auto-enrollment notices, SECURE 2.0 amendment deadlines, fidelity-bond renewal, testing coordination). Data/rules, not ML; ~3–4 dev-weeks for v1 + ongoing curation.
- Async/scheduled work: a cron/queue layer (pg-boss or a hosted scheduler) that fires deadline reminders, generates notice instances, and escalates at-risk items. Low volume — thousands of obligations, not millions.
- Document generation: server-side template merge (HTML-to-PDF) for participant notices + the audit-ready fiduciary packet. Notice text from maintained templates, not LLM free-generation.
- Notice distribution: transactional email (Postmark/SendGrid) and, where physical mailing is required under ERISA delivery rules, a print-and-mail API (Lob). Mail delivery is a real integration with cost + audit-trail implications.
- AI/LLM: strictly assistive and bounded — drafting client-comms, summarizing a TPA document, extracting fields from an uploaded 5500/plan document for human review. Must NOT be the system of record for whether an obligation is met.
- Integrations: the soft spot. No standard API exists for legacy recordkeepers/TPAs. Data ingestion is largely document upload + manual/AI-assisted extraction + email coordination. Government data (EFAST2 for 5500, DOL DFVCP) is partly portal/filing-based, not clean API.
- Auth/hosting: managed auth (Clerk/Auth0/WorkOS), Vercel + managed Postgres (Neon/RDS), encrypted S3 for documents. SOC 2 Type II attainable on this stack — and the founders have run SOC 2 Type II cycles before.
Where complexity hides: (1) the obligation rules-engine being correct and current — a wrong deadline or omitted notice is a liability event, not a bug; (2) document/data ingestion from non-API counterparties; (3) ERISA-compliant electronic-delivery rules for participant notices (DOL safe-harbor requirements) which constrain how you may deliver and what you must log.

BUILD PLAN TO REVENUE-EARNING MVP
~9–13 dev-weeks of one developer to a chargeable service, because the first paying clients can be served with a thinner-than-full software layer (manual rules curation + spreadsheet-backed calendar behind a clean client dashboard). The managed-service model lets you sell before the software is complete.
1. Plan/obligation data model + obligation rules-engine v1 (~3–4 wks) — encode the most common archetype (calendar-year safe-harbor 401(k)) first; hand-curate edge cases. The wedge.
2. Specialist ops console + sponsor dashboard with per-obligation status/owner + the audit-ready fiduciary-file export (~2–3 wks) — the fiduciary file is the deliverable clients pay to produce.
3. Notice template library + PDF generation + email/mail distribution with delivery logging (~2–3 wks) — start with highest-frequency notices.
4. Auth, billing (Stripe subscriptions + setup/remediation invoicing), document vault with encryption + audit logging (~2 wks).
5. Punt to v2: AI field-extraction from uploaded documents/5500s; any TPA/recordkeeper "integration"; multi-plan-type rules coverage beyond common cases; self-serve onboarding.
The honest sequencing point: the binding constraint on time-to-revenue is NOT engineering, it is (a) recruiting/retaining the named ERISA specialist and (b) getting the first plan's facts loaded. You can sell the managed service on a spreadsheet+templates backbone in week 4–6 and let software catch up.

CRITICAL ASSUMPTIONS
1. The obligation rules-engine can be authored correctly by a versatile dev + a named ERISA specialist, and kept current. If the ruleset is wrong, the product actively creates the liability it sells against. Verify: have the specialist hand-build the full obligation calendar for 5 real (anonymized) plans of different types and diff it against the engine's output.
2. A named, reliable ERISA-administration specialist is recruitable and stays. The correctness and credibility of the service depends on this person; the founders' background scaffolds but doesn't replace ERISA-specific judgment. Verify: line up the specialist before writing code; confirm via a paid pilot on 2–3 plans.
3. Plan facts can be ingested without API access at acceptable cost. Verify: run onboarding on 3 real plans manually and time it; if loading a plan takes 8+ hours every time and never gets cheaper, unit economics suffer. [ASSUMED] onboarding 2–6 hrs/plan dropping with templates.
4. The service can sit "independent of and alongside" the TPA without itself becoming the ERISA fiduciary or unauthorized practice. More legal than technical, but shapes what the software may assert. Verify with ERISA counsel before building the "we verify the TPA filed it" workflow.
5. ERISA electronic-disclosure safe-harbor rules can be satisfied by your delivery + logging design. Verify: read the DOL electronic-disclosure regulation and design the delivery-receipt/audit-log to match before shipping notice distribution; physical mail (Lob) as fallback.

INFORMATION SECURITY POSTURE
Data: employer/plan identifying data (EIN, plan numbers, prior 5500s, plan documents, asset figures) + participant PII — at minimum names, and depending on notice handling, potentially SSNs, compensation, dates of birth flowing through testing and notice processes. Sensitive: participant PII + financial data makes you an attractive target and a fiduciary-relevant data custodian. Lives in Postgres + encrypted S3 (uploaded plan documents, generated notices, fiduciary files). Threat model: account takeover of the specialist console (highest-value — reads every client's plan + participant data), document-store exfiltration, and tampering with the audit trail that is itself the product's evidentiary value. Controls at launch: SSO/MFA on the internal console, RBAC (specialists scoped to assigned plans), TLS, encryption at rest (Postgres + S3 SSE), per-tenant isolation, secrets in a managed vault, immutable/append-only audit logging for every obligation status change and notice delivery (this log IS the deliverable, so its integrity is paramount), encrypted tested backups. At scale: SOC 2 Type II (clients will ask; founders have run it before, attainable within the first year on this stack); IR + breach-notification runbook (participant PII may trigger state breach laws); a data-processing addendum + clear data-ownership terms. PII minimization is the cheapest risk reducer: avoid ingesting SSNs where the obligation can be tracked without them; let the TPA retain raw census data where possible. Incident readiness must exist at launch given the data class.

AVERAGE SCORE: 7.5 / 10

TOP 3 TECHNICAL STRENGTHS
- The technology is deliberately boring and almost entirely buyable: the only custom asset is a deterministic obligation rules-engine + an audit-ready fiduciary-file export — no ML on the critical path, ~9–13 dev-weeks to chargeable software.
- The managed-service wrapper decouples revenue from a finished platform: the first paying plans can be served on a curated spreadsheet+template backbone (~4–6 weeks in) while the software hardens.
- Security and SOC 2 are realistically in reach on the proposed commodity stack, and the founders have personally run SOC 2 Type II cycles — a credible, named control capability.

TOP 3 TECHNICAL RISKS
- The obligation ruleset must be correct and perpetually current; the product sells liability-avoidance, so an encoding error or stale deadline turns the software into a liability generator.
- Data ingestion from non-API legacy TPAs/recordkeepers is structurally manual; if per-plan onboarding stays labor-heavy, the software leverage erodes and the model drifts toward a consultancy.
- The system is custodian of participant PII + the evidentiary audit trail, raising the security/integrity bar above a generic SaaS app and making any breach or audit-log tampering a credibility-ending event.

BIGGEST SINGLE RISK
The correctness-and-currency of the obligation rules-engine, because the product's entire value proposition is "we make sure nothing falls through the cracks" — which means a defect in the ruleset does not merely degrade the product, it manufactures the exact harm the customer paid to avoid (a missed 5500, an un-distributed notice, a blown amendment deadline), with penalties and fiduciary exposure attached. Unlike a typical SaaS bug, this is not self-correcting from user feedback and not cheap to detect: a wrong deadline can sit silent until the day it is missed, and the law underneath it (SECURE 2.0 phase-ins, annual IRS/DOL deadline and notice changes) keeps moving, so the ruleset is a living artifact requiring ongoing specialist curation forever. Simultaneously the wedge (the defensible domain asset) and the bomb (most likely to cause a client loss event and an E&O claim). Technically tractable — the 5-plan diff test verifies the encoding and a versioned ruleset with specialist sign-off contains it — but it converts what looks like a low-maintenance app into a business with a permanent, non-negotiable domain-maintenance and QA discipline that must be staffed and resourced from day one.

QUESTIONS THE FOUNDERS MUST ANSWER BEFORE I'M COMFORTABLE
- Who is the named ERISA-administration specialist, are they committed before build, and what is the documented process and accountability for keeping the obligation ruleset current as SECURE 2.0 phases in and annual deadlines change?
- Real per-plan onboarding cost in specialist hours on the first 3–5 real plans, and the concrete plan to drive it down with templates/AI extraction so the model stays software-leveraged rather than a consultancy?
- Given custody of participant PII + an evidentiary audit log, will you ingest SSNs/census data at all, and if so what is the day-one control set — and have you confirmed with ERISA counsel exactly what the software may assert about TPA filings without becoming the plan fiduciary or engaging in UPL?
- How will you handle ERISA electronic-disclosure safe-harbor requirements for participant notices — the delivery-receipt/logging design, and where physical print-and-mail (Lob) becomes mandatory?

RECOMMENDATION: GO
From a purely technical and information-security standpoint this is a clean, well-scoped, bootstrap-appropriate build: commodity-buyable infrastructure, a genuinely thin custom wedge (the rules-engine + fiduciary-file export), ~9–13 dev-weeks to chargeable software with revenue available even earlier via the managed-service wrapper, and a SOC 2-attainable security posture the founders are personally qualified to stand up. My reservations are narrow and named: the obligation ruleset is both the moat and the bomb and must be staffed for perpetual correctness/currency from day one, and the non-API ingestion friction must be measured early so the model does not silently degrade into a labor-heavy consultancy. Neither is a technical blocker — both are de-risked by the 5-plan diff test (rules correctness), a timed manual onboarding of the first 3–5 plans (ingestion economics), and locking in the named ERISA specialist before writing code. Green on the technology; the open questions are domain-staffing and legal-scope, not engineering.
